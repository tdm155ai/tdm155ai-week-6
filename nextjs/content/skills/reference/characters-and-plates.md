# Character references and background plates

These are studies you can use to develop a skill and a quality check. The [model sheet](../../concepts/visual-art/model-sheet.md), [storyboard](../../concepts/visual-art/storyboard.md), and [continuity record](../../concepts/film/continuity.md) already give us ways to hold decisions steady while making variations.

## Character and prop studies

- **One character, several views.** Make front, side, three-quarter, and full-body views. Choose the details that identify the character, then check whether every view agrees. Keep the individual images as well as the sheet.
- **One prop, several states.** Make a case closed, open, and held by the character. Check handle placement, latch count, material, and scale. A skill can flag contradictions before generating a shot.
- **One action, different coverage.** Plan a wide view, a medium view, and a detail of the same action. Compare the reference images and generated frames side by side. Keep the cause of a continuity change separate from its effect on the story.
- **Two frames, one transition.** Approve a beginning and ending image, then test a supported first/last-frame endpoint. Review what happens between them, especially hands and the prop's changing state.

Use [still capture](/capture) to collect real poses, prop handling, and the stage camera's perspective. A captured reference can make a vague instruction much easier to inspect.

## Plates for our stage

A background plate is the environment behind the performer in a composite. At this station the performance happens live on the greenscreen stage, and the ATEM does the key. The web app helps you make and capture references; it does not perform the composite.

Try a workshop, a station platform, or an observatory. Leave a deliberate area for the person to stand. Record the stage camera's height, framing, and light direction, and use those to plan the plate. A horizon at the wrong height or a tiny doorway can reveal a mismatch even when the image looks good on its own.

For a moving plate, try a fixed camera with one background event: a train passes in the distance, clouds move outside a window, or lights change on a console. Ask whether the movement helps the performance. A moving viewpoint can make a stationary studio camera much harder to match.

## Review the live composite

Look at the performer and plate together in the ATEM output. Compare scale, horizon, light direction, color, and empty space. Capture a still of the program feed and write down one revision to try. Keep the original plate and the composite reference as different assets; they answer different questions.

If the program feed shows your own capture page on a studio display, use a clean output or hide the preview before grabbing a reference to avoid a nested screen-in-screen image.
