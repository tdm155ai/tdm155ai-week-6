# Copying the masters: the Louvre's copyists, casts, and imitatio

**Wall label**

Before you invent, you copy. Roman rhetoric teachers called this *imitatio*: learn to write by imitating the best writers, then go beyond them. Art academies applied the same idea in stages. Students copied prints, then drew from plaster casts of ancient sculpture, then drew the living model. The Louvre has run an office for copyists since it opened in 1793. Today artists still set up easels in front of paintings, under rules meant to prevent forgery. The copy itself is not the goal. The goal is what your hand learns by following, stroke by stroke, decisions another painter already made.

## What it is

Copying is a practice method. The finished image already exists, so the learner can attend to *how* it was made: the order of marks, the proportions, the edges, the colour mixtures. Because the answer is in front of you, copying comes with built-in feedback. You can compare your copy to the original after every stroke.

**Imitatio.** Quintilian (*Institutio Oratoria*, Book X, ch. 2, 1st century CE) held that much of an orator's art comes from imitation. He also warned that "imitation alone is not sufficient" (10.2.4) and that it is "a positive disgrace to be content to owe all our achievement to imitation" (10.2.7, Butler's translation). The tradition always treated copying as a stage, not the endpoint.

**The academic sequence.** Nineteenth-century French academic training typically ran from copying drawings and prints, to drawing plaster casts, to the live model. The best-known printed form of this sequence is the *Cours de dessin* by Charles Bargue, made with Jean-Léon Gérôme and published by Goupil in the late 1860s–1870s. It has 197 lithographed plates in three parts: casts, master drawings, and the figure. Vincent van Gogh worked through it.

## What the evidence says

**Documented (the Louvre):**

- The Louvre describes itself as the last museum to hold a *bureau des copistes*, which has existed since the museum opened in 1793.
- In 2025 the Louvre and the Centre Pompidou-Metz mounted *Copistes* (14 June 2025 – 2 February 2026). About a hundred contemporary artists were invited to make copies based on Louvre works.

**Reported, details vary by source (check with the Louvre before you tell students):** press accounts describe the current rules as follows.

- Permits run for up to three months.
- Copyists work in the galleries in the morning, outside weekends and holidays, from September to June.
- The copy must be one-fifth larger or smaller than the original in each dimension.
- The original artist's signature may not be reproduced.
- Finished canvases are stamped by the museum before they leave.

Reported numbers of permits per year differ widely, from about 90 to about 250. Waiting lists of up to two years are also reported.

**Research.** We found no controlled studies of master-copying as a training method in drawing or painting. The closest research is on *observational learning* and *worked examples*: novices learn well from studying finished solutions before solving problems on their own (Sweller & Cooper, 1985). This benefit fades, and can even reverse, as learners gain expertise (the "expertise reversal effect"; Kalyuga et al., 2003). Interpretation: master-copying works like a worked example for the hand. It is most useful early, and becomes less useful once the learner can generate solutions alone. Those studies used algebra and similar tasks, not painting.

## Optional exercises


Students rarely copy a whole painting. More often they copy a passage:

- a hand
- a fold of drapery
- one transition from light to shadow
- one head

One repetition is a single copy of that passage, typically 10–45 minutes. Copying the same passage twice, a few days apart, shows what was learned and what was only traced.

### More ways to try it
- Assign a *passage*, not a painting. Smaller areas allow several attempts in one session.
- Ask students to copy the *order* of making where possible. Lay in the big shapes first, as the original painter probably did, rather than starting with the eye.
- Follow every copy with an "after" piece: something new made in the same manner. This is Quintilian's second step, going beyond the model.
- Discuss the ethics openly. The Louvre's size and signature rules show that copying and forgery are separated by convention and by intent.
