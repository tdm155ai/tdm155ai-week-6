# Franklin's Spectator exercise

**Wall label**

Benjamin Franklin taught himself to write by taking good essays apart and rebuilding them. In his mid-teens, an apprentice in his brother's Boston print shop, he bought a stray volume of the London *Spectator*. He wrote short notes on what each sentence said. He put the notes away for a few days. Then, without the book, he wrote the essay again in his own words and checked it against the original. He found his faults and fixed them. He also turned stories into verse and back, and shuffled his notes to practice ordering ideas. He did this at night, early mornings, and on Sundays.

## What it is

A self-designed writing drill, described by Franklin in his *Autobiography* (Part One, written 1771). The model text was the *Spectator*, the daily London paper of essays by Joseph Addison and Richard Steele (1711–12), collected in volumes. Franklin says he met with "an odd volume," the third [1].

## What it was for

Franklin's father read his letters in a written debate with a friend and told him he fell short "in elegance of expression, in method and in perspicuity" [1]. The exercise isolates those skills, one at a time:

- **Expression:** rebuild each sentence from a bare note and compare word choices.
- **Vocabulary:** turn prose into verse and back, which forces a search for words "of the same import, but of different length" [1].
- **Method (arrangement):** jumble the notes, wait weeks, and put them back in order before writing.

The hidden key is the **delay**. Waiting long enough to forget the wording makes the rebuild a test of skill, not memory.

## Look closely

- The core steps, in Franklin's words: "I took some of the papers, and, making short hints of the sentiment in each sentence, laid them by a few days, and then, without looking at the book, try'd to compleat the papers again ... Then I compared my *Spectator* with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them" [1].
- The verse variant: "I took some of the tales and turned them into verse; and, after a time, when I had pretty well forgotten the prose, turned them back again" [1].
- The ordering variant: "I also sometimes jumbled my collections of hints into confusion, and after some weeks endeavored to reduce them into the best order ... This was to teach me method in the arrangement of thoughts" [1].
- The payoff was modest and honest: he sometimes had "the pleasure of fancying that, in certain particulars of small import," he had improved on the original [1].
- **Dating.** Franklin gives no year. He tells this during his apprenticeship to his brother James (begun at age twelve), just before a passage that opens "When about 16 years of age" [1]. Franklin was born in January 1706, so this is probably around 1720–21 (an estimate). His first published essays, the "Silence Dogood" letters, appeared in his brother's *New England Courant* in 1722 [2].

## From study to practice

This is practice in the strict sense. Nothing Franklin wrote here was meant to be published. Each round repeats one small slice of the writing process (sentence, word choice, order) against a fixed standard. The comparison step is the teacher.

There is a study-like element too (interpretation). The "short hints" are a skeleton of the essay: a plan reverse-engineered from a finished work. Franklin practiced moving from plan to prose by making his own plans out of other people's prose.
