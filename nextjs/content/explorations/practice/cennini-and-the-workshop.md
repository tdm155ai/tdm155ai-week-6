# Cennino Cennini and the workshop: thirteen years, a little each day

**Wall label**

Cennino Cennini trained for twelve years under Agnolo Gaddi, whose father had worked with Giotto. Around 1400 he wrote *Il libro dell'arte*, a handbook for apprentice painters. His plan was this. Spend a year drawing on a small prepared panel. Then spend six years in a workshop grinding colours, boiling glue and gilding. Then spend six more years learning to paint. All the while you draw, "never leaving off, either on holidays or on workdays." He says to copy one great master at a time, then draw from nature. And draw only a little each day, so you do not tire of it. It is a historical account of workshop training.

## What it is

*Il libro dell'arte* is a practical handbook from the late 14th or early 15th century, usually dated to around 1400. It covers drawing, pigments, fresco, panel painting, gilding and more. Its opening chapters set out a *course of practice*. The steps below are from Daniel V. Thompson's 1933 translation.

- **Start small.** Draw on "a little boxwood panel, nine inches wide in each direction," coated with ground bone, using a silver-tipped stylus (ch. V, VIII).
- **Build up slowly.** Run the style so lightly "that you can hardly make out what you first start to do," then strengthen the strokes "little by little, going back many times to produce the shadows" (ch. VIII).
- **Keep it daily and short.** "Practice drawing, drawing only a little each day, so that you may not come to lose your taste for it, or get tired of it" (ch. VIII).
- **After about a year,** move on to pen on paper (ch. XIII).
- **Copy one master, not many.** "Take pains and pleasure in constantly copying the best things which you can find done by the hand of great masters... if you undertake to copy after one master today and after another one tomorrow, you will not acquire the style of either one" (ch. XXVII).
- **Then draw from nature.** It is "the most perfect steersman." Also: "do not fail, as you go on, to draw something every day" (ch. XXVIII).
- **The full term.** One year of drawing, "a good six years" of workshop crafts, then "six more years" of painting (ch. CIV).

## What the evidence says

**Documented:** the text itself, and Cennini's account of his own training ("twelve years by my master, Agnolo di Taddeo"). Agnolo di Taddeo is Agnolo Gaddi. Cennini also gives the chain from Giotto to Taddeo Gaddi to Agnolo to himself. The National Gallery, London, dates the book "probably" to the late 1390s and calls it "an invaluable source" on the late-medieval workshop. It also notes that no painting by Cennini has been securely identified.

**Interpretation, and not to be taken literally:** the National Gallery of Art's Italian Renaissance teaching resource notes that Cennini's thirteen years was "considerably longer than usually occurred." In Venice an apprentice could become a journeyman after two years; in Padua the minimum was three. Read the schedule as an ideal and an argument for seriousness, not as a record of typical training.

**An interesting tension with modern research.** Cennini advises copying *one* master consistently, so that you absorb a style. Kornell and Bjork (2008) found that *mixing* artists helps people learn to *recognise* styles (see *Desirable difficulties*). These are different goals: producing a style versus telling styles apart. They need not conflict. A course might use both: interleave to learn to see differences, and stay with one model to learn to make.

## Optional exercises


Cennini's unit of practice is small and repeated daily.

- **The mark:** one drawing on the little panel, built up by going back over the same strokes "many times."
- **The session:** "a little each day."
- **The term:** years.

He also treats making the materials as practice. Grinding pigment and preparing panels are repeated tasks that teach the medium before you paint with it.

### More ways to try it
- Use Cennini's light-to-dark method as a warm-up. Start with the faintest possible line and add only by going back over it. This teaches restraint and the order of making.
- Make the "little each day" schedule concrete. A five-minute daily drawing log for one week is easy to assign and easy to check.
- Discuss the one-master versus many-masters tension above. Ask students which they need now: to learn to see differences, or to learn to make something.
- Let students make a material, such as ink, a gesso panel or a ground pigment, at least once. Interpretation: knowing the material changes how you practise with it.
