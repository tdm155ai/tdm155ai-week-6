# Queneau's Exercises in Style

**Wall label**

A man with a long neck and a strange hat gets on a crowded Paris bus. He argues with another passenger, then grabs a free seat. Two hours later the narrator sees him at the Gare Saint-Lazare, where a friend tells him to add a button to his coat. That is the whole story. Raymond Queneau told it ninety-nine times: as bare notes, as a dream, backwards, by understatement, in sound effects. *Exercices de style* came out in 1947. The story never improves. The telling changes every time. It has been staged many times since 1949.

## What it is

A book of 99 short pieces, each retelling the same anecdote in a different style or under a different rule. Published by Gallimard in 1947 [1][2]. Queneau wrote the first twelve in May 1942 under the title *Dodécaèdre*, and several appeared in the review *Messages* in 1943 [2]. Barbara Wright's English translation, *Exercises in Style*, appeared in 1958; a 2013 New Directions edition adds exercises Queneau wrote but did not include [1].

Some style titles from the English edition: Notation, Litotes, Retrograde, Precision, Dream, Metaphorically, Onomatopoeia [1].

## What it was for

The book isolates **form** by holding **content** still. Because the events never change, every difference you notice is a choice of voice, order, vocabulary, length, or rule.

Queneau linked the idea to music. French Wikipedia says the book was inspired by Bach's *Art of Fugue* [2], and secondary accounts report that around 1930 Queneau and Michel Leiris heard the work at the Salle Pleyel and wondered whether a literary version was possible. English Wikipedia instead points to Erasmus's *De copia* (1512), whose chapter 33 rewrites one Latin sentence, "Your letter has delighted me very much," about 150 ways [1][3]. Both are fair parallels; only the Bach link has Queneau's own claim behind it, and that claim is reported secondhand here.

## Look closely

- **Same facts, every time.** Bus, long neck, hat, quarrel, seat, Gare Saint-Lazare, button. Check any two exercises: the facts match.
- **Some rules are mechanical, some are tonal.** "Retrograde" reverses the order of events. "Litotes" understates. "Notation" is a flat report. Ask which kind of rule is harder to sustain.
- **From book to stage.** Yves Robert staged exercises at the cabaret La Rose Rouge in Saint-Germain-des-Prés in 1949; Les Frères Jacques performed them in 1954 [2]. The photograph shows a 1981 Dutch production at the Haagse Comedie [4]. Performers treat the exercises like scales.
- **A forerunner of the Oulipo.** Queneau co-founded the Oulipo in 1960, a group that writes under formal constraints [6]. English Wikipedia notes that the book prefigures its work [1]. The exercises came first.

## From study to practice

This sits in the middle. Each exercise is a finished piece, like a study. But the book as a whole works like practice: one small, fixed piece of material, repeated with a single variable changed each time. You learn what each style does by running the same scene through it.

For makers (interpretation): this is a useful model for theater. Rehearsing a scene "as a whisper," "as a farce," "backwards" is the same move.
