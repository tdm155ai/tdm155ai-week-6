# Marking a phrase instead of dancing it full out

**Wall label**

In rehearsal, dancers often "mark." They walk through a phrase small: a flick of the hand for a leap, a step for a turn, the arms roughly in place. Ask them why and most will say it saves energy. Researchers think it may also help the mind. In a 2013 study at UC Santa Cruz, advanced ballet students learned two short phrases. They practised one by marking it and the other full out. Judges then watched them dance both full out. The marked phrases scored better. Marking is a sketch made with the body: it keeps the structure and leaves out the effort.

## What it is

Marking means performing a reduced version of choreography. The cognitive scientist David Kirsh defines it as executing "a dance phrase in a simplified, schematic or abstracted form" [1]. His team observed and interviewed the dancers of Wayne McGregor's company Random Dance while they made a new piece [1][2]. Kirsh reports that, judging from interviews across classical, modern and contemporary dance, "most dancers mark in the normal course of rehearsal and practice" [1].

Marking takes many forms. Kirsh describes "hand marking," where the fingers stand in for the legs [1]. Most marking is closer to full size, but the jumps, speed and force are reduced.

## What it was for

Dancers' own reasons, as Kirsh reports them, are to save energy, to avoid strenuous moves such as jumps, and sometimes to review one aspect of a phrase, "such as tempo, movement sequence, or intention" [1]. He identifies three uses: marking for others (showing someone a phrase), marking to coordinate with a partner, and marking for oneself [1].

The Santa Cruz study tested the cognitive idea directly. Edward Warburton, Margaret Wilson, Molly Lynch and Shannon Cuykendall had dancers rehearse by marking or by dancing full out. They found that "performance was superior in the dancers who had marked" [3]. Their explanation is an "embodied-cognitive-load hypothesis": full-out practice makes the dancer handle the whole physical task and the memory task at once [3]. Warburton told the university's news office that "by reducing the demands on complex control of the body, marking may reduce the multi-layered cognitive load used when learning choreography" [4].

The skill it isolates is the *sequence and structure* of a phrase, apart from its physical execution.

## Look closely

- **Who was tested.** A dance magazine's report gives 38 advanced ballet students, matched for age, gender and training, with the marked and full-out phrase swapped between two groups [5]. We could not read the full paper to confirm these numbers.
- **How simple the phrases were.** The university's report says the routines were relatively simple and designed to be learned quickly [4]. The result may not hold for long or complex choreography.
- **What improved.** The marked phrases were judged "more seamless" and "more fluid" [4][5].
- **Kirsh's argument.** Kirsh compares marking to a pencil sketch: an external structure that the mind can "hang" more detail on than it could hold in imagination alone [1]. This is a theoretical argument based on observation, not the result of the Santa Cruz experiment.
- **The image.** We found no openly licensed picture of marking. The Degas above shows a rehearsal in general.

## From study to practice

Marking sits exactly between the two halves of this pack. It is practice, because dancers do it to learn and keep a phrase in the body. But it works like a sketch. It is a quick, low-cost, reduced version of the final thing, made to think with.

The lesson for other media is tempting but untested (interpretation). A rough read-through, an animatic or a hummed melody may do for actors, animators and musicians what marking does for dancers: separate learning the structure from performing it well.
