# Scales, drills, and chunks: practising the hard bit on its own

**Wall label**

In 1873 Charles-Louis Hanon published sixty piano exercises meant to train finger "agility, independence, strength and evenness." There is no music to interpret in them, only the same finger pattern moved up and down the keyboard. Drills like this isolate one variable. Research on chess suggests why that can work. Masters do not seem to have better memories in general. They have a large store of familiar *chunks*, patterns they see as single units. Drills can build such chunks. But a study of pianists found that *how* they practised predicted next-day performance. How long they practised did not.

## What it is

A **drill** pulls one component out of a skill and repeats it with everything else held constant. Examples include scales, arpeggios, Hanon patterns, *pliés* at the barre, and typing drills. The aim is to make that component automatic, so attention is free for everything else when the skill is used as a whole.

Hanon's *Le Pianiste virtuose en 60 exercices* (Boulogne, 1873) is the textbook case. Its full title promises "agility, independence, strength and the most perfect evenness of the fingers, as well as suppleness of the wrists." Each exercise is a short figure repeated as a sequence up and down the keyboard.

**Part versus whole practice** is the motor-learning name for the same question. Wightman and Lintern (1985) identified three ways to break a task into parts:

- **Segmentation:** practise one stretch of a sequence, such as the last bars of a passage.
- **Fractionation:** practise one of several things normally done at the same time, such as the left hand alone.
- **Simplification:** practise an easier version of the whole task.

## What the evidence says

**Chunking: Chase & Simon (1973).** The study had three players: a master, a Class A player and a beginner. Each viewed a chess position for 5 seconds and then reconstructed it from memory.

- On real middle-game positions, the master placed about 16 pieces correctly on the first try. The Class A player placed about 8 and the beginner about 4.
- On *randomly arranged* boards, the master's advantage disappeared. All three did poorly.

The authors concluded that skill lies in a large store of familiar patterns in long-term memory, not in a larger short-term memory. The sample was three people, so the study is best read as a demonstration of a pattern later research confirmed and refined. It is not a measured effect size.

**Part versus whole.** Naylor and Briggs (1963) proposed a rule of thumb that motor-learning textbooks still use:

- Practise in parts when a task is *complex* but its parts are only loosely connected.
- Practise the whole when the parts are tightly *organised*, that is, when each one depends on the timing of the others.

Wightman and Lintern's review of tracking and manual-control training found that whether part-task training helped depended on how the task was divided. One method they described was backward chaining, a form of segmentation in which the last part is learned first. Interpretation, following Naylor and Briggs: playing hands separately removes the coordination between the hands. If coordination is the actual problem, separate-hands practice may not solve it.

**How, not how much: Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009).** Seventeen advanced piano students practised a difficult three-measure passage from a Shostakovich concerto. They were told to practise until confident, and they were tested the next day.

- Practice time, number of practice attempts and number of complete run-throughs did *not* predict next-day performance.
- Accuracy during practice *did* predict it. Pianists who made more errors while practising performed worse the next day.
- The top-ranked pianists are described as finding exactly where errors happened, fixing them there, and controlling tempo, rather than replaying the whole passage. (We checked this point against summaries of the paper, not the full text.)

**About Hanon specifically,** we found no controlled studies. Its long use is evidence of tradition, not of effectiveness. Some teachers argue that mechanical patterns played without musical attention transfer poorly to real pieces. That view fits the part/whole research, but it has not been tested on Hanon itself.

## Optional exercises


- **Scale or Hanon figure:** one pattern, played over a few octaves at one tempo. Each repetition takes about 10–30 seconds.
- **"The hard bit":** the 1–3 beats (or brush strokes, or lines of code) where the error actually happens. Repeat until correct several times in a row, then put it back into the bars around it.

The second is the more important habit. Most of the work is finding the exact location of the difficulty.

### More ways to try it
- Teach students to *find* the error before drilling it. Ask them to mark the exact beat, stroke or line where things go wrong.
- Count correct repetitions, not total repetitions. Duke and colleagues' data suggest that practising mistakes is worse than practising less.
- Always end a drill by putting the part back into the whole. A part-practice block should end with the full passage, the full drawing or the full program run.
- Use the chess result as a design rule. Build drills from patterns students will actually meet. Random patterns build random skill.
