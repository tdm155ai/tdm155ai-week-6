# The ballet class: the same exercises, every day

**Wall label**

Most ballet classes follow the same order. First the barre: slow exercises holding a rail, for balance and warmth. Then the same exercises in the centre of the room, without support. Then *adage*, slow and sustained. Then *allegro*, the jumps. Ballet dancers take a class like this daily, as students and as professionals. In 1922 a manual of Enrico Cecchetti's method compared the barre to scales and arpeggios. It also set a fixed weekly plan, with particular steps for Monday, Tuesday, and so on. The reason it gives is plain. Once the sequence is memorised, attention is free for weight, line and quality.

## What it is

*A Manual of the Theory and Practice of Classical Theatrical Dancing (Cecchetti Method)* was first published in London in 1922. Cyril W. Beaumont wrote it with the dancer Stanislas Idzikowski and with Cecchetti's help [1][2]. Cecchetti was an Italian dancer and teacher. The Cecchetti Society was founded in London the same year, with him as its first president [2].

The book's practical part runs in the order of a class: exercises at the barre, port de bras (carriage of the arms), centre practice, adage, allegro [1]. Most ballet classes today still follow this order; that is common studio practice, which we did not trace to a single source. The manual ends with a "Table of Daily Exercises for the Week." Each day lists its own adage steps and its own kind of allegro step (Monday: assemblés; Tuesday: petits battements), Monday through Saturday [1].

Quotations below are from the revised edition of 1932, the copy we could read online [1]. We have not compared them with the 1922 text.

## What it was for

The manual says the barre and centre exercises "are to the dancer what scales and arpeggios are to the musician," and that a musician practises scales for "one, two, or more hours" every day [1].

It gives two reasons for fixing the weekly programme [1]:

1. "Regular practice enables the student to memorise the adages so that, not being forced to concentrate on the sequences of movement, he can devote all his attention to the proper distribution of weight, and the development of a sense of line and a correct feeling for quality of movement."
2. In allegro, a fixed week "ensures that every type of step is used," instead of depending on "the whim or mood of the teacher."

The skill being isolated is not a single step. It is the ability to do correct basic movement automatically, so that thought can go elsewhere.

## Look closely

- **Repetition, but not too much.** The manual refuses to say how many times to do each exercise. A good teacher decides per pupil, "for one he will prescribe six pills, for another a single pill will suffice" [1].
- **Quality over quantity.** "It is of far greater importance to execute ten movements correctly than to execute a hundred in a careless or slovenly manner" [1].
- **Fixed and new together.** The set programme does not replace novelty. As pupils progress they should be given "new enchaînements daily," to sharpen their "aptitude for assimilating new sequences of movement" [1].
- **No mirror.** The manual advises against using a mirror at the barre: to see your legs you must drop your head, which is itself a fault [1].
- **In the painting.** Degas shows the barre as a place of work, not performance. The watering can was a fixture of rehearsal rooms, used to damp the floor and keep down dust [3].

## From study to practice

This is practice in its purest form. Nothing in the daily class is a plan for a particular dance. It isolates the basic parts of the vocabulary and repeats them until they no longer need attention.

Near its end, the manual links the two: "let none of this labour be visible in your performance... the true art is that which conceals the labour that produced it" [1]. The class is where the labour happens, so that the stage can hide it.
