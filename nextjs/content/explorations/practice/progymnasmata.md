# The progymnasmata

**Wall label**

For more than a thousand years, students learned to write by climbing the same ladder. The *progymnasmata*, Greek for "preliminary exercises," began with retelling a fable and ended with arguing for or against a law. Each step added one skill to the last. The most used version was written by Aphthonius, a teacher in fourth-century Antioch. Renaissance schools taught it in Latin. In 1563 Richard Rainolde put it into English. His instructions read like a recipe: tell the fable, praise its author, state the moral, compare, give an example, conclude. Students ran the same recipe on new material, again and again.

## What it is

A sequence of short composition exercises, ordered from simple to hard. Several Greek handbooks survive: by Aelius Theon (1st century CE), Hermogenes of Tarsus (2nd century), Aphthonius of Antioch (4th century, a student of Libanius), and Nicolaus the Sophist (5th century) [1][2]. Aphthonius's fourteen, in order [1]:

1. Fable · 2. Narrative · 3. Chreia (anecdote of a saying or deed) · 4. Maxim · 5. Refutation · 6. Confirmation · 7. Commonplace · 8. Encomium (praise) · 9. Invective (blame) · 10. Comparison · 11. Characterization (*ethopoeia*, a speech in someone's voice) · 12. Description (*ekphrasis*) · 13. Thesis · 14. Introduction of a law

## What it was for

Each exercise isolates a move that a full speech or essay needs: telling events clearly, praising, blaming, comparing, imagining a character's voice, describing. Students practiced the parts before they had to combine them. Rainolde calls them "profitable introduccions, or fore exercises, to attain greater arte and knowlege in Rhetorike" [3].

## Look closely

- **A fixed recipe.** Rainolde gives eight steps for an oration on a fable: (1) "recite the fable, as the aucthour telleth it"; (2) praise the author; (3) "place the morall"; (4) declare the nature of the things in the fable; (5) set the characters reasoning with one another, "as the Ant with the Greshopper"; (6) make a similitude; (7) give an example; (8) the epilogue [3]. The fixed form is what makes repetition possible.
- **The ladder is printed as a list.** Rainolde's book opens the exercises with a bracketed list: "A Fable, a Narracion. Chria, Sentence. Confutacion, Confirmacion. Common place..." [3].
- **Latin first.** Rudolph Agricola translated Aphthonius into Latin in the fifteenth century [1]. Reinhard Lorich's annotated Latin edition (Marburg, 1542; revised 1546) "was reprinted hundreds of times by presses all over Europe" [4]. Rainolde's English book is a free adaptation from Lorich's edition [5].
- **In English classrooms.** T. W. Baldwin's study of Elizabethan grammar schools traces Aphthonius in the curriculum Shakespeare's generation would have met [6]. (Claims that Shakespeare used these exercises directly are interpretation.)
- **The image** is the binding of a 1564 Paris edition held by the British Library. A schoolbook, handled.

## From study to practice

This is the clearest case of practice in the pack. Nothing here plans a particular work. The exercises are drills that build up a repertoire of moves, in a fixed order, through repetition on new material.

One link back to studies (interpretation): each finished exercise is a small, complete piece, like an art student's study of a hand. A later speech could draw on a praise, a comparison, or a description practiced before.
