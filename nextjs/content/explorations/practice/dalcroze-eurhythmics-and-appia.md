# Dalcroze eurhythmics and Appia's rhythmic spaces

**Wall label**

Émile Jaques-Dalcroze taught music in Geneva. He found advanced students who could read and play but could not handle "the simplest problems in rhythm." So he made them move. Arms beat time. Feet walked note values. When the teacher called "hopp!" the class switched, mid-step, to a new division of the beat. The aim was to make rhythm automatic in the body, so the mind was free for the next thing. The stage designer Adolphe Appia saw a demonstration and was struck. He drew bare stairs and platforms for these bodies to move on: his "rhythmic spaces." At Hellerau, near Dresden, practice and design shared one building.

## What it is

A system of music training through whole-body movement, which Jaques-Dalcroze (1865–1950) developed while teaching at the Geneva Conservatoire, first with a volunteer class working "outside official hours" [1]. It began with students beating time to their own singing and grew into a full system of movements for every metre and note value [1]. The first public demonstration was at a music festival in Solothurn in 1905 [1]. In 1910 the brothers Wolf and Harald Dohrn invited him to Hellerau, a new garden suburb of Dresden, where a college "for Rhythmic Training" was built for him [1]. By 1912–13 it had over 200 students on the teachers' course and over 600 pupils in all, from sixteen countries [1]. It closed when war broke out in August 1914 [1].

Adolphe Appia (1862–1928) was a Swiss designer and theorist of the stage. He saw Jaques-Dalcroze's demonstration in the spring of 1906 [2]. In 1909–10 he made around twenty drawings of "spaces for rhythmic evolutions," the *Espaces rythmiques*: steps, platforms and walls with no painted scenery [2].

## What it was for

The skill isolated is rhythm: hearing it and doing it at once, without stopping to think. The 1917 English account of the method puts it this way: "There is not time to analyse the music heard; the body must realize before the mind has a clear impression." Once one rhythm is automatic, the pupil learns to take in "a fresh rhythm while still performing the old one" [1].

Jaques-Dalcroze framed the goal as building "an automatic technique" for movements that do not need conscious attention, "so that the latter may be reserved" for expression [1].

Appia's drawings answered a design question: what space does a trained, rhythmic body need? His answer was solid, three-dimensional and plain: stairs and levels that make the body work against weight, rather than painted flats.

## Look closely

- **A concrete drill.** In the exercises on dividing time, "at *hopp* the crotchet must be divided into quavers, triplets, semiquavers, etc.," or the teacher calls a number and the class shows that subdivision in steps [1].
- **Not mere repetition.** "As soon as movements become automatic they are used as units for building up more elaborate movements, and no time is wasted in doing merely mechanical exercises" [1]. Copying a neighbour, the book warns, leaves a pupil "hopelessly confused" [1].
- **The theatre link.** Jaques-Dalcroze names Appia in his own essay as one of the few artists with "an inborn knowledge of the rhythms of space" [1].
- **Performance as the test.** Accounts differ in detail. One modern account says Appia and Jaques-Dalcroze staged the second act of Gluck's *Orpheus* at Hellerau in summer 1912 and repeated it in 1913 [2]. The 1917 book says the June 1913 festival gave "the whole of Gluck's *Orpheus*," with over five thousand attending [1].
- **The photo.** The Bibliothèque de Genève description on Commons names the students, among them Marie Rambert (later a dancer and teacher in London) and Suzanne Ferrière. It places them in front of the Festspielhaus at Hellerau and dates the photo 30 September 1911. The 1917 book says the college was completed in 1911 [1]; we have not checked the building's exact completion date, so treat the setting as the library's identification.

## From study to practice

This case puts the two halves of the week in one place. The eurhythmics class is practice: small units, repeated until automatic, then combined. Appia's rhythmic spaces are studies: drawings made to work out what a stage should be, before any was built. At Hellerau the designer drew for the practitioners, and the practice shaped the design (interpretation, but Appia's own sequence, from seeing the demonstration in 1906 to drawing the spaces in 1909, supports it [2]).
