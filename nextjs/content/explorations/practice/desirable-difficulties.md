# Desirable difficulties: why practice that feels worse often works better

**Wall label**

Some kinds of practice feel smooth. You re-read the notes, or you drill one thing many times in a row. Other kinds feel clumsy. You test yourself before you are ready, wait days between sessions, or mix different problems together. Robert Bjork called the clumsy kinds *desirable difficulties*. They make practice harder and slower. In many experiments they also improve what people remember and can do later. In one study, people learned painters' styles better from mixed examples than from grouped ones. Most participants still believed the grouped examples had worked better. How practice feels is a poor guide to whether it is working.

## What it is

Bjork (1994) distinguished between *performance during training* and *learning*, the lasting change that shows up later and in new situations. Conditions that raise performance during practice can leave little behind. Some conditions that lower performance during practice improve long-term retention and transfer. Bjork called these conditions "desirable difficulties." Three are well studied:

- **Retrieval practice:** recall the material from memory instead of re-reading it.
- **Spacing:** spread practice sessions out over time instead of packing them together.
- **Interleaving:** mix different kinds of examples or problems instead of practising one kind in a block.

The word "desirable" matters. A difficulty helps only if the learner can still respond to it successfully. A task that is too hard is simply too hard.

## What the evidence says

**Retrieval practice: Roediger & Karpicke (2006).** Washington University undergraduates read short prose passages about science topics.

- In Experiment 2 (180 students), one group studied a passage four times (SSSS). Another studied once and then recalled it three times without feedback (STTT).
- On a test five minutes later, the repeated-study group recalled more: 83% versus 71%.
- On a test one week later, the result reversed. The repeated-testing group recalled 61% and the repeated-study group 40%.
- The repeated-study group predicted they would remember *more* after a week.

**Spacing: Cepeda et al. (2006).** This quantitative review of verbal-recall studies covered 839 assessments from 317 experiments. Spaced study beat massed study. The best gap between sessions grew longer as the retention interval grew longer. In other words, if you need to remember something for longer, leave longer gaps between sessions.

**Interleaving and art: Kornell & Bjork (2008).** UCLA students learned the styles of 12 relatively unknown landscape painters from six paintings each. Each painting was shown for 3 seconds.

- Massed condition: one artist's paintings appeared in a row.
- Spaced (interleaved) condition: paintings by different artists were mixed.
- Students then had to name the artist of *new* paintings.
- In Experiment 1a (120 students), accuracy on the first test block was 61% after interleaved study and 35% after massed study. Experiment 1b (72 students) found a similar gap.
- 78% of participants did better with interleaving. 78% said massing had been as good or better.

**Limits.** Most of this evidence comes from laboratory tasks with undergraduates: word lists, prose passages, categories. For complex motor skills, simple-skill findings do not always carry over (Wulf & Shea, 2002). Interleaving also has conditions. In Carvalho and Goldstone (2014), it helped most when categories were similar to each other and easy to confuse. Blocking helped when categories were dissimilar, because then the challenge was noticing what one category's members share. Treat these as strong tendencies, not laws.

## Optional exercises


The repetition is short and specific. Examples:

- one act of recall (close the book and write what you remember)
- one identification (whose painting is this?)
- one problem, chosen from a mixed set

The difficulty comes from the *arrangement* of repetitions, not from making each one longer. You do not need to add time. You put the gaps and the mixing in different places.

### More ways to try it
- Start sessions with a two-minute recall of last week's material before re-showing it. Students will feel less prepared than they would after a review. That feeling is expected.
- Interleave examples whenever students must learn to *tell things apart*: typefaces, artists, compositional strategies, bugs. Use blocking when students first need to see what one category's examples have in common.
- Before a practice activity, ask students which method they expect to work better. Record the vote, then show the result. Kornell and Bjork's 78%/78% finding is a useful thing to discuss.
- When a student says "but I knew it when I re-read it," name the difference between fluency and learning.
