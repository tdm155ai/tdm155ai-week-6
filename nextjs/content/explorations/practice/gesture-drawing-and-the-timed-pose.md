# Gesture drawing and the timed pose

**Wall label**

A life-drawing session usually starts fast. The model holds a pose for thirty seconds, then one minute, then two. You cannot finish anything in that time, and that is the point. Each drawing records only the action of the body: the lean, the twist, where the weight falls. Kimon Nicolaïdes made this *gesture drawing* the centre of his course, published in 1941 as *The Natural Way to Draw*. His idea was to draw what the figure is doing before you draw what it looks like. The timer prevents polishing. Each pose is one repetition. You will do dozens in a single evening.

## What it is

Gesture drawing is rapid drawing of a figure's movement and weight, usually from a model holding short poses. Nicolaïdes (1891–1938) taught at the Art Students League of New York. His book was published after his death, completed by his former student Mamie Harmon. It is organised as a schedule of exercises meant to be worked through over months. Gesture drawing comes early, alongside slow *contour drawing*, where the eye follows the edge of the form very slowly. Later exercises cover weight and mass, and drawing from memory.

The book pairs a fast exercise with a slow one. Gesture trains you to see the whole action at once. Contour trains slow, careful attention to edges. Each corrects the faults the other encourages. That pairing is our interpretation of how the course is structured, not a quotation from it.

In the French studio vocabulary, a quick figure sketch is a *croquis*. Many open studios run "croquis" sessions of short poses only.

## What the evidence says

**Documented:**

- Nicolaïdes' book was published by Houghton Mifflin in 1941 and has been in print for decades.
- Its core methods are contour, gesture, weight/mass and memory drawing [2]. Secondary accounts place gesture and contour at the start of the course.
- Its programme is long and based on a schedule. One reader who worked through it estimated that it calls for thousands of gesture drawings [5].

**Convention, not doctrine:** the familiar warm-up sequence of 30-second, 1-, 2- and 5-minute poses, followed by longer poses, is a common studio practice. It varies from studio to studio. We did not find a single documented origin for it. Nor did we verify Nicolaïdes' exact pose timings against the 1941 text.

**What the research does and does not show:** we found no controlled study of gesture drawing itself. Two lines of research are relevant. Ericsson's work (see *Deliberate practice*) says that short, focused, repeated attempts with feedback build skill. Desirable-difficulty research (see *Desirable difficulties*) says that varied rather than identical repetitions tend to transfer better. Changing poses every 30–60 seconds builds that variation into the format. Treat this as a plausible link, not a tested result.

## Optional exercises


What gets isolated is **the line of action**: the one or two curves that carry the figure's weight and direction. Proportion, anatomy, shading and likeness are left out on purpose.

- A single repetition is 15 seconds to 2 minutes.
- A warm-up block is commonly 10–20 poses.
- Because the timer ends each attempt, every repetition is followed at once by the next, slightly different problem.

### More ways to try it
- Use 30-second poses at the start of any studio session, not only figure drawing. Short timed drawing of objects, hands, crowds or architecture works the same way.
- Have students hold the drawing tool loosely and keep it moving for the whole pose. If a student stops to erase, the pose is probably too long for them.
- Keep the gesture drawings. Laying out 40 of them on a table shows progress better than any single finished drawing.
- Pair a fast round with a slow one, such as a 10-minute contour drawing, and ask what each made easier to see.
