# Deliberate practice, and what the 10,000 hours left out

**Wall label**

In 1993 three psychologists asked student violinists in Berlin how much they had practised alone since childhood. The best students reported the most hours. The authors called this kind of practice *deliberate practice*. It is effortful, aimed at a specific weakness, and gives you feedback. It is often not enjoyable. In 2008 Malcolm Gladwell turned an average from this study into the "10,000-hour rule." The paper did not propose such a rule. A 2014 meta-analysis found that practice explains some of the differences in skill, but far from all of them. How you practise matters as much as how long.

## What it is

Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer defined deliberate practice as structured activity designed to improve performance. Typically a teacher designs it. It targets what you cannot yet do, gives feedback, and allows repetition and correction. They separated it from *work* (performing for pay or reward) and *play* (doing the activity for enjoyment). On their account, deliberate practice is effortful and not inherently enjoyable, and people can sustain only a few hours of it a day.

The 1993 paper argued that "individual differences in ultimate performance can largely be accounted for by differential amounts of past and current levels of practice" (p. 392). That is a strong claim. Later work tested it.

## What the evidence says

**The original study (1993).** The main study had 30 violin students at a West Berlin music academy, 10 in each of three groups: "best" violinists (judged by their professors as likely soloists), "good" violinists, and students in the music-education programme. Participants kept a one-week diary and gave retrospective estimates of their weekly practice at each age. Researchers summed these estimates to age 18. The best group reported a mean of about 7,410 hours of solitary practice by age 18. The good group reported about 5,301 hours. The 10,000-hour figure comes from the same estimates extended to age 20. It is a group average for the top group. It is not a threshold. Retrospective estimates going back to childhood are also a weak measure.

**The popularization (2008).** Gladwell's *Outliers* has a chapter titled "The 10,000-Hour Rule." It presented roughly 10,000 hours as the amount needed for world-class skill. Ericsson later objected in print (Ericsson & Pool, *Peak*, 2016). His points: the number was an average, not a requirement. Many of the best violinists had fewer hours. And the type of practice mattered more than the total.

**The meta-analysis (2014).** Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald pooled 157 effect sizes from 88 studies (11,135 participants). The average correlation between deliberate practice and performance was r = .35. That means practice explained about 12% of the variance in performance. The figure differed by domain:

| Domain | Variance explained by deliberate practice |
|---|---|
| Games (e.g., chess) | 26% |
| Music | 21% |
| Sports | 18% |
| Education | 4% |
| Professions | less than 1% |

Practice explained less in studies that logged it as it happened (5%) than in studies that relied on retrospective interviews (20%). This suggests that memory-based estimates may inflate the relationship.

**The replication (2019).** Macnamara and Maitra repeated the violin study with 39 violinists and kept the researchers blind to each participant's skill group. They did not replicate the key result. The best violinists had *not* accumulated significantly more solitary practice by age 18 than the good violinists. Practice remained associated with performance overall, but more weakly than in the original report; this association does not isolate a causal effect.

**The response.** Ericsson and Harwell (2019) argued that many studies in the meta-analysis measured "practice" loosely. In their view, these studies did not measure deliberate practice as he had defined it, and so they underestimated its effect. This dispute is not settled. These findings leave room for a qualified account of practice. Practice is necessary and important, but it is not the only cause of skill. Its quality is hard to measure.

## Optional exercises


Deliberate practice is not "doing the thing a lot." It is doing *the part you are bad at*, at the edge of your ability, with feedback after each attempt. One repetition is short. It might be one passage, one move, one stroke, or one function. The session ends while you can still concentrate. The 1993 participants reported practising alone for a few hours a day at most.

### More ways to try it
- Ask students to name the specific sub-skill they are working on before they start. "Getting better at drawing" is not a practice target. "Ellipses in perspective" is.
- Build in feedback for each attempt, such as a reference image, a test case, a recording, or a peer. Without feedback, repetition mostly makes existing habits stronger.
- When students cite the "10,000-hour rule," use it to teach how a study becomes a slogan. Show them the three numbers: 7,410 hours (an average), 12% (the variance explained), and 39 (the replication sample).
- Keep practice blocks short. Interpretation: an hour of focused work on one weakness is probably worth more than an afternoon of rehearsing what students can already do.
