# Meyerhold's biomechanics études

**Wall label**

The director Vsevolod Meyerhold once played Treplev in Stanislavski's *Seagull*. By the early 1920s he was training actors in a very different way. He called it biomechanics. Students learned short, fixed sequences of movement called études: Shooting the Bow, Throwing the Stone, the Slap, the Stab to the Chest. Each tells a tiny story of an action, usually with no real object at all. The bow and the stone are mimed. Every move has the same three beats: prepare, do, stop. Students repeat the études until the pattern lives in the body. Then they bring that control to any role.

## What it is

A set of choreographed training sequences, each lasting a minute or two, built from a fixed pattern of moves. A 1922 *Programme of Biomechanics* from Meyerhold's workshop, held in the Russian State Archive of Literature and Art, sets out the basic unit of acting as a three-part cycle: intention, execution, reaction [1]. Later teachers use Russian terms such as *otkaz* (preparation), *posyl* (sending, the action) and *tochka* (the point or stop) for the parts of a movement; spellings and the exact list vary between teachers [1][2][8]. Meyerhold presented the idea publicly in a lecture, "The Actor of the Future and Biomechanics," at the Moscow Conservatoire on 12 June 1922 [3].

Meyerhold was arrested in June 1939 and shot in February 1940 [4]. The études survived through his collaborator Nikolai Kustov. In 1972 the Moscow Theatre of Satire invited Kustov to train a small group of its young actors, among them Gennadi Bogdanov and Alexei Levinsky, who later taught the études internationally [8].

## What it was for

Not to plan a production, but to build an actor who can carry out any plan precisely. Jonathan Pitches describes the three-part structure working "from the micro-gestural to the macro-textual": in a single gesture, in a whole étude, and in a director's staging [1].

The skill isolated is control of the body in time: a clear preparation, a committed action, and a clean stop, done the same way each time and in rhythm with a partner.

## Look closely

- **The named études.** Pitches lists the "post-perestroika" set taught by Kustov's pupils as Throwing the Stone, Shooting the Bow, Stab to the Chest and Leap on the Chest [1]. Other sources add the Slap (to the face) and the Stab with the Dagger [5].
- **How many survive.** A 1995 article reports that "of the twenty eight that Meyerhold created, only five 'pure' ones now exist" [5]. We have not confirmed the figure of twenty-eight in a scholarly source; treat it as reported.
- **Missing objects.** In four of the five extant études the weapon is imagined, not held [1]. Pitches traces this to stick work: students first balance and throw real sticks, then mime absent ones [1].
- **From drill to stage.** Pitches writes that the Leap on the Chest variant "was, famously, incorporated" into *The Magnanimous Cuckold* [1]. That production opened in Moscow in 1922 on Lyubov Popova's bare constructivist set, shown above [6]. (Several secondary sources give 25 April 1922 as the opening night; we could not confirm it in a source we could read.)
- **Older roots.** Before the Revolution, Meyerhold's studio drilled commedia dell'arte skills: "ways of walking, leaps, bows and blows with a stick" (a student, quoted in [1]).

## From study to practice

This is practice in the strict sense. An étude is a small, complete, repeatable piece. It is not a scene from any play. It is drilled until it is automatic, so that the actor's attention is free during performance.

The word matters. In music an *étude* is a study written to drill one technical problem (see the Chopin entry in this pack). Meyerhold's études do the same for the actor's body. Set them beside Stanislavski's 1898 score: there the director wrote every move in advance; here the actor is trained so that moves can be set, changed and repeated with precision (interpretation).
