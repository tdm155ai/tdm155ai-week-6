# Seurat's croquetons for La Grande Jatte

**Wall label**

*A Sunday on La Grande Jatte* is about two by three metres. On the way to it, Seurat made many small oil sketches on wooden panels about the size of a paperback cover. He called them *croquetons*. He painted them outdoors on the island in the Seine. In them he tried out where the shade falls, which greens to set against which oranges, and where a figure should stand. The Art Institute of Chicago counts more than twenty painted studies. In one of them, most of the figures never reached the final picture. The sketches were cheap enough to be wrong.

## What it is

A group of preparatory works for Seurat's *A Sunday on La Grande Jatte — 1884* (Art Institute of Chicago, 1926.224). The final painting is oil on canvas, 207.5 × 308.1 cm. The museum dates it "1884–86, border added 1888–89."

The preparatory works include small oil sketches on panel, conté crayon drawings, and larger studies on canvas. The Art Institute says one of its panels "is one of over 20 painted studies Georges Seurat made" for the picture. The museum's 2004 exhibition catalogue, *Seurat and the Making of La Grande Jatte*, brought together more than 55 preparatory works across media.

The National Gallery, London, holds several of the panels. The one shown here (NG6556) is oil on wood, 16 × 25 cm, dated 1884–5. The National Gallery notes that Seurat "made small studies outdoors on wooden panels, which he called croquetons."

## What the sketch was for

Each croqueton answers a narrow question. The National Gallery reads NG6556 as one of the earliest. It was painted on unprimed wood, probably in the morning, so the light falls from the opposite side to the finished picture. Grass fills about three-quarters of it. The question there is landscape and light, not people.

Other panels test figures. The Art Institute's own oil sketch (1981.15, 15.5 × 24.3 cm) has "20 or more figures" that "have little to do with the final version." A trio at the right, with an elderly seated figure, was replaced in the final painting by a man in a top hat and a woman walking a monkey on a leash.

Seurat kept revising the big canvas too. The Art Institute notes that he "returned to the work two years after its start date, amplifying the silhouettes of some figures and adding others."

I read the croquetons as the place where Seurat could afford to be loose. The final canvas is tight and slow. The panels are quick, brushy, and disposable.

## Look closely

- The brushwork is short, criss-crossed strokes, not dots. In its note on a companion panel (NG6560), the National Gallery dates these sketches by exactly this: Seurat "had not yet fully developed the pointillist technique he used in the painting itself."
- Look in the greens for flecks of orange, pink, wine red, and light blue. The National Gallery points these out as Seurat's way of keeping a mostly green panel from going flat.
- The figures are tiny dark verticals near the tree line. They are placeholders, not people yet.
- The water at upper left is a band of pale, broken strokes, almost white. Compare it with the river in the final picture.
- Light comes from a different side than in the finished canvas. This is a study of a time of day, not yet of the composition.

## From study to practice

Seurat made more than twenty panels of the same island. That is close to practice: one motif, repeated, until the eye can predict what the light will do. But the goal is still one finished painting. The repetition serves a plan.

What carries over to practice, I think, is the size. A panel this small takes minutes. You can make several in a session, compare them, and throw some away. The croqueton is the visual-art version of a short drill: limited in scope, quick to finish, easy to repeat.
