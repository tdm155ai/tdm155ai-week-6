# Ground plans in the Sergeyev notations

**Wall label**

Around 1900, the Imperial Ballet in St Petersburg began writing its ballets down. The notators used a system by the dancer Vladimir Stepanov that borrowed from music: note heads on staves for the head, the arms and the legs. Above the steps they drew *ground plans*: little maps of the stage with a mark for each dancer and arrows for where they go. After the Revolution, the régisseur Nikolai Sergeyev took the notations to the West and used them to stage Petipa's ballets in Paris and London. Since 1969 they have been at Harvard's Houghton Library. Choreographers still use them to rebuild Petipa's ballets.

## What it is

The Sergeyev Collection (Houghton Library, MS Thr 245) holds choreographic notations and music for more than twenty ballets from the Imperial repertory [1][2]. Most of the ballets are by Marius Petipa or Lev Ivanov.

Stepanov's system is built on Western music notation. Doug Fullington describes it as note heads and stems on three staves: two lines for the head, neck and torso; three lines for the arms and hands; four lines for the legs and feet [3]. The notations include ground plans, "showing the position of dancers on stage," that correspond to a run of notated bars [3]. Mime is written out in prose.

After Stepanov died in 1896, Alexander Gorsky continued the work. Sergeyev then supervised it [1]. Who actually wrote the pages is disputed. The Petipa Society says most of the notation was written by his assistants, all dancers in the company [2]. Fullington, who studied the hands closely, concludes that most of the surviving notations are in Sergeyev's own hand [3]. Harvard bought the *Swan Lake* notation in 1967 and the rest in 1969 [1][2].

## What it was for

The official aim was a complete written record of the repertory. Fullington argues that the most detailed early notations, including *La Bayadère*, aimed at "a finished, prescriptive document" [3].

Most of the surviving pages are something else. Fullington calls them "unpolished eyewitness records of dance and action as rehearsed in the studio or performed on stage." He quotes the historian Roland John Wiley: they are mostly "reminders for dancers already familiar with the choreography," full of erasures, "practical documents" [3]. In many of them Sergeyev wrote down only the ground plans and the leg and foot movements. The upper body appears only sometimes [3].

The ground plans answer the blocking question: who stands where, and when they move. For a corps of dozens of dancers, that is the hardest part to remember and the easiest to draw.

## Look closely

- **Numbered dancers.** The small circles and squares carry numbers. Each mark is a particular dancer, not just "a dancer."
- **Arrows.** Dotted lines with arrowheads show which way a group travels during the passage.
- **Plan before steps.** Fullington notes that in the *Bayadère* notation, ground plans "are written on the staves ahead of each corresponding passage of notated steps" [3]. The page shows the formation first, then what happens in it.
- **Margin box.** The ruled box at right is for prose notes. Fullington says Sergeyev used the right margin for annotations in this manuscript [3].
- **Pencil.** The whole *Bayadère* notation is in pencil, bound as one volume of 141 pages [3].

## From study to practice

These pages sit in an awkward, useful place. They look like plans, and they were later used as plans: Sergeyev staged ballets from them in Paris and London, and Alexei Ratmansky and others have used them since [1][2]. But most of them were written *after* the dance existed, by people watching rehearsal.

That makes them a memory aid for practice. A répétiteur who already knows the ballet needs only the formation and the feet. Everything else is in the dancers' bodies. The notation fills the gaps that repetition leaves (interpretation).
