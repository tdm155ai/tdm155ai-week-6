# Hokusai Manga: a sketchbook printed for copying

**Wall label**

On an autumn visit to Nagoya, Hokusai met with pupils there. According to the preface of the first volume, he made "over three hundred sketches of all kinds" there, from Buddhas and scholars "on down to birds, beasts, grasses, and trees." His pupils gathered them and a Nagoya publisher printed them in 1814 as *Hokusai manga*, roughly "random sketches." The book sold. More volumes followed, fifteen in all by 1878. Each page is a crowd of small figures with no story connecting them. It looks like a sketchbook. It worked like a copybook. Students and amateurs drew from it, again and again.

## What it is

A series of woodblock-printed books of Hokusai's drawings. The first volume appeared in 1814, when Hokusai was 55. Ten volumes came out between 1814 and 1819. Five more were added between 1834 and 1878, three of them after Hokusai's death in 1849. Art historians do not accept the last volume as authentic. The pages are printed in black, grey, and a pale flesh tone.

The preface to volume one, by the Nagoya artist Hanshū Sanjin, says the sketches were made during a visit to the pupil Gekkōtei Bokusen. The publisher was Eirakuya Tōshirō of Nagoya.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a set of volumes 1–11 (JIB111a–k), each about 22.7 × 15.8 cm.

## What the sketch was for

The original sketches were Hokusai's own quick studies of whatever he saw or imagined. Printing them changed their job. They became models. Wikipedia's summary of the scholarship calls the first volume "an art instruction book." In the Edo period the word *manga* meant informal or random drawings.

Hokusai made other books in the same years that were more openly instructional. *Ryakuga hayaoshie* (Quick Lessons in Simplified Drawing), volume 1 of 1812, builds subjects from circles, triangles, and straight lines. The University of Pennsylvania's catalogue note says Hokusai "believed that anything could be rendered from simple shapes."

I read the *Manga* as a sketchbook that was made public so other hands could repeat it.

## Look closely

- On the left page of the image: a smith swinging a hammer, two men sawing a plank on a trestle, a man bending a long bow or pole, porters with loads on shoulder poles, a dog.
- On the right page: acrobats tumbling over a giant anchor, a man pounding in a tub, fishermen raising square dip nets.
- No frame or ground line around most figures. Each one floats on the page, like a study on a sheet.
- Many figures are seen from behind or mid-action. The brush records the pose, not the face.
- Scale is mixed. A huge net sits beside a tiny crouching figure. This is a page of separate studies, not a scene.

## From study to practice

This is the clearest case in this slice where a sketch becomes a practice tool. The sketches were made once, quickly. Then they were printed so that many people could copy them many times. The same pose of a man with a saw could be drawn a hundred times by a hundred students.

Drawing manuals with pictures to copy were an established kind of book in Edo Japan. Hokusai published more than one in the 1810s, including the *Ryakuga hayaoshie* volumes of 1812 and 1814. The model is not the plan for one finished work. It is a stock of small forms that the hand learns, so it can use them later without looking.
