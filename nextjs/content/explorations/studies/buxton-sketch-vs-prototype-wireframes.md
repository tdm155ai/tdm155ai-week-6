# Buxton's sketch vs. prototype: paper wireframes in interaction design

**Wall label**

A paper wireframe is a sketch of a screen: boxes for images, lines for text, an arrow for "tap here." In *Sketching User Experiences* (2007), the designer and researcher Bill Buxton argues that a sketch and a prototype are not the same thing at different levels of polish. They have different jobs. A sketch should "suggest," "explore," "question," and "provoke." A prototype should "describe," "refine," "answer," and "resolve." Sketches are cheap, quick, and plentiful. "If you can't afford to throw it away when done," Buxton writes, "it is probably not a sketch." Make many. Keep few.

*The photo is of Buxton himself. His drawings are in copyright, and we did not find a verified, openly licensed wireframe photograph. Students' own sketches work better here anyway.*

## What it is

Bill Buxton's book *Sketching User Experiences: Getting the Design Right and the Right Design* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2007). At the time Buxton was at Microsoft Research. The book argues that interaction design needs its own kind of sketching, in the way that architecture and industrial design have theirs.

A short companion text by Buxton, "What Sketches (and Prototypes) Are and Are Not," lists the qualities of a sketch. Sketches are, in his words:

- **Quick.** "A sketch is quick to make, or at least gives the impression that that is so."
- **Inexpensive.** "Cost must not inhibit the ability to explore a concept, especially early in the design process."
- **Disposable.** "If you can't afford to throw it away when done, it is probably not a sketch."
- **Plentiful.** "Their meaning or relevance is generally in the context of a collection or series, not as an isolated rendering."
- **Ambiguous.** Sketches "are intentionally ambiguous," and can be read in new ways "even by the person who drew them."

He also lists timely, clear vocabulary, distinct gesture, constrained resolution, and appropriate degree of refinement.

## What the sketch was for

Buxton's table sets the two side by side by intent:

| Sketch | Prototype |
|---|---|
| Invite | Attend |
| Suggest | Describe |
| Explore | Refine |
| Question | Answer |
| Propose | Test |
| Provoke | Resolve |
| Tentative, non-committal | Specific depiction |

He says sketches "dominate the early ideation stages," while prototypes cluster later, "where things are converging within the design funnel." The reason is cost. The investment in a prototype is larger, so there are fewer of them.

For a student making an app or a website, the paper wireframe is the sketch. It lets you try ten layouts before you build one. It lets a user point at a box and say "I thought that was a button" before you have written any code.

## Look closely

Use your own wireframes, or a classmate's, for this.

- Line quality. Buxton says sketch lines often run past their endpoints. Do yours? Does that make the drawing look more open to change?
- Resolution. Is there real text in the boxes, or squiggles? Buxton warns against going "beyond 'good enough'."
- Number. Is there one wireframe or a stack? His word is "plentiful."
- Does the drawing look more finished than the thinking behind it? He calls this "appropriate degree of refinement."
- Ask someone else to read it aloud. Do they see something you did not intend? That is the ambiguity working.

## From study to practice

Buxton is writing about sketching as a design stage, not about drills. But two of his qualities point toward practice: "quick" and "plentiful." A sketch only stays quick if your hand already knows the conventions — box, line, arrow, squiggle for text. Designers build that vocabulary by drawing many screens.

So there is a practice underneath the sketch. I read Buxton's list as a description of what a practised sketcher's output looks like. Students can drill the vocabulary separately, so that when they sketch for real, the drawing does not slow down the thinking.
