# Bach's Inventions and Sinfonias

**Wall label**

In 1720 Bach started a little keyboard book for his nine-year-old son, Wilhelm Friedemann. It begins with how to read clefs and play ornaments, and a short piece with the fingering written in. Toward the back are short pieces labeled "Praeambulum" and "Fantasia." In 1723 Bach copied them out again, cleaned them up, put them in order of key, and gave them a title page. That page promised two things at once: to teach the hands to play two and three independent lines, and to give "a strong foretaste of composition." The exercise and the art were meant to be the same thing.

## What it is

- **The notebook.** The *Clavier-Büchlein vor Wilhelm Friedemann Bach* is dated "begun in Cöthen, 22 January 1720." It is now at Yale. Its first pages explain clefs and ornaments. It includes the *Applicatio* in C major, BWV 994, a very short piece with Bach's own fingering, which is rare for him (Wikipedia, Klavierbüchlein; Yale catalog).
- **The early versions.** At the back of the notebook are the pieces we now call the Inventions (there labeled "Praeambula") and Sinfonias ("Fantasiae"). The last pages are lost, so one Fantasia is incomplete and one is missing (Scheideler 2014).
- **The fair copy.** In 1723 Bach wrote all thirty pieces out again in a separate manuscript (Berlin, Mus. ms. Bach P 610). He made small changes and reordered them by ascending key: 15 two-part Inventions (BWV 772–786) and 15 three-part Sinfonias (BWV 787–801). He was not working from scratch. He was revising (Scheideler 2014; IMSLP).
- **The title page.** It begins "Auffrichtige Anleitung," usually translated "Honest" or "Upright Instruction." One common translation of the whole reads: "Forthright instruction, wherewith lovers of the clavier, especially those desirous of learning, are shown in a clear way not only 1) to learn to play two voices clearly, but also after further progress 2) to deal correctly and well with three obbligato parts, moreover at the same time to obtain not only good ideas, but also to carry them out well, but most of all to achieve a cantabile style of playing, and thereby to acquire a strong foretaste of composition" (Wikipedia translation). The German key phrases are "eine cantable Art im Spielen" and "einen starcken Vorschmack von der Composition."
- The collection was not printed until 1801 (Scheideler 2014).

## What the study was for

The title page names the skills it isolates:

1. **Two hands, two independent voices.** Each hand carries its own melody. Neither is "just accompaniment."
2. **Then three voices,** which is harder because one hand must sometimes carry two lines.
3. **Inventing an idea and carrying it out.** In the title page's words: "not only good ideas, but also to carry them out well." Each Invention takes one short idea and builds the whole piece from it. (Scholars often connect the word *inventio* to rhetoric, where it meant finding the material for a speech. That is background, not something Bach says here.)
4. **Singing on the keyboard.** The word is *cantable*: play so each line sounds like it is sung.

We know Bach taught this way. Ernst Ludwig Gerber wrote about his father's lessons with Bach around 1725: "In the first lesson he put his Inventions before him. After he had studied these to Bach's satisfaction, there followed a series of suites and then the Well-Tempered Clavier" (quoted in Scheideler 2014). Christoph Wolff has suggested that the fair copy, with its teaching-focused title page, may also have helped Bach show he could teach when he applied for the Leipzig cantor's post in 1723. That is a hypothesis, not a documented fact (Scheideler 2014, citing Wolff 2000).

## Look closely

Score: [Inventions and Sinfonias on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Inventions_and_Sinfonias,_BWV_772-801_(Bach,_Johann_Sebastian)), including a facsimile of the 1723 autograph (P 610). The 1720 notebook: [Klavierbüchlein on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Klavierb%C3%BCchlein_f%C3%BCr_Wilhelm_Friedemann_Bach_(Bach,_Johann_Sebastian)).

Invention No. 1 in C major, BWV 772:

- **Bar 1.** The right hand states a short figure (a quick run up and a turn back), then the left hand repeats it one octave lower. One idea, two voices.
- **Bars 3–4.** The figure appears upside down (inversion) in the top voice. Under it, the bass plays the first notes of the figure in slower values (augmentation). The passage moves toward G major (teoria.com analysis).
- **Bars 7–8.** The music of bars 1–2 comes back in G major, with the hands swapped: what the right hand played, the left hand now plays, and vice versa. This is called invertible counterpoint (teoria.com).
- **Bars 15–22.** The figure and its inversion are traded between the hands, and the music finds its way back to C (teoria.com).
- **Whole piece.** Count how many bars contain *no* form of the opening figure. The usual claim is that almost every note comes from it. Check it yourself.

## From study to practice

The Inventions sit exactly in the middle of this pack's two meanings. They began as material in a family practice book, ordered by difficulty. Then Bach revised them as finished works, with a title page saying what they train. A student repeats them to get the hands independent. But the same pieces model *how to compose*: take one idea, turn it upside down, slow it down, move it to a new key, give it to the other hand. Playing them is practice. Studying how they are made is a lesson in planning. For a student, the "practice piece" and the "model of composition" are the same object.
