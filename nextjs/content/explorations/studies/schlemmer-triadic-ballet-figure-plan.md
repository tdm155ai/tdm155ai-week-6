# Schlemmer's figure plan for the Triadic Ballet

**Wall label**

Oskar Schlemmer's *Triadic Ballet* had three dancers, twelve dances and eighteen costumes. The costumes were the point: padded cloth and stiff papier-mâché shapes of discs, spirals, spheres and cones that turned a dancer into a moving sculpture. This chart is the ballet on one sheet. Three columns are the three parts of the evening. Twelve numbered rows are the twelve dances. Each box shows which figures are on stage. You can read the whole structure at a glance: where a solo sits, where three figures meet, how the evening builds. It works like a storyboard, with costumes in place of camera shots.

## What it is

*Das Triadische Ballett* had its full premiere on 30 September 1922 at the Württemberg State Theatre in Stuttgart [1]. Schlemmer developed it from 1912 with the dancers Albert Burger and Elsa Hötzel. Part of it was shown in 1916 [1]. Three dancers, one woman and two men, perform twelve dances in eighteen costumes [1][2]. The evening has three parts: a yellow series, a pink series and a black series [2][3].

The chart shown here is one of several versions of a figure plan. Commons gives its source as *Die Bühne im Bauhaus* (Bauhausbücher 4, Munich, 1925). We did not check the plate against a copy of the book.

Harvard's Busch-Reisinger Museum owns a coloured version of the same kind of sheet: *Costume Designs for the "Triadic Ballet"*, 1926, ink, watercolour and metallic paint (BR50.428). Its catalogue describes twenty figures in a grid of three columns: yellow boxes at left, white in the centre, black at right [4]. The Bauhaus-Archiv in Berlin holds a later figure plan (1927) for a shortened, eight-dance version that Hermann Scherchen was to set to percussion music. That project was never realised [5].

## What it was for

The chart answers a structural question: what is the order of the evening, and who is on stage in each part? It does not record steps. It records casting, costume and sequence.

That is why it looks so much like a storyboard. A director can check the rhythm of a whole piece on one sheet: solos against groups, simple shapes against complicated ones. The 1927 plan in Berlin shows Schlemmer using a figure plan again, this time for the eight-dance version first shown at Donaueschingen in 1926 [5]. We have not compared its layout with this one. (The comparison to a storyboard is our interpretation.)

## Look closely

- **Three columns, twelve rows.** The rows are numbered 1–12. Rows 1–5 sit in the first column, 6–8 in the second, 9–12 in the third.
- **Count the figures.** One, two or three per box. We count twenty in all, the same number Harvard's catalogue gives for its coloured sheet [4].
- **Empty boxes.** Most rows have more boxes than figures. The blank cells are as much a part of the plan as the drawn ones.
- **Shapes, not people.** Faces are tiny. The drawing is about silhouette: hoop skirts, discs, a spiral, a body made of spheres.
- **Row 12.** The last row has a single figure. Compare it with the busy rows just above it.

## From study to practice

This is clearly planning. It fixes the structure of the work before, or alongside, rehearsal. It tells the dancers what each scene is, but not how to move.

The costumes were partly padded cloth and partly stiff papier-mâché, coated with paint [6]. They limited what a body could do. Much of the movement had to be discovered in the costumes, by repetition. The plan sets the frame, and the dancers work out the movement inside it (interpretation).
