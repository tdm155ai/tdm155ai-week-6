# Kurosawa's paintings for Kagemusha and Ran

**Wall label**

In the late 1970s Akira Kurosawa could not get money for *Kagemusha*. Toho balked at the budget. So he painted the film instead. These were not thumbnails for a crew. They were finished pictures in strong color, scene after scene. Kurosawa had trained as a painter before he went into film. He said he usually sketched when words failed him on set, but this time he wanted "to leave behind some record of my plans." George Lucas and Francis Ford Coppola helped bring in 20th Century-Fox, and the film was made. For *Ran* (1985) he painted again.

## What it is

A large body of mixed-media paintings (one account lists pencil, ink, crayon, pastel and watercolor [4]) made by the director himself as pre-visualization for *Kagemusha* (1980) and *Ran* (1985), and later films. Many were published and exhibited. A 2004–05 exhibition at the Mori Art Gallery in Tokyo is reported to have shown storyboards from *Kagemusha*, *Ran*, *Dreams*, *Rhapsody in August*, *The Sea Watches* and *Madadayo* [4].

## What it was for

Three jobs at once (the first two documented, the third interpretation):

1. **Holding a film that might never be made.** Kurosawa said: "In many of my movies when I could not find words to explain to my cast and crew what I wanted, I would make a sketch. But this case was special. I wanted to leave behind some record of my plans" [1].
2. **Showing what the money would buy.** Toho withdrew when it saw the budget; Coppola and Lucas then persuaded Fox to cover part of it, and Toho agreed to the rest [1][2]. A painted film is a persuasive object.
3. **Deciding color and mass before the shoot.** *Ran* and *Kagemusha* are films of large colored groups moving across landscapes. A painting fixes those relations (which color army, against which sky) before costumes are dyed and extras are hired.

## Look closely

- **Who made them.** The director, not a hired storyboard artist. That is unusual in feature filmmaking.
- **Finish level.** Compare these with the Disney boards. Disney's were quick and replaceable; these are finished pictures. Ask what each level of finish is good for.
- **The funding gap.** TCM's account: Toho dropped out over a $5.5 million budget; Lucas and Coppola went to Fox; then Toho came back in [1]. Wikipedia says Lucas and Coppola are credited as executive producers on the international version for this reason [2].
- **Ran as the long project.** Wikipedia reports that Kurosawa wrote the *Ran* script after *Dersu Uzala* (1975), let it sit for years, called *Kagemusha* a "dress rehearsal" for it, and painted storyboards for the film; the paintings were later published with the screenplay [3]. Wikipedia's claim that he painted "every shot" over "ten years" is probably an overstatement; treat it with care.
- **Images here are omitted** because the paintings are under copyright. Look at them in the published books or the Criterion release of *Ran*.

## From study to practice

This is a study in the painter's sense: a full working-out of an image before the final version. It plans decisions about composition and color.

It also shades into practice (interpretation). Kurosawa kept painting through years of waiting for money, first for *Kagemusha*, then for *Ran*, then for later films. Painting the same imagined film again and again seems to have kept his visual idea alive and specific. The plan became a habit.
