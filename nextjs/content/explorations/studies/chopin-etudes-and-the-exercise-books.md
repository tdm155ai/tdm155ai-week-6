# From Czerny and Hanon to Chopin's Études: exercise raised to art

**Wall label**

*Étude* is French for "study." In the 1800s, piano students worked through books of them. Some, like Charles-Louis Hanon's *The Virtuoso Pianist* (1873), are pure exercise: one finger pattern, moved up the keyboard step by step, then back down. No one plays them in concert. Carl Czerny's studies sit in the middle: short pieces built around one skill. Then there are Frédéric Chopin's 24 Études, published in 1833 and 1837. Each one also drills a single problem, such as stretches, weak fingers, or black keys. But each is also a full piece of music that pianists play on stage. Same format, different ambition.

## What it is

Three points along one line, from drill to art:

- **Hanon, *Le Pianiste virtuose* (The Virtuoso Pianist), 60 exercises, Boulogne, 1873.** Each early exercise is a figure of eight sixteenth notes starting on C, then repeated from D, from E, and so on up two octaves, then back down. The three parts aim at finger strength and independence, then speed and agility, then advanced control. Hanon recommends playing through them daily once mastered, to keep the technique (Wikipedia, Virtuoso Pianist).
- **Czerny, *Die Schule der Geläufigkeit* (The School of Velocity), Op. 299, 1833.** The original subtitle is "40 Übungsstücke um die Schnelligkeit der Finger zu entwickeln": 40 practice pieces to develop the speed of the fingers. These are short pieces with real harmony and form, but their purpose is stated in the title (IMSLP). Czerny studied with Beethoven and taught Liszt (Wikipedia, Czerny).
- **Chopin, 12 Études, Op. 10 (composed 1829–1832, published 1833, dedicated to Franz Liszt) and 12 Études, Op. 25 (published 1837).** They were, as the standard account puts it, "the first to become a regular part of the concert repertoire" (Wikipedia, Études). Liszt in turn later dedicated his own *Transcendental Études* to Czerny (Wikipedia, Czerny). The three are linked by teaching and by dedication.

## What the exercise was for

Each Chopin étude isolates one technical problem, much as a drill does:

- **Op. 10 No. 1 in C major**: wide right-hand arpeggios that stretch and contract the hand, often a tenth across three notes.
- **Op. 10 No. 2 in A minor**: a chromatic scale in the weak outer fingers (3, 4, 5) of the right hand, while the thumb and index finger play short chords in the same hand.
- **Op. 10 No. 5 in G-flat major ("Black Keys")**: the right hand plays only black keys, with a single exception, an F natural in bar 66.

But the problem is turned into the piece's character. The arpeggios of No. 1 are the melody's surroundings, not a warm-up. The strain in No. 2 is part of what you hear: a nervous, whispering line. Chopin himself was sharp about this difference. When he heard that Clara Wieck had played the "Black Keys" étude, he wrote to his friend Julian Fontana in 1839: "How could she have chosen precisely this Étude, the least interesting for those who do not know that it is intended for the black keys" (as quoted in Wikipedia, Op. 10 No. 5). Read that way, Chopin himself held that an étude should be more than its exercise.

## Listen closely

Scores: [Chopin Études Op. 10 on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/%C3%89tudes,_Op.10_(Chopin,_Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric)); [Czerny Op. 299 on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/The_School_of_Velocity,_Op.299_(Czerny,_Carl)); [Hanon on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/The_Virtuoso_Pianist_(Hanon,_Charles-Louis)).

- **Hanon, Exercise 1.** Listen for 30 seconds. Count how many times the same eight-note figure repeats at a new pitch. There is no melody, no change of character, no ending other than "back home."
- **Czerny, Op. 299 No. 1.** Notice that there *are* phrases, a harmonic plan and an ending, but the running notes never stop. Ask: is this a piece, or an exercise dressed as one?
- **Chopin, Op. 10 No. 1.** Listen for the slow bass octaves in the left hand under the right-hand sweeps. That melody in the bass is what makes the drill a piece.
- **Chopin, Op. 10 No. 2.** In the score, note that Chopin wrote out the fingering for the chromatic runs in great detail. Look at bars 1–2: the 3-4-5 fingers carry the scale.
- **Chopin, Op. 10 No. 5, bar 66.** Find the one white key in the right hand.

## From study to practice

This case shows the whole range of the word "study" in one room. Hanon is practice with the music removed: pure repetition, aimed at the body. Czerny is practice wrapped in small, functional pieces. Chopin keeps the drill's single focus but makes it carry expression. A pianist practices a Chopin étude for the technique, and also performs it as art. For students in a making course, the question is useful: when you design an exercise for yourself, is it Hanon (a pure rep), Czerny (a small functional piece), or Chopin (a constraint that becomes the work)?
