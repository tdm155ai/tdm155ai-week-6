# Constable's full-scale study for The Hay Wain

**Wall label**

Most sketches are small. This one is about 1.4 by 1.9 metres, the same size as the painting it prepares. Constable painted it fast, in the studio, with much of the brown canvas left showing. It holds the whole of *The Hay Wain*: the cottage, the trees, the cart in the river, the sky. It also holds a rider on a horse in the foreground. Constable kept that rider in the finished painting until late, then painted him out. Making a sketch this big cost canvas and days. What it bought was a full-size test of the picture before the real one.

## What it is

An oil on canvas, about 137 × 188 cm, in the Victoria and Albert Museum (987-1900). The V&A calls it the *Full-Scale Study for The Hay Wain* and dates it about 1821. It was lent to the South Kensington Museum (now the V&A) by the collector Henry Vaughan in 1862 and came by his bequest in 1900.

The finished painting was shown at the Royal Academy in 1821 as *Landscape: Noon*. It is now in the National Gallery, London (NG1207), oil on canvas, 130.2 × 185.4 cm.

Constable's large exhibition canvases are often called the "six-footers." For several of them he made a separate sketch at the same size. His friend and biographer C. R. Leslie wrote in 1855 that "Constable made a sketch of the full size of every large picture he painted." The scholar Charles Rhyne notes that Leslie overstated this, and that scholars still debate which large canvases count as full-size sketches.

## What the study was for

According to the V&A, Constable "used a broad painting style to establish the general balance of the composition and its colours." The National Gallery adds that he first made a small oil sketch of the hay wagon (Yale Center for British Art), then "a full-scale oil sketch to develop the composition," which he "painted quickly with large areas of the brown ground of the canvas left bare."

Rhyne gives a likely origin for the habit. X-rays of Constable's earlier full-size sketch for *The White Horse* (c. 1818) show an abandoned landscape underneath. His reading: Constable's first try at a canvas this large failed, so before the next one "he decided to work out the problems first in a large sketch." Rhyne also cautions that Constable never mentions full-size sketches in his surviving letters, so their purpose is partly inference.

I read the full-size study as a way to try the composition at the scale where its problems actually show up. A small sketch can hide a weak middle. A six-foot one cannot.

## Look closely

- Large patches of warm brown canvas show through, especially in the river and the foreground. The ground colour does a lot of the work.
- The trees and sky are laid in with broad, broken strokes. Leaves are not drawn; masses are.
- Foreground left of centre: a rider on a horse at the water's edge, with a dog nearby. The National Gallery says the horse and rider were kept in the final painting but painted out at a late stage.
- The hay wain itself sits in the river at right of centre, the same place it sits in the finished picture.
- Compare the edges with the National Gallery painting. The study is slightly larger in both directions.

## From study to practice

The full-scale study is a one-off. It is a single big rehearsal, not a repeated exercise. But Constable's practice around it was full of repetition. He painted the same stretch of the Stour valley for years, and the V&A holds many of his small outdoor oil sketches and sky studies. The National Gallery says *The Hay Wain* was painted in the London studio from "open-air sketches made over several years."

Constable valued that store of studies. The V&A quotes him saying he had "no objection to part with the corn, but not with the field that grew it." I read this as his own account of the difference between the study and the product. The finished painting is the corn. The repeated studies are the field.
