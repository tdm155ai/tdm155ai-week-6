# Feuillet's Chorégraphie: a dance written as a floor plan

**Wall label**

In 1700 a Paris dancing master, Raoul-Auger Feuillet, published a book that taught you how to write a dance on paper. The page is the dance floor. A line traces the dancer's path across the room. Small signs along the line mark each step: a sink, a rise, a spring, a turn. The music runs across the top, and short bar lines cut across the path, so you can see which steps fall in which bar. The method was devised at Louis XIV's court by Pierre Beauchamp. Feuillet published it. More than 300 dances survive in this notation. A dancing master in another city could rebuild a dance from the page alone.

## What it is

*Chorégraphie, ou l'art de décrire la dance par caracteres, figures, et signes démonstratifs* was published in Paris in 1700 [1][2]. It sets out the rules for reading and writing the notation, with charts of steps and their variations [2][4]. Feuillet also began publishing complete dances in the notation [3]. John Weaver translated the book into English as *Orchesography* in 1706 [3].

The system is now called Beauchamp–Feuillet notation. Pierre Beauchamp devised it in the 1680s, on commission from Louis XIV [3]. In 1704 Beauchamp took Feuillet to the king's council, claiming the invention was his. According to Marie Glon, the council recognised Beauchamp as "auteur et inventeur" of the method but let Feuillet keep his privilege to publish [4].

How a page reads [5]:

- The page stands for the room. It is held with the music side toward the "presence" (the front of the room), even when the dancer turns.
- A line, the *tract*, is the dancer's path across the floor.
- Step signs sit on either side of the tract, one for each foot.
- Small lines across the tract match the bar lines of the music. Everything between two of them happens in one bar.
- Signs for the arms existed but were rarely used. Signs for taking and letting go of hands were common.

## What it was for

The notation solves one problem: how do you send a dance somewhere without sending a dancer? Before it, dances moved from person to person. Once a dance was on paper, a master in London or Madrid could read it and teach it [3].

It was also sold as a way to learn alone. The full title promises that you can learn dances "de soy-même" (by yourself). Glon notes that the *Mercure galant* praised the book as letting young women learn without a dancing master [4].

Mostly, these pages record finished dances. But the form is also a plan of the room. Each page answers the questions a choreographer settles in blocking: where the dancer goes, how far, and in which bar. (That reading is our interpretation; the sources present the notation as a record and a teaching tool.)

## Look closely

- **The music on top.** Two staves of the Folie d'Espagne run across the head of the page. The dance below is aligned to them.
- **The path.** The tract is a tall rectangle. The dancer goes down one side of the room and comes back up the other.
- **Signs on both sides.** The hooked marks along the path are steps, left foot on one side, right foot on the other. Ticks across the path are the bar lines.
- **Arms and castanets.** The note at upper right says this couplet shows "the arms and the beating of the castanets." The book says arms were seldom notated, so this page is a demonstration [5].
- **The edition.** Commons labels this scan as the 1700 edition, from a private collection. We could not check the page against a library copy. The Library of Congress has digitised editions of the book [1].

## From study to practice

This sits on the planning side, but at its far end. It is not a sketch for one dance. It is a system for writing any dance down, so that it can be studied, taught, and rebuilt.

It also points toward practice. A reader learns the dance by working through the page bar by bar, then dancing it until the page is no longer needed. The notation stores the plan. Practice moves it into the body.
