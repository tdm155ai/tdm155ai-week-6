# Picasso's studies for Guernica, and Dora Maar's photographs of its states

**Wall label**

On 1 May 1937, days after the bombing of Guernica, Picasso started drawing. That first day he made four composition sketches and two studies of a horse. Over about five weeks he made roughly fifty drawings and studies: the bull, the horse, a weeping woman, a mother with a dead child. The canvas itself was nearly eight metres wide. From 11 May to 4 June, the photographer Dora Maar recorded it as it changed. Her photographs show figures moved, removed, and redrawn on the canvas itself. Here the sketching did not stop when the big picture began. It moved onto it.

*The photo shows the plaque at Picasso's studio, where Guernica was painted, not the work. Picasso's and Dora Maar's works are still in copyright. The Museo Reina Sofía's research site shows the studies and photographs; see Sources.*

## What it is

Two kinds of preparatory record for *Guernica* (1937), which Picasso painted for the Spanish Republic's pavilion at the 1937 Paris World's Fair. The painting is in the Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofía, Madrid.

1. **Picasso's studies.** The Reina Sofía's research site says that "in barely a month and a half, Picasso produced fifty or so drawings and sketches." Many are in the museum through the Picasso Bequest of 1981. The earliest are dated in Picasso's hand. *Composition Study [I]* is dated Paris, 1 May 1937 (museum register DE00053).
2. **Dora Maar's photographs.** Maar photographed the canvas in Picasso's studio at rue des Grands-Augustins, Paris, between 11 May and 4 June 1937. The Reina Sofía holds 28 of these photographs. Its site groups them into stages, labelled I to VII. The museum says Christian Zervos, editor of *Cahiers d'art*, asked Maar to document the painting's evolution.

## What the sketch/study was for

The studies let Picasso try each figure separately before placing it. W. J. H. B. Sandberg, writing in *Dædalus* in 1960, gives the sequence: "On 1 May, Picasso begins with four sketches for the composition and two studies for the horse." He then quotes Picasso, in a remark to the dealer Kahnweiler: "In the beginning one should have an idea, but a vague one." According to Sandberg, "On 11 May the canvas is ready, and immediately the composition is laid down as a linear structure," and "work on the mural is accompanied by more than thirty studies for the details."

The photographs show what happened next. The Reina Sofía says that in the early stages "one can already see the principal figures": the mother with the dead child, the bull, the horse, the fallen soldier, the figure holding a light, and a figure with arms raised. Across the stages Picasso was "progressively correcting the poses of the figures and eliminating certain elements in order to give the composition greater clarity." Sandberg notes one dated change: "On 20 May the horse lifts its head."

I read this case as a counterexample to a clean plan-then-execute model. Picasso did plan on paper. But the canvas also acted as a sketch for a month. The photographs are a record of revision at full size.

## Look closely

Use the Reina Sofía's online documents for these (links in Sources).

- *Composition Study [I]*, dated 1 May 1937. Compare how many figures it has with the final painting. Which ones are already there?
- The horse studies. Look for how the horse's head and neck change between drawings, and then in the canvas stages.
- Dora Maar's Stage I photograph. The figures are outlines only. The canvas is mostly bare.
- The later stages. Look for the figure with raised arms and for what happens to it. Look for where the lamp and the light-bearer settle.
- Compare a late stage with the finished painting. Note the dark and light shapes: what got filled in, and what was left white?

## From study to practice

This is not repetition in the drill sense. Picasso drew the horse many times, but each drawing was looking for something new. It is closer to rapid iteration: same figure, many variations, choose one.

What students can take from it is the record-keeping. Picasso dated his studies, and Maar's photographs fixed each stage before it was painted over. That made the process visible, to him and to us. The museum quotes Picasso's wish to use the camera to fix "not the stages of a painting, but its metamorphosis." I read the photographs as the documentation step that turns iteration into something you can learn from.
