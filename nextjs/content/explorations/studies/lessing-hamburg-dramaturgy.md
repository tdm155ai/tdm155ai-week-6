# Lessing's Hamburg Dramaturgy

**Wall label**

In April 1767 a group of backers opened a "national theatre" in Hamburg. They hired the writer Gotthold Ephraim Lessing. He was not to write plays, and not to act. His job was to watch and write. Twice a week he would publish a short paper on what the company staged: the plays, the acting, the choices. He called the paper a *Dramaturgie*. "I am neither actor nor poet," he wrote. He soon gave up judging the actors. Pirated reprints undercut the paper. The theatre failed by 1769. The job title survived. Every dramaturg since is in some sense his descendant.

## What it is

A serial publication of 104 numbered "pieces" (*Stücke*) written by Lessing for the Hamburg National Theatre. An announcement is dated 22 April 1767, the day the theatre opened. The first piece is dated 1 May 1767 [1]. Lessing planned two pieces a week for a year [1]. The last four numbers carry the date 19 April 1768, but Lessing admits in them that it took "two years, and with difficulty," to finish what was promised for one [1]. The collected work is usually dated 1767–69 [2][3].

The theatre itself was led by Abel Seyler, with Konrad Ekhof as its leading actor. It premiered Lessing's own *Minna von Barnhelm* on 30 September 1767 and closed in 1769 when the money ran out [3].

## What it was for

The announcement states the plan in one sentence: "This Dramaturgy shall keep a critical register of all the plays to be performed, and accompany every step that the art of both the poet and the actor will take here" [1] (our translation).

The problem it set out to solve: when an audience likes or dislikes a night at the theatre, who gets the credit or the blame? Lessing wrote that the critic's finest skill is to tell, in every case, "what, and how much of it, should be put to the account of the poet, or of the actor" [1] (our translation). Blaming one for the other's fault, he said, "spoils both."

That is a research and planning question, not a performance. The dramaturg stands beside the making and keeps the record.

## Look closely

- **The job description.** In the closing numbers Lessing explains why he was hired: "I am neither actor nor poet." He says what he can do is criticism, slowly. "My first thoughts are certainly not a hair better than anyone's first thoughts" [1] (our translation).
- **He nearly called it something else.** He planned to name the paper *Hamburgische Didaskalien*, after the ancient Greek production records of plays. He chose *Dramaturgie* because it left him free to decide what to include [1].
- **He dropped half the plan.** "The latter half I soon grew tired of": the critique of actors. His reason is worth reading in a practice week: "We have actors, but no art of acting." Actors had only "a fortunate routine," and no shared, precise rules by which to judge a performance, so every criticism felt like an insult [1] (our translation). Many accounts say actors in the company took offence; the English Wikipedia article reports this without detail [2]. We could not confirm from an open source exactly which number marks the change.
- **Acting is transitory.** The announcement asks for special fairness to actors because the poet's work "remains" and can be read again, while "the art of the actor is transitory in its works" [1]. That is an early statement of why theatre needs records at all.
- **Piracy.** Lessing blames pirated reprints for the delays and for the paper's end. The theatre had paid for printing and hoped to recover the cost from sales [1].

## From study to practice

This sits at the research end of planning. It does not plan one production. It builds a habit of asking, play after play, what the text asks for and what the staging delivered. That is the work of a production dramaturg today (interpretation, but a common one: the English Wikipedia article calls Lessing the first dramaturg [3]).

It also touches practice, in a negative way. Lessing could not judge acting because acting had no agreed technique to measure against, only "routine." Much of the twentieth century (Stanislavski, Meyerhold, Dalcroze, and others in this pack) can be read as the attempt to build exactly what he said was missing: a teachable, repeatable art of acting.
