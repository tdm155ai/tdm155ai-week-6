# Leonardo's perspective study for the Adoration of the Magi

**Wall label**

In 1481 monks near Florence asked Leonardo for an altarpiece about 2.4 metres square. Before he touched the panel, he worked out its back half on a sheet of paper about 16 by 29 centimetres. He ruled a floor grid with a straightedge and stylus. He set ruined arches and a double staircase on it. Then he let horses and riders loose across the grid. The sheet is a rehearsal for space: where things stand, how far away they are, how big they must be. Leonardo left for Milan in 1482. The painting was never finished. The study is finished in its own way.

## What it is

A preparatory drawing for the background of Leonardo's *Adoration of the Magi*. It is in the prints and drawings department of the Uffizi, Florence (GDSU, inv. 436 E). Pen and brown ink, white highlights, and traces of metalpoint over stylus and compass marks, on light brown prepared paper. Roughly 16 × 29 cm.

The commission came in 1481 from the Augustinian monks of San Donato a Scopeto, outside Florence. The panel Leonardo started is about 244 × 240 cm and is also in the Uffizi. He left it unfinished when he moved to Milan. The monks later gave the commission to Filippino Lippi, who completed his own *Adoration* in 1496.

Another, looser compositional drawing for the same picture is in the Louvre. Scholars read the Louvre sheet as an early stage and the Uffizi sheet as a later, more calculated one.

## What the study was for

It let Leonardo solve the space before committing to a panel taller than a person. A 2021 study by Apollonio, Foschi, and Gaiani notes that the Louvre sheet is "in the process of radical reworking," while the Uffizi sheet was "scrupulously finished and calculated." Several motifs moved between the two. The double staircase and the fighting horsemen were mirrored to the other side.

The Library of Congress, which showed the drawing in 2006, describes the order of work: he "drew the ground first, then a plan of the buildings, and finally animated the scene with human figures and animals." The painting still did not copy the study exactly. According to the same exhibition text, Leonardo moved the viewpoint in the painting and compressed the background space, and the temple ruins on the right of the drawing are missing from the panel.

I read this as a cheap place to make expensive decisions. A ruled sheet can be redrawn in an afternoon. A gessoed altarpiece cannot.

## Look closely

- The floor is a fan of straight lines running back to a point, crossed by horizontal lines that get closer together as they recede. This is the grid everything else stands on.
- On the left: round arches and a double flight of stairs, drawn with ruled edges. The architecture is the most "finished" part of the sheet.
- In the middle distance, horses rear and riders twist. These are drawn fast and loose, over the ruled lines, not under them.
- Faint vertical lines rise from the floor at regular intervals, like the posts of an unbuilt roof. Some figures are only a few strokes.
- The figures in the foreground of the final picture (the Virgin, the kneeling Magi) are absent. This sheet is only the back of the stage.

## From study to practice

This drawing is a study, not a drill. It is one problem solved once. But Leonardo's own writing makes perspective a thing learned by repetition before it is used. In a passage from his notes on painting he says: "The youth should first learn perspective, then the proportions of objects. Then he may copy from some good master, to accustom himself to fine forms. Then from nature, to confirm by practice the rules he has learnt." (Richter, §483)

So the study sits at the end of a long practice. The ruled grid is a procedure Leonardo had done many times. What is new on this sheet is the particular scene laid over it. I read the contrast as useful for students: the grid is the practised part, the horses are the sketch.
