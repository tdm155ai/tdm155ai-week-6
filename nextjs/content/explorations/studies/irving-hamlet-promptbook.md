# A promptbook for Henry Irving's Hamlet

**Wall label**

A promptbook is the book that runs the show. This one, now at Harvard, belongs to Henry Irving's *Hamlet*. Someone cut up a printed copy of the play and pasted each page onto blank ruled paper. That left wide margins to write in. On the left page is a drawing: two trees, the Ghost between them, and rays of green light from the flies. On the right are the lines, cut and marked. "Seizes his L. arm." "Ghost music, till he is in situation next scene." "When Mr Irving across stage, Change." Every note is a decision someone made in rehearsal and had to repeat each night.

## What it is

A marked-up copy of the play used to run performances. Harvard's catalogue lists the volume at shelfmark TS Promptbook Sh154.75 as an 1865 school edition of *Hamlet* (edited by the Rev. John Hunter, London: Longman), with Henry Irving and J. H. Allen named as former owners [1]. Houghton Library's own description on Wikimedia Commons calls it the promptbook "from an 1874 staging of *Hamlet*" by Irving [2].

Irving (1838–1905) first played Hamlet at the Lyceum Theatre in London on 31 October 1874, under H. L. Bateman's management. The *Dictionary of National Biography* says it "was cheaply mounted," that the audience at first failed "to see the drift of a very quiet and original performance," and that it "ran for 200 nights" [3].

## What it was for

A promptbook holds the decisions that make a production repeatable. The plan in a director's notebook becomes, here, a set of cues a stage manager or prompter can call the same way every night: when the light changes, when music starts and stops, where an actor crosses, when the scene shifts.

## Look closely

- **Light as a plan.** "Green Lime Light from R Flies, to remain till Ghost Exit." Limelight was a bright stage light (Houghton's description glosses it as "burning calcium oxide" [2]). Here it is coloured green and aimed from above right. Below the drawing: "Gauzed tree."
- **Third person.** "When Mr Irving across stage Change." The marker writes about Irving, not as Irving. That suggests a stage manager's or prompter's hand (our inference; the catalogue does not name the annotator).
- **Cuts.** A line is struck through in Horatio's speech ("Which might deprive your sovereignty of reason"). Irving's acting text was shorter than Shakespeare's.
- **Moves written as abbreviations.** "R. Ghost xes to behind tree up L.C." most likely means the Ghost, from the right, crosses to behind the tree, up left of centre ("xes" for crosses, "L.C." for left centre; our reading of the hand).
- **A numbered cue.** A circled "9" marks a music cue ("Ghost music"), closed further down by "Music stops." Numbered cues let a crew run the same sequence every night.

## From study to practice

A promptbook sits exactly between plan and practice. It begins as a plan: someone decides the light, the cuts, the moves. Through rehearsal it is corrected until it matches what the company actually does. Then it becomes the score for repetition, run night after night (if Houghton's date is right, for some two hundred nights). It is also, after the fact, the best record we have of a performance that otherwise vanished.

Harvard's Theatre Collection, at Houghton Library, holds many such books, including later Irving *Hamlet* promptbooks [1].
