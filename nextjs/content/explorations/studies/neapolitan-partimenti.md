# Neapolitan partimenti: learning to compose from a bass line

**Wall label**

Naples in the 1700s had four music conservatories. They began as charities for orphans and poor boys. They became the most successful music schools in Europe. Their main tool was the *partimento*: a single bass line on one staff. The student sat at the harpsichord, played the bass with the left hand, and made up the rest with the right. Small numbers above the notes hinted at the chords at first. Later the numbers were taken away. After years of this, a student could hear a whole piece in a bass line. The exercise was the composing.

## What it is

- A **partimento** (plural *partimenti*) is a bass line, sometimes with figures, written as a guide for improvising or composing a complete piece at the keyboard. Giorgio Sanguinetti calls it "a thread that contains in itself all, or most, of the information needed for a complete composition" (quoted in Wikipedia, Partimento).
- **The institutions.** Naples had four conservatories: Santa Maria di Loreto, Sant'Onofrio a Porta Capuana, the Pietà dei Turchini, and the Poveri di Gesù Cristo. They were founded in the 1500s as religious charities for orphans and foundlings. By the 1700s they also took paying students. Boys could train there for up to ten years (Gjerdingen 2020; Università di Teramo, Digital History).
- **The teachers.** Important partimento masters include Francesco Durante, Leonardo Leo, and Fedele Fenaroli (1730–1818). Fenaroli studied with Durante at Santa Maria di Loreto. His pupils included Domenico Cimarosa and Niccolò Zingarelli. His *Regole musicali per i principianti di cembalo* (Rules for beginning harpsichordists) was printed in Naples in 1775 and stayed in print for more than a century (IMSLP; Wikipedia, Fenaroli).
- **The sequence.** Robert Gjerdingen, who edits the online *Monuments of Partimenti*, describes a typical collection: a few pages of rules (*regole*), then figured basses, then many unfigured basses, then advanced fugal partimenti. "The figures were like the training wheels on a child's bicycle; once the apprentice achieved a measure of competence, the figures were removed" (Gjerdingen, "History of Partimenti").
- Fenaroli's partimenti, as edited online, run from Book 1 ("simple figured basses") to Book 6 ("advanced fugues and canons").

## What the drill was for

Partimenti trained fluency, not knowledge *about* music. Gjerdingen puts it this way: "It was not enough to learn 'about' music. The child needed to become fluent in the courtly style." A student who had played through hundreds of basses would recognize stock patterns from one part alone and complete them without thinking. Gjerdingen's 2007 book *Music in the Galant Style* names many of these patterns, or "schemata": the Romanesca, the Prinner, the Fonte, the Monte, and others.

One core pattern is the **rule of the octave**. It gives one standard chord for each step of the scale in the bass. In its simplest form, the stable steps (1 and 5) get a plain triad; the other steps get a chord with a sixth. Going up, steps 4 and 7 get an added dissonance that pulls toward the next stable note (Gjerdingen, "Illustrated Guide to the Rule of the Octave"). Students played it in every key until it was automatic.

## Look closely

- **The image: Fenaroli, Book 1, No. 1.** It is a bass line in G major. The numbers above the notes (6, ♯6, 6/5) tell the player which intervals to add above the bass. Find the 6/5 just before a few of the cadences: that is the "pull" toward a stable note described above.
- **Where the bass rises step by step**, check the figures against the rule of the octave. Do steps 1 and 5 have no figure (meaning a plain triad)? Do the steps in between have a 6?
- **The last bar** has stacked figures (8, 5, 3) on a long G. That spells out the home chord. The piece ends where it began.
- **Full books:** Fenaroli Book 1 as a free PDF from [Monuments of Partimenti](https://partimenti.org/partimenti/collections/fenaroli/index.html). Gjerdingen's [illustrated guide to the rule of the octave](https://partimenti.org/partimenti/about_parti/rule_of_the_octave.pdf) is four pages and readable without much theory.

## From study to practice

Partimenti sit at the practice end of this pack. They are drills: short, many, repeated, and graded. But they were never only finger work. Each one is the skeleton of a possible piece. A student who realizes the same bass ten times, ten ways, is both drilling and composing. Gjerdingen describes the result as being able to "speak" the style. That is what internalization looks like: a pattern played so many times that it becomes something you think *with*, not something you think *about*. This is an interpretation, but the conservatory records he and Sanguinetti draw on support it.
