# Bach's Art of Fugue: one subject, studied to exhaustion

**Wall label**

Take one short tune, twelve notes in D minor. Now write fourteen fugues and four canons on it. Play it upside down. Play it twice as slow, or twice as fast. Combine it with itself, entering before the last entry has finished. Write whole fugues that can be flipped like a mirror and still work. This is *The Art of Fugue*, Bach's last large project. It was printed in 1751, the year after he died. The last fugue breaks off in the middle. It is a study in the oldest sense: a systematic investigation of one idea. It is also music people still play and record.

## What it is

- A cycle of 14 fugues (Bach called them *Contrapunctus*) and 4 canons, all in D minor and all based on variants of one main subject (Wikipedia).
- Bach's autograph score (Berlin, Mus. ms. Bach P 200) dates from around 1740, perhaps earlier. He kept working on it from about 1747 onward. The first printed edition appeared in 1751, after his death in July 1750 (Wikipedia).
- It is written in open score, one staff per voice, with no instrument named. Most scholars now accept keyboard performance, but it is also often played by organists and by ensembles (Wikipedia).
- Christoph Wolff describes the governing idea as "an exploration in depth of the contrapuntal possibilities inherent in a single musical subject" (quoted in Wikipedia).

## What the study was for

Interpretation, with evidence: this is not a teaching book in the way the Inventions are. There is no title page addressed to students. But it works like a catalogue of technique. Each Contrapunctus shows one more thing you can do with the same subject:

- the subject in its original form and upside down (inversion);
- faster and slower versions (diminution and augmentation);
- *stretto*, where voices enter with the subject before the previous entry is finished;
- double and triple fugues, which combine the main subject with new ones;
- mirror fugues, which can be inverted in every voice and still follow the rules.

The study is of the *material*, not of a performer's hands. It asks: how much can one idea hold?

## Listen closely

Scores, including a facsimile of the 1751 print and a modern edition of the autograph: [Die Kunst der Fuge on IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Die_Kunst_der_Fuge,_BWV_1080_(Bach,_Johann_Sebastian)).

- **Contrapunctus 1, opening.** Learn the subject by ear first: it outlines the D minor triad (D–A–F–D), turns on C-sharp, climbs to G, and steps back down to D. Twelve notes. Sing it before you listen further.
- **Contrapunctus 1 vs. Contrapunctus 3.** In Contrapunctus 3 the subject enters upside down. Can you still recognize it?
- **Contrapunctus 6** ("in Stylo Francese") uses a dotted, French-overture version of the subject, including in diminution (halved note values). Listen for one voice that seems "in a hurry" against another.
- **Contrapunctus 7** ("per Augmentationem et Diminutionem") states the subject slower and faster at once. Listen for the very slow (augmented) entries: they stand out like landmarks.
- **Contrapunctus 14, bar 193.** A new subject begins on B-flat, A, C, B-natural. In German note names these spell **B–A–C–H**, Bach's name. All three subjects of the fugue are combined at bar 234. The manuscript stops at bar 239.
- **The note at the end.** Bach's son Carl Philipp Emanuel wrote on the manuscript: "At the point where the composer introduces the name BACH in the countersubject to this fugue, the composer died." Modern scholars dispute this on the basis of the manuscript evidence, which suggests the fugue was written before his final illness (Wikipedia). Treat it as family story, not fact.

## From study to practice

The Art of Fugue is a study in the scientific sense: a set of experiments on one specimen. But it also shows what systematic practice produces. You only get mirror fugues after decades of writing ordinary fugues. In 1880, Gustav Nottebohm showed that the three subjects of the unfinished fugue fit together with the main Art of Fugue subject. Many people infer that Bach was planning a fourfold combination as the finale. Musicians have been "practicing" on this incomplete study ever since, by writing completions of the last fugue. The study became a prompt for others' practice.
