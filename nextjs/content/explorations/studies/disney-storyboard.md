# The Disney storyboard

**Wall label**

Before anyone animated a frame, Disney's story artists drew the cartoon as a row of rough sketches and pinned them to a board. Studio lore credits Webb Smith with the idea in the early 1930s: one drawing per sheet, pinned in order, so the whole story hung on the wall at once. A board could be read in a minute. A sheet could be moved, swapped, or torn down. Story meetings could happen in front of the boards. The early *Three Little Pigs* (1933) is often called the first fully storyboarded short. Who had the idea first is disputed.

## What it is

A storyboard is a sequence of drawings, one per shot or story beat, pinned or mounted in order. Disney's own reference entry describes "4×8-foot boards" with "story sketches pinned up on them in order," and says the artists "found it much easier to visualize a story this way than to read a script" [1].

The key move is physical: separate sheets on a corkboard, not drawings bound in a book. That makes the order of the story something you can change by hand.

## What it was for

The board resolved questions of **sequence and timing** before the expensive stage. Animation in the 1930s meant thousands of drawings per short. Changing the order of events after animation had started was costly. On the board, it cost a pushpin.

It also made the story a shared object. A script is read by one person at a time. A wall of sketches can be read by a room of people standing together, which suited Disney's story meetings.

## Look closely

- **Who invented it is contested.** Christopher Finch, in *The Art of Walt Disney*, reports that Disney credited Webb Smith with drawing scenes on separate sheets and pinning them to a board [2]. Pete Docter and Don Peri report that animator Dick Huemer credited Ted Sears, who had tried the idea earlier at the Fleischer studio, where no one was interested [2]. Earlier filmmakers, including Georges Méliès, also drew shot plans [2]. Treat "Webb Smith invented the storyboard" as studio tradition, not settled fact.
- **The format was physical.** Disney's D23 reference gives the board size as 4×8 feet [1]. Pinned sheets, not a bound book, are what made reordering easy.
- **Three Little Pigs as the early full case.** Diane Disney Miller's 1956 biography of her father is cited for the claim that the first complete storyboards were made for *Three Little Pigs* [2]. Story work on that short was under way by mid-December 1932, with Webb Smith, Ted Sears, Pinto Colvig and Albert Hurter as the main story artists; animation began by mid-February 1933; it premiered at Radio City Music Hall on 27 May 1933 [3].
- **Gag meetings came first.** Devon Baxter's production history describes gag meetings and a three-page outline before animation [3]. The board sat between talk and drawing.

## From study to practice

This is a pure planning case. The board is a wireframe of a film: it tests order, pacing, and gags while they are still cheap to change. It is not practice in the sense of repetition for skill.

One point of contact (interpretation): story artists who drew boards every week for years built a fast, shared shorthand. The board was a planning tool for each film, but making boards over and over was also how a story artist got good.
