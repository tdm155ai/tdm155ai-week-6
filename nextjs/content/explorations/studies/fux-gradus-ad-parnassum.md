# Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum and species counterpoint

**Wall label**

*Gradus ad Parnassum* means "steps to Parnassus," the mountain of the Muses. Johann Joseph Fux, music director at the imperial court in Vienna, published it in Latin in 1725. It is written as a conversation between a teacher and a student. The student writes a line of music against a given melody. The teacher marks the mistakes. They do it again, a little harder each time. There are five "species," or steps. The young Haydn taught himself from this book. Mozart taught from it. Beethoven's teachers used it. Composition students still do these exercises 300 years later.

## What it is

- A treatise in Latin, printed in Vienna in 1725 at imperial expense, and dedicated to Emperor Charles VI. Lorenz Christoph Mizler translated it into German in 1742 (Mann 1965; Wikipedia, Fux).
- It is a dialogue. Fux explains in his foreword: "By Aloysius, the master, I refer to Palestrina... By Josephus I mean the pupil who wishes to learn the art of composition" (Mann 1965, foreword).
- Fux says he gave "very little space in this book to theory and much more to practice" (Mann 1965, foreword).
- The core is **species counterpoint**. The student writes a new line (the counterpoint) above or below a given slow melody (the *cantus firmus*). The five species are:
  1. note against note;
  2. two notes against one;
  3. four notes against one;
  4. syncopation, where the new line is tied across the beat (suspensions);
  5. florid counterpoint, which mixes the other four.
- The standard English translation of the counterpoint part is Alfred Mann, *The Study of Counterpoint* (W. W. Norton, 1943; revised 1965).

## What the exercise was for

Species counterpoint takes the hardest problem in composing (many voices moving at once, each one singable, all sounding good together) and breaks it into small, separate steps. In first species, you only think about which intervals are allowed and how the voices move. Rhythm is taken away on purpose. Each new species adds back one kind of difficulty: passing notes, then faster motion, then dissonance on the beat.

The method depends on correction. In the first lesson, the student asks, "Is this all that is needed for this first species of counterpoint?" Aloysius answers: "It is not all, but it is enough to begin with; the rest will become clear by the correction" (Mann 1965, ch. 1). A little later, one of the student's attempts is marked wrong because it starts outside the mode. The teacher says: "Don't worry, my son."

Who used it, according to Mann's introduction:

- **Haydn**, as a poor young musician, worked through it on his own. His early biographer Griesinger reports that Haydn wrote out the exercises, put them aside for a few weeks, and then revised them "until he was satisfied he had done everything exactly right." Haydn later wrote his own condensed version for teaching, the *Elementarbuch*.
- **Mozart** used it as a teacher. The composition studies of his English pupil Thomas Attwood (1785–1787) contain Mozart's corrections and his adaptations of Fux's models. Leopold Mozart's own copy, inscribed 1746, survives in Salzburg.
- **Beethoven** studied with Haydn, then with Johann Schenk and Johann Georg Albrechtsberger. All three based the course on the *Gradus*, and Beethoven wrote out a summary of it.

## Look closely

- **The title page** (image). Note the subtitle: *Manuductio ad compositionem musicae regularem*, "a leading-by-the-hand to regular musical composition." The book presents itself as a guide, not a reference.
- **The first cantus firmus.** Fux's first exercise melody is in the Dorian mode on D: **D F E D G F A G F E D**, eleven whole notes (Mann 1965, Fig. 1). Sing it slowly. Notice that it moves mostly by step, has one high point (A), and ends by stepping down to D.
- **The first mistake.** When Josephus writes a counterpoint *below* the D melody, he starts a fifth below, on G. Aloysius marks the first two bars wrong, because the counterpoint must stay in the same mode as the cantus firmus. Josephus says he is "very ashamed." The teacher replies that it was not his fault: he had not yet been told that rule (Mann 1965, ch. 1).
- **Read it yourself.** Mann's translation is in print (Norton) and in most libraries. The 1725 Latin original is on [IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/Gradus_ad_Parnassum_(Fux,_Johann_Joseph)). *Open Music Theory* has a modern set of the exercises: [Gradus ad Parnassum Exercises](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/gradus-ad-parnassum-exercises/).

## From study to practice

This is practice in the strict sense: a narrow, repeatable task, done many times with feedback, in graded steps. No one performs a first-species exercise as a piece. But it is not mindless drill. Each exercise is a tiny composition, with choices and a right-or-wrong check. Haydn's method, as Griesinger describes it, is worth noting: do the exercise, put it away, come back weeks later with fresh eyes, revise. That is closer to a sketch-and-revise cycle than to finger repetition. The *Gradus* shows that "practice" and "planning" can share one format: a small study, then correction.
