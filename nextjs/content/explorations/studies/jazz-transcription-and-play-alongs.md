# Jazz practice: transcribing solos and playing along

**Wall label**

Improvisation looks like the opposite of practice: the music is made up on the spot. But jazz musicians practice for it in very concrete ways. They copy solos from records, note by note, by ear, and play them along with the recording until they match. They learn short phrases, sometimes called "licks," and move them through every key. Since 1967, many have practiced with Jamey Aebersold's play-along records: a band with the soloist left out, so you can be the soloist. What sounds spontaneous on stage sits on years of this kind of repetition.

## What it is

- **Transcribing.** A player listens to a recorded solo, works out every note by ear, and learns to play it with the record. Some write it down; others learn it only by ear. Paul Berliner's *Thinking in Jazz* (University of Chicago Press, 1994), based on interviews with more than fifty professional musicians, documents "the rigorous practice and thought artists devote to jazz outside of performance" (publisher's description).
- **A famous story, which may be legend.** Charlie Parker is said to have spent a summer in the Ozarks around 1937 learning Lester Young's solos from Count Basie records by heart. Parker researchers note that this is part of "Parker's folklore," and that the real history was probably more spread out over time (Bird Lives, citing Driggs and Haddix 2005). Keep the story as an example of a practice, not as a documented event.
- **Play-alongs.** Jamey Aebersold released the first volume of his Play-A-Long series in 1967. The series grew to more than 130 volumes. Each recording has a professional rhythm section (usually piano, bass and drums) with no melody instrument. Piano and bass are panned to opposite stereo channels, so a pianist or bassist can turn off "their" part and play it themselves (Wikipedia, Aebersold).
- Volume 1, *How to Play Jazz and Improvise*, contains tracks such as minor chords held for 8 or 4 bars, blues in B-flat and F, a cycle of dominant seventh chords, and a minor-to-dominant progression in all 12 keys (publisher listing). These are drills: one harmonic pattern, many times, in many keys.

## What the drill was for

Transcribing trains the ear, the hands, and the style at once. You have to hear a phrase, find it on your instrument, and match its timing and sound. After many solos, a player has a store of phrases and a feel for how great players connect them. Musicians often describe this as learning a language. Berliner's book is framed the same way: jazz improvisation "as a language, an aesthetic, and a tradition" (publisher's description).

The play-along isolates a different skill: keeping your place in the form while making something up. The recording does not stop when you get lost. You have to find your way back in.

## Listen closely

- **A solo that players have long studied:** Lester Young's tenor saxophone solo on "Lady Be Good," recorded by a small group from the Count Basie band under the name Jones-Smith Incorporated in late 1936 (Vocalion 3459). Sources disagree on the date: some discographies give October 9; others give November 9, in Chicago (Wikipedia, "Oh, Lady Be Good!"; Wikipedia, *Classic Columbia, Okeh and Vocalion: Lester Young with Count Basie*).
- **Listen for the chorus structure.** "Lady Be Good" is a 32-bar song in AABA form. Count bars in groups of eight. Can you hear where the contrasting B section (the "bridge") starts? A published transcription of Young's solo is linked below.
- **Listen to one phrase five times.** Pick any two-second phrase from Young's solo. Each time, listen for one thing only: first the pitch shape (up or down), then the rhythm, then where it starts relative to the beat, then the tone (breathy or hard), then how it ends.
- **A play-along track:** if you can access Aebersold Vol. 1, play a blues track and notice what is *missing*: the melody. The silence is the space for you.

## From study to practice

Transcription is practice in the strict sense: a small piece of someone else's work, repeated until it is in your body. But it is also a study in the art-school sense, like copying a master painting in a museum. The copy is not the goal; what the copy teaches is. The play-along adds the other half of this pack's theme. It is a stage set with a hole in it. You rehearse the real situation, minus the risk. Between them, transcription supplies the vocabulary and the play-along gives you a place to use it.
