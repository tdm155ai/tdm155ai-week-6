# Stanislavski's production score for The Seagull

**Wall label**

In the summer of 1898 Konstantin Stanislavski was sent away to plan a play he did not like. Chekhov's *The Seagull* had flopped in St. Petersburg two years before. Stanislavski found it "strange and monotonous." Still, he was the only one at the new Moscow Art Theatre who knew how to write a full staging plan. So he sat in a tower on his brother's estate, looking at the steppe, and wrote down everything: how each line is meant, what voice to use, where each actor moves, how they walk and what they do with their hands. He mailed the plans to rehearsals near Moscow. The production made the theatre's name.

## What it is

The director's copy of the play, with the staging written out beside the text. Stanislavski called it the "mise en scène"; it is usually called his production plan or score. He described what such a copy held: "how, where, and in what way one was to understand the role and the hints of the author, what voice one was to use, how to act and move, where and how to change position." There were "special drawings" for entrances, exits and changes of position, and notes on scenery, costumes, make-up, "manners, way of walking" [1].

He says he had three or four weeks to do it [1]. Accounts date the start to August 1898 at the estate near Kharkiv [2]. The premiere was on 17 December 1898 (Old Style), 29 December (New Style) [2]. The score was published in Russian in 1938 and in English in 1952, edited by S. D. Balukhaty and translated by David Magarshack [2][3].

## What it was for

To fix the whole performance before the actors arrived at it. Stanislavski wrote the plans in the country and sent them to Moscow, "from where they were to be taken to Pushkino where the preparatory rehearsals were taking place" [1]. Nemirovich-Danchenko ran most of those rehearsals [2].

Stanislavski later described the method plainly and critically. The director, "hiding in his study," made a detailed staging "that agreed with his emotions, his inner sight and hearing," and "these orders were binding for all of the actors." It was, he wrote, the "despotic" method of a time when the actors "were still merely pupils" [1].

## Look closely

- **He planned what he did not yet understand.** "I wrote the mise en scène, and still I did not understand, although, unknown to myself, I had apparently felt its substance" [1]. A plan can be ahead of its maker.
- **Drawings as well as words.** He "storyboarded key moments of the play with small drawings," according to the Wikipedia account drawn from Benedetti [2].
- **Tiny physical business.** The score has the characters "wipe away dribble, blow their noses, smack their lips, wipe away sweat, or clean their teeth and nails with matchsticks" (Worrall, quoted in [2]). The aim, on most readings, was to show an inner life under the surface of the lines.
- **Praise from the rehearsal room.** He was surprised to get praise from Pushkino, "because the actors themselves, who did not like the play, wrote the same things" as Nemirovich-Danchenko [1].
- **The set.** Viktor Simov designed the early Art Theatre productions [4]. His design shows the lake behind the makeshift stage of Act One. In Chekhov's stage direction, that little stage "screens the lake from view" until its curtain rises (Fell translation, [6]); Treplev then boasts that "the eye travels direct to the lake." Commons dates the image 1898 on the authority of a print seller; treat that date with care.

## From study to practice

This is planning at its most complete: a whole performance written down by one person, away from the actors. In that sense it resembles Kurosawa's paintings or a full-size study in painting.

It also marks a turning point (interpretation). Stanislavski spent the next forty years moving away from this method. His later work put the actor's own repeated, exploratory rehearsal at the centre, rather than a score handed down from the director's study. Read his own judgment of the 1898 plan (in [1]) next to the Meyerhold and Dalcroze cases in this pack: the century's question became how to train the actor so the plan does not have to do all the work.
