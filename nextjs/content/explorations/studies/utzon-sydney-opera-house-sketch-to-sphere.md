# Utzon's Sydney Opera House: from competition sketch to one sphere

**Wall label**

In 1957 a Danish architect, Jørn Utzon, won the competition for an opera house in Sydney. His entry was a set of drawings of sail-like roofs over a stepped platform. The judges said they had "returned again and again to the study of these drawings." But the shells were freehand curves, each one different. Nobody knew how to build them. For about four years Utzon and the engineers at Ove Arup tried scheme after scheme. The answer came from a model: every shell could be cut from the surface of one sphere. Then the roof could be made from repeated, precast parts, like the ribs in this photograph.

## What it is

The design history of the Sydney Opera House roof, from Jørn Utzon's competition drawings to the "spherical solution."

- **The competition.** The New South Wales government held an international competition, announced in 1956. The Sydney Opera House says there were more than 220 entries from 28 countries. Utzon's entry was number 218. The winner was announced on 29 January 1957.
- **The drawings.** The Sydney Opera House's online story reproduces several sheets, including a site plan and a west elevation. The assessors' report praised the concept and warned: "Because of its very originality, it is clearly a controversial design."
- **The problem.** According to *Civil Engineering* (ASCE), the original shell forms were freehand and individual. The design team worked through about twelve schemes before the spherical solution.
- **The solution.** The Sydney Opera House says that in late 1961, in his office at Hellebæk, Denmark, Utzon was stacking shell models and saw that each shell could derive "from a single, constant form, such as the plane of a sphere." In 1962 he had the Helsingør shipyard make a wooden model of the top of a sphere to show it. The final geometry was set out in the "Yellow Book" (1962).
- **The result.** The roof was built from 2,194 precast concrete segments (ASCE). More than a million tiles cover the shells. The building opened on 20 October 1973.

## What the sketch/study was for

The competition sketches did one job well. They sold an idea. The judges could see the platform and the shells. The sketches did not have to say how the shells would stand up. I read that looseness as a strength at the competition stage. It also left years of engineering work for later.

The models did a different job. They were where the geometry was solved. A shell cut from a sphere has the same curvature everywhere. ASCE quotes the design documentation: "The appearance of the free-form roof was replicated closely, but the curvature throughout is constant." That meant the ribs could be made as repeated precast segments.

I read this case as two stages of preparation. The sketch opened the question. The model closed it. Buxton's distinction between sketch and prototype (see the design entry in this pack) fits closely: the competition drawings "suggest" and "provoke"; the sphere model "answers" and "resolves."

## Look closely

Look at the photograph of the shell underside.

- The ribs are made of segments. Each rib is a stack of pieces with visible joints.
- Neighbouring ribs look nearly identical. That is the point of the sphere: same curvature, so similar parts.
- The ribs fan out from a ridge, forming a chevron pattern where they meet.
- The glass wall at right sits on its own copper-coloured framing. It is a different system from the concrete shell.

## From study to practice

This case connects to practice in an unusual way. Repetition entered the building itself. Once every shell came from one sphere, the parts could be made in series. The roof was "solved" by turning a set of one-off curves into a family of repeated elements.

There is a lesson for students in that. A design that only works as a sketch may need to be redrawn in terms of something you can repeat. In practice, that is a module, a unit, or a rule. Utzon's sphere is a rule. Once you have it, you can make the next shell without starting from zero.
