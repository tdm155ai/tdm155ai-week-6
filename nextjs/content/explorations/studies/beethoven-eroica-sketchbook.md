# Beethoven's Eroica sketchbook (Landsberg 6)

**Wall label**

Between late 1802 and early 1804, Beethoven filled a bound book of music paper with drafts. Most of the first 90 or so pages are the Third Symphony, the "Eroica." He did not write the piece out once and then polish it. He wrote one stretch, then wrote it again, and again, with alternatives stacked above the line. The book has 182 pages and is almost all in ink. It was a desk book, used at home. Today it is in Kraków. Its pages show that one of the boldest symphonies ever written started out sounding, in places, quite ordinary.

*(The image shows a page from a later Beethoven sketchbook, not Landsberg 6. No public-domain image of Landsberg 6 was found.)*

## What it is

- A bound sketchbook in oblong folio, 182 pages, with 16 or 18 staves per page. Gustav Nottebohm, who first described it in 1880, noted that apart from one pencil page it is written in ink. He concluded that Beethoven "can only have used it at home" (Nottebohm 1880, p. 3–4; my translation).
- It is now called **Landsberg 6**, after Ludwig Landsberg, a collector who owned it in the 19th century. It belonged to the library in Berlin until the end of the Second World War. Since 1945 it has been in Kraków, now at the Biblioteka Jagiellońska (Center for Beethoven Research, Boston University; Lockwood and Gosman 2013).
- Nottebohm notes that Beethoven worked through the Eroica with few interruptions. Between the symphony sketches there are only small items, such as marches and a song. On page 64 there is a melody later used in the "Pastoral" Symphony (Nottebohm 1880).
- It covers late 1802 to early 1804. Besides the Eroica, it holds large sketch groups for the "Waldstein" Piano Sonata, Op. 53, and the first numbers of the opera *Leonore* (later *Fidelio*) (Lockwood and Gosman 2013).
- The earliest Eroica ideas are not in this book but in the slightly earlier "Wielhorsky" sketchbook (fall 1802 to early 1803) (Lockwood 1981; Center for Beethoven Research).

## What the sketch was for

Beethoven used sketching to solve continuity problems: how to get from one idea to the next, and how to make a long movement hold together. Nottebohm observed that pages 10 to 41 deal "almost exclusively" with the first movement. There is no single sketch of the whole movement. The largest sketches each cover one section, and there are four large drafts of the first part (the exposition), each with variants.

Nottebohm also noticed something useful for anyone who makes things. The first drafts of developmental passages, he wrote, have "little or nothing of Beethoven's manner" and "border on the ordinary." They were, in his image, the soil in which something grew (Nottebohm 1880, p. 53; my paraphrase and translation). The sketch is where the ordinary version gets written down so it can be improved.

## Listen closely

Use a full score on IMSLP: [Symphony No. 3, Op. 55](https://imslp.org/wiki/Symphony_No.3,_Op.55_(Beethoven,_Ludwig_van)).

- **First movement, bars 394–395.** Just before the recapitulation, a single horn plays the opening theme in E-flat while the violins are still holding the dominant harmony. It sounds like a mistake. Ferdinand Ries, Beethoven's pupil, thought so at the first rehearsal and said so. Ries later wrote that he thought he was "in danger of getting my ears boxed." Nottebohm names this "much-discussed horn passage" as an exception to his rule that first drafts sound ordinary: it is odd, on purpose (Nottebohm 1880, p. 53).
- **Finale, the opening.** After a short, loud flourish, the strings play a bare, plucked line. This is the *bass* of the theme, not the tune. The tune arrives later in the oboe.
- **The same theme in four works.** Beethoven used this tune in his Contredanse WoO 14 No. 7, in the finale of his ballet *The Creatures of Prometheus*, Op. 43 (1801), in the Piano Variations Op. 35 (1802), and in the Eroica finale. Listen to them in that order. Op. 35 also starts with the bass alone ("Introduzione col basso del tema"). The piano variations work like a study for the symphony finale. That is an interpretation, but a common one.
- **Scores to compare:** [Contredanses WoO 14](https://imslp.org/wiki/12_Contredanses,_WoO_14_(Beethoven,_Ludwig_van)), [Prometheus Op. 43](https://imslp.org/wiki/Die_Geschöpfe_des_Prometheus,_Op.43_(Beethoven,_Ludwig_van)), [Variations Op. 35](https://imslp.org/wiki/Variations_and_Fugue_in_E-flat_major,_Op.35_(Beethoven,_Ludwig_van)).
- **Nottebohm's 1880 book** with his transcriptions of the sketches is free on the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/einskizzenbuchvo00beet). Even without reading German you can see the drafts lined up one after another.

## From study to practice

This is the clearest *sketch* in the music section. It is planning, not repetition for its own sake. But the drafts are repetitive in a way that looks like practice. Beethoven wrote the same passage many times, changing a little each time, until the continuity worked. The four-work history of the finale theme adds a second layer. A dance tune became a ballet finale, then a set of piano variations, then a symphony movement. Each version was a finished piece. Each also worked as a study for the next one. Seen this way, a "sketch" does not have to be a scrap. It can be a complete work that is also a rehearsal for a bigger one.
