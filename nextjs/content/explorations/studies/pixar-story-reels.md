# Pixar story reels

**Wall label**

At Pixar, a film exists as a rough movie long before it is animated. Story artists draw thousands of boards. Editors cut them together with scratch dialogue, often read by staff, and temporary music. The result is the reel: a crude, watchable version of the whole film. Ed Catmull, Pixar's co-founder, put it bluntly: "early on, all of our movies suck." The reel is how they find out. In November 1993, a reel of the first half of *Toy Story* went so badly with Disney executives that production stopped. Pixar rewrote the story, and Woody, before animation resumed.

## What it is

Catmull's description: "first we draw storyboards of the script and then edit them together with temporary voices and music to make a crude mock-up of the film, known as reels" [1]. A storyboard drawn to pin on a wall (see the Disney entry) becomes a storyboard cut to time. Some writers call this an animatic.

## What it was for

The reel tests things a board cannot: **timing, voice, and feeling over the full length**. How long does a pause last? Does a line land when you hear it? Is this character likeable for ninety minutes?

The Toy Story case shows what that buys. On 19 November 1993, Pixar screened the first half of the film for Disney executives. Peter Schneider, head of Disney feature animation, halted production [2]. During scratch recording for the reels, Tom Hanks had already said Woody had become a "real jerk" [2]. The story was rewritten over about three months: Woody went from tyrant to leader, and Buzz was changed to make it clearer that he really does not know he is a toy. Production resumed in February 1994 [2]. Making that discovery in drawings cost months. Making it in finished animation would have cost far more (interpretation, but widely repeated).

## Look closely

- **The materials are cheap on purpose.** Drawings, not models. Staff voices, not stars, for most scratch tracks. Temp music. Everything can be thrown away.
- **Volume.** Pixar says more than four thousand storyboard drawings are made as "the blueprint for the action and dialog" of a feature [3]. Many are redrawn more than once.
- **The reel is made for critique.** Catmull's Braintrust is a group of directors who watch the latest reel and talk for hours about what is not working; the film's director decides what to change [1].
- **"From suck to not-suck."** Catmull: "Pixar films are not good at first, and our job is to make them so — to go, as I say, 'from suck to not-suck'" [1]. The reel is a plan designed to fail early.
- **Images omitted** because Pixar's boards and reels are under copyright. Some reels appear as DVD and Blu-ray extras.

## From study to practice

A reel is a plan: it is the wireframe of the film, but one that runs in time. It sits further toward the finished work than a pinned storyboard.

It also has a rhythm like practice (interpretation). A sequence may go through many reel versions. Each cycle repeats the same steps: draw, cut, watch, critique, redraw. The team gets better at the film by remaking the same stretch of it. That is closer to rehearsal than to a one-time blueprint.
