# Seurat's Grande Jatte: panels, drawings, and a big canvas

## The story

In 1884 Georges Seurat, aged 24, began a very large painting of Parisians at leisure on the island of La Grande Jatte in the Seine. The canvas is about 2 by 3 metres. He worked on it from 1884 into 1886.

His preparation fell into two groups, as the Art Institute of Chicago describes it. First, small oil sketches on wooden panels, many probably painted on the island. These test colour, light and the placement of figures. The Art Institute counts "over 20" painted studies. Second, black conté crayon drawings on rough paper, made to work out the shapes of single figures, groups and trees. The Art Institute counts about 40 studies overall. Then came larger canvases, like the Met's, where the whole design was set out before the final.

The final painting is in the Art Institute. Seurat returned to it after about two years. He strengthened some silhouettes and added figures. Close up, the surface is small touches of complementary colours laid side by side. In 1888–89 he added a painted border.

## Plan or practice?

Nearly all of these are planning for one picture. Each panel, drawing or canvas tests a choice that ends up in, or is cut from, the final.

The conté drawings are the interesting case. Seurat had been drawing in conté crayon on rough laid paper for years before 1884. The Art Institute's own collection has examples from 1881–83. The way he makes form out of tone, with no outlines, is a practised habit he brought to this job. So the *technique* is practice; the *subjects* (this woman, this monkey, these trees) are planning. Our reading, not a documented claim: the drawings are where long practice meets a specific plan.
