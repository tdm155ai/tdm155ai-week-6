# Leonardo's horses in battle: from the Adoration to Anghiari

## The story

This sequence covers two pictures, twenty-five years apart, and neither one exists as Leonardo meant it.

In 1481 the monks of San Donato a Scopeto, outside Florence, commissioned an altarpiece of the Adoration of the Magi. Leonardo laid in a crowded scene on a panel about 2.4 metres square. Behind the Virgin he put ruins, and on the right, horsemen fighting. In 1482 he left for Milan, and the panel stayed unfinished. It is in the Uffizi. The horse drawings of c. 1480–81 belong to this period.

In 1503 Florence asked him for a huge mural in the council hall of the Palazzo della Signoria (now Palazzo Vecchio). The subject was the Battle of Anghiari, a Florentine victory over Milan in 1440. A revised contract followed in May 1504. Leonardo noted that he began painting on the wall on 6 June 1505, as a storm broke. He used an oil-based method instead of true fresco. The paint would not dry properly and colours ran. He left for Milan in 1506, and the mural was never finished. During Giorgio Vasari's remodelling of the hall in the mid-16th century, it was lost.

We know the central group, the fight for the standard, mainly through copies. The most famous is the Louvre drawing reworked by Rubens around 1603. Leonardo's own studies for horses, riders and faces survive, many of them at Windsor.

## Plan or practice?

**Planning a specific work:** the Uffizi perspective study (it fixes the Adoration's space), *A cavalcade* and *Galloping and kicking horses* (they test groups and actions for Anghiari).

**Practice, or research that serves many works:** RCIN 912315, with its one horse seen from three sides, could serve almost any picture with horses. The fury sheet is, in the Royal Collection's words, "background research". It compares a horse, a lion and a man to understand expression. It doesn't place anything in a composition.

Our view is that Leonardo's horses show the two kinds of study feeding each other across decades. The Sforza work of c. 1490 sits between these two groups. It plausibly explains why the Anghiari horses look so sure of their anatomy. That's an interpretation, not a documented link.
