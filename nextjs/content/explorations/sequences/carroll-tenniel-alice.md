# Alice: from Carroll's handmade book to Tenniel's engravings

## The story

On 4 July 1862, Charles Dodgson (Lewis Carroll) and Robinson Duckworth rowed three daughters of Henry Liddell up the river from Folly Bridge in Oxford to Godstow. One was Alice, aged 10. Carroll told a story on the way. He started writing it down the next day and began work in earnest that November [1].

The result was *Alice's Adventures Under Ground*, a small book written out by hand with his own drawings. He gave it to Alice on 26 November 1864, inscribed "A Christmas Gift to a Dear Child in Memory of a Summer's Day" [1]. It is now in the British Library, Add MS 46700 [1, 2].

Meanwhile Carroll had met the publisher Alexander Macmillan in October 1863 and asked the *Punch* cartoonist John Tenniel to illustrate a longer version [1]. The published book is about twice as long as the manuscript. Episodes such as the Mad Tea-Party are not in *Under Ground* [1]. Tenniel made 42 illustrations, which engravers cut into wood blocks [1]. Some of his preliminary drawings are at Harvard's Houghton Library [3].

The first printing of 2,000 copies, in July 1865, was withdrawn because Tenniel objected to the print quality. Carroll paid for a new printing by Richard Clay, and the book was published in November 1865 [1].

## Plan or practice?

Carroll's manuscript is not a plan for Tenniel. It is a finished gift for one reader. It became a first draft only when Carroll decided to publish. Tenniel's caterpillar keeps Carroll's moment; his pack of cards moves to a later one.

Tenniel's drawings are plans in the strict sense. Each one was made to be cut into wood by someone else. Compare the Houghton study with the printed jury box: the pose is fixed in pencil, and the engraving keeps it while turning soft tone into hatched line.
