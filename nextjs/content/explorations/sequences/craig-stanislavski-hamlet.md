# Craig and Stanislavski's Hamlet: from a model stage to the Moscow premiere

## The story

In 1908 the English designer and director Edward Gordon Craig was invited to the Moscow Art Theatre. Stanislavski had met him through the dancer Isadora Duncan. The theatre gave Craig *Hamlet*. Rehearsals began in March 1909. Stanislavski fell ill with typhoid in 1910 and the opening was postponed. The production finally opened on 23 December 1911 (Old Style), 5 January 1912 (New Style) [1].

Craig went away to prepare, then came back "with a complete plan" and models of the scenery, Stanislavski recalled. In a rehearsal room the theatre built "a large model reproduction of our stage," lit by "an exact copy" of the real lighting. Craig refused painted scenery and used "simple convex screens which could be placed on the stage in endless combinations." He sat at a table and moved "figures cut out of wood" around the model "with the help of a long stick," and "actually demonstrated all the movements of the actors." Stanislavski and his assistants wrote the motives for each move into their own copies of the play [2].

Full size was harder. "The great screens could not stand up well and would fall. If a single screen fell, all the others followed it." Shifting them needed "many long rehearsals with the stage hands" [2]. Stanislavski wrote that about an hour before the first performance, after a last rehearsal of the scene changes, one screen leaned, fell, and brought down the whole set "like a house of cards." From then on the screens were changed behind a curtain, not in view of the audience as Craig had wanted [2].

Accounts conflict. A popular story says the screens fell during the performance. The Wikipedia account, drawing on Laurence Senelick, reports that Craig objected to Stanislavski's anecdote and that Stanislavski agreed it happened only in rehearsal; it also says the screens had been built taller than Craig specified [1]. Stanislavski's own 1924 text places the collapse just before the premiere, in an empty house, not during the show [2].

Craig published his designs the next year. Of the court scene he wrote: "You see the stage divided by a barrier. On the one side sits Hamlet, fallen, as it were, into a dream, on the other side you see his dream" [3].

## Plan or practice?

This is a sequence about planning. The model let Craig make decisions about space, light and movement without the cost of the full stage. It also let him rehearse with figures that did exactly what he wanted. That is the plan's strength and its weakness. Wooden figures do not get tired, and model screens do not fall over.

Practice came in at full scale. The stage hands needed "many long rehearsals" to shift the screens, and still could not do it reliably in view [2]. Craig was also unhappy with the actors [2]. In his book he wrote, "Art is useless after events: it must precede them" [3]. The Moscow *Hamlet* shows both sides of that claim: the plan came first and was powerful, but the production also needed repeated practice by the people who had to carry it out (interpretation).
