# Jefferson's Rotunda: drawings, a building, a fire, a rebuilding

## The story

The Rotunda is the library at the head of the Lawn at the University of Virginia. Thomas Jefferson designed it after the Pantheon in Rome. Its dome is 77 feet across, "half that of the Pantheon and consequently one fourth in area, and one eighth in volume" [1]. Benjamin Henry Latrobe had suggested a domed central building in a letter of 17 July 1817 [1].

Jefferson drew the south elevation from 1818 to 29 March 1819 [2]. He drew on paper ruled with coordinate lines, like graph paper. The floor plans followed. Commons, citing O'Neal (1960), dates them before the Board of Visitors approved the plans on 2 April 1821 [3]. Inside the circle he fitted oval rooms below and the library under the dome. Construction began in 1822 and finished in 1826, shortly after Jefferson died [1].

In 1853 an Annex was added on the north side. On 27 October 1895 a fire started in the Annex and gutted the Rotunda [1, 4]. Stanford White, of McKim, Mead & White, rebuilt it. His version had two floors instead of Jefferson's three and a larger dome room, under a new fireproof dome [1]. For the Bicentennial in 1976, White's interior was gutted and rebuilt, at a cost of $2.4 million, to Jefferson's original design [1]. A further renovation ran from 2012 to 2016 [1].

## Plan or practice?

Pure plan. Every drawing here was made for one building. The elevation and plans set out geometry for builders to follow.

The building's later life turns the drawings into something more. White did not follow them after the fire. In 1976 they became the record from which the lost interior was rebuilt. A plan made to start a building ended up as the means of bringing it back.
