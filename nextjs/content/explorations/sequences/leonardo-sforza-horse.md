# Leonardo's Sforza horse: studies for a bronze that was never cast

## The story

Ludovico Sforza, ruler of Milan, wanted a giant bronze horse and rider in memory of his father, Francesco. Leonardo had offered to make it around 1482. The Royal Collection dates the commission itself to the mid-1480s. It was meant to be the largest equestrian statue in the world.

The first designs show the horse rearing over a fallen enemy. Around 1490 he changed to a walking horse. That pose is less dramatic and, we'd guess, far easier to support in bronze. From then on, the Royal Collection says, he studied the horse intensively, in casual and formal poses. He drew horses from the side and the front and wrote measurements on the drawings.

By 1493 he had a full-size clay model of the horse, without its rider. It was shown in Milan for the wedding of Bianca Maria Sforza and the Emperor Maximilian. He worked out how to cast it in pieces, with iron frames holding the moulds. In November 1494 Ludovico sent the bronze to his father-in-law, Ercole d'Este, to make cannon against the French. In 1499 the French took Milan. Their soldiers shot at the clay horse, and it did not survive.

So the "final" work in this sequence is lost and was never a bronze. What survives is the preparation.

## Plan or practice?

Most of these sheets plan one specific object. The measured drawings (RCIN 912319, 912290) and the casting mould only make sense for the monument. They plan.

The c. 1490 profile studies are harder to call. Leonardo had been drawing horses since the late 1470s (see the companion sequence on the Adoration and Anghiari). Our reading is that these sheets do both jobs: they are research for the statue and also part of a long habit of drawing horses. Support for that reading: the horses of Anghiari, a decade later, are not measured or posed for bronze, yet they draw on the same close knowledge of the animal's body. That is an interpretation, not a documented fact.
