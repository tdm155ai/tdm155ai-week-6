# Constable's Hay Wain: from a pocket sketch to a six-foot canvas

## The story

John Constable painted the Stour valley in Suffolk, where he grew up, all his life. *The Hay Wain* shows the millpond at Flatford. An empty farm wagon pulled by three horses stands in the shallow ford. Willy Lott's farmhouse is on the left. Haymakers work in the far meadow.

He painted it in his London studio, not on site. He drew on oil sketches made in Suffolk over many years. According to the National Gallery catalogue, he began the composition late in 1820 and sent it to the Royal Academy by 10 April 1821. It was shown that year as *Landscape: Noon*. "The Hay Wain" is a later nickname. In 1824 the painting went to the Paris Salon, where it caused a stir, and Constable won a gold medal from Charles X.

For his big exhibition pictures, the "six-footers," Constable often made a full-size oil sketch first. The V&A owns two: this one and the one for *The Leaping Horse* (1825). He kept them. Richard and Samuel Redgrave, writing in 1866 and quoted by the V&A, recalled: "He used to say...that he had no objection to part with the corn, but not with the field that grew it."

## Plan or practice?

The Yale sketch and the V&A full-scale study plan one specific picture. The full-scale study is an unusual kind of planning: a whole second painting, the same size, made to make decisions at real scale.

*Willy Lott's House* is different. It was painted in 1816, years before this picture existed. Constable painted this house and this stretch of river again and again (Wikimedia Commons holds versions dated from 1802 to about 1830). Our reading: these repeated sketches are practice on a motif, the way a musician runs scales. When he came to plan *The Hay Wain*, the motif was already in his hand. That supply of practised motifs is arguably what let him compose a Suffolk scene in a London studio.
