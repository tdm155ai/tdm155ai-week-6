# Géricault's Raft of the Medusa: choosing the moment

## The story

In July 1816 the French frigate *Méduse* ran aground off West Africa. About 150 people were put on a makeshift raft and cast off. After 13 days, only 15 were alive when the brig *Argus* picked them up. They had fought, killed, and eaten the dead. Two survivors, Savigny and Corréard, published an account in 1817, and it became a political scandal.

Géricault, then in his late twenties, decided to paint it. He spent about 18 months on the project and about eight months on the canvas itself, from November 1818 to July 1819. The finished painting is nearly 5 by 7 metres. It hung at the Salon of 1819 under the bland title *Scène de naufrage* (Shipwreck scene). It is in the Louvre.

The studies show two different problems. First: *which moment?* He drew the mutiny, the cannibalism, the rescue by boat, and the first sighting of the *Argus*. He chose the sighting, the moment of hope that might still be false. Second: *how to build it?* He worked through pen sketches and two oil sketches, posed survivors and friends as models, built a scale model of the raft with the ship's carpenter, and, as reported, studied corpses and severed limbs from a hospital.

## Plan or practice?

Almost everything here is planning. Each sheet is aimed at one painting and one decision. Most of them test the moment, the arrangement, or a figure.

The exception is the anatomical work: the studies of corpses and severed limbs that Géricault reportedly made in this period. These train the eye on how dead flesh looks under light. They don't solve a composition problem. Our reading is that they are practice in service of a plan. He trained his hand on a motif so the foreground dead would be believable. None of those sheets is in this filmstrip; see the notes file for candidates.
