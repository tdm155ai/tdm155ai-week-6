# Keats's To Autumn: the draft at Harvard

## The story

On 19 September 1819, John Keats walked near Winchester along the River Itchen. He wrote "To Autumn" after it [1]. Two days later he wrote to his friend John Hamilton Reynolds: "How beautiful the season is now – How fine the air." The stubble fields, he said, looked "warm – in the same way that some pictures look warm" [1]. He also copied the poem into a letter to his friend Richard Woodhouse [1].

Houghton Library at Harvard holds an early draft: one leaf, written on both sides, with revisions [2]. It shows the poem being made. Wikipedia lists changes between this draft and the printed text: "Drows'd with red poppies" became "Drows'd with the fume of poppies," "While a gold cloud" became "While barred clouds," and "whoever seeks for thee may find" became "whoever seeks abroad may find" [1]. Several of these changes are already visible on the leaf itself, written over the struck-out words.

The poem was printed in 1820 in Keats's third and last collection, *Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems*, published by Taylor and Hessey [1]. Keats died in Rome in February 1821.

## Plan or practice?

Neither, exactly. This is a draft, the writer's version of working on the canvas itself. There is no separate sketch to compare: the trying out happens line by line on the page.

Look at what gets crossed out. Wikipedia notes that critics single out the poppies: "red" (a colour) becomes "the fume of" (a smell). The gold cloud becomes *barred* clouds. The second stanza was rewritten in place, with new lines squeezed between the struck ones. The third stanza, on the back, has fewer corrections.
