# Sargent's Triumph of Religion: from sketch to wall

## The story

In 1890 Charles Follen McKim, the architect of the new Boston Public Library, gave John Singer Sargent, then 34 and known for portraits, a long barrel-vaulted hall on the third floor and freedom over the subject. Sargent called his scheme *Triumph of Religion*: a few moments in the history of Egyptian and Assyrian belief, Judaism, and Christianity. He painted it on canvas in England, in a studio he shared with Edwin Austin Abbey (then the largest in England, the library says), and shipped it to Boston to be fixed to the walls in four installments: the north end in 1895, the south end in 1903, the east and west lunettes and the vault in 1916, and the framed *Synagogue* and *Church* panels in 1919. The central east-wall panel, *Sermon on the Mount*, was never painted. Sargent died in London in 1925, before he could travel to Boston, and the panel is still blank.

Why this mural and not his later one in the rotunda of the Museum of Fine Arts? Both have plenty of studies. But the library project ran 29 years rather than nine, and its studies are spread much wider: oil sketches and scale-model panels at Harvard's Fogg, a whole bound album of 111 leaves of drawings for a single lunette (*Israel and the Law*, also at Harvard), oil studies and drawings at the library itself, charcoal figures at the National Gallery of Art, archway and vault designs at Yale, and stray studies in Fitchburg and Raleigh. This filmstrip is a small selection.

## How it scales up

Read the strip as a zoom. Sargent kept switching between the whole and the part, and between small and large.

- **The room.** Quick oil sketches of a whole end of the hall in perspective (Harvard 1937.213), then a single wall head on (1937.215). These decide where things go.
- **The figure.** Charcoal drawings from posed, draped models (the NGA's *Ezekiel*) and oil studies of a single robe (the Fitchburg *Hosea*). These solve one figure at a time.
- **The section.** Big oil studies that put the figures back together (Harvard's *Frieze of Prophets* study is almost two metres wide) and test colour across a run of them.
- **The model.** For the south end he built a scale model of that end of the hall, with painted panels, cast plaster and gold paper standing in for relief. The library describes his model of the hall as one-third scale. At one third, a figure that is a metre tall in the model is three metres tall on the wall, and you can look up into the model and see whether it reads from the floor.
- **The arithmetic.** On the east-wall oil sketches the scale is written down: one Gog and Magog sketch is marked '1/4 scale.'
- **The wall.** The final canvases, with about 600 raised and gilded relief elements (the library's count) built up to catch the light in a dim hall.

The sequence is grouped by wall in installation order; within each wall the order follows the zoom. Many of the dates are ranges, so the order within a wall is an editorial grouping, not a proven chronology.

## Plan or practice?

Almost all of this is planning. Each object answers a question about one wall: where the figures go, how a robe falls, whether the gold will glitter, whether a figure will read from twenty feet below. The scale model is the clearest case: a plan built in three dimensions, to be looked at the way the hall would be.

There is practice too, at the level of the figure. Harvard's album for *Israel and the Law* holds sheet after sheet of drapery, arms, legs and hands drawn from models, many more than the panel strictly needed. Those sheets look like Sargent getting his hand used to a body or a fold before he committed it to a canvas metres wide. See [study](/concepts/visual-art/study), [preparatory drawing](/concepts/visual-art/preparatory-drawing), [color study](/concepts/visual-art/color-study), and [hundred hands](/concepts/visual-art/hundred-hands).

The blank panel is the other lesson. For *Sermon on the Mount* there is one faint pencil sketch and one small passage in an oil elevation, and they don't agree. Curators have noted how few studies exist for it, and the controversy over *Synagogue* in 1919 may have left Sargent unsure how to go on. Without the long run of sketch, study, model and section that every other wall had, the panel never reached full size.
