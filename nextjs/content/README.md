## Who used to make this?

Not long ago, a modest web app (sign-up, log-in, uploads, payments, email) took a founder, a product manager, a UX researcher, designers, front-end and back-end engineers, a database engineer, DevOps, QA, security, a technical writer, and a lawyer. A two-minute cinematic short took a writer, a director, producers, a storyboard artist, a casting director, a location manager, a production designer, a DP, a camera operator, a focus puller, a gaffer, grips, a script supervisor, a sound mixer, hair and makeup, actors, an editor, VFX artists, a colorist, a sound designer, and a composer. Fifteen to fifty people for the app; twenty-five to a hundred for the short. ([The full headcount](/who-used-to-make-this).)

Today one person with a prompt can produce a convincing version of either. Coding agents absorb the wiring, the boilerplate, and the config; video models absorb the camera move, the lighting, the set, the costume, and even the performance. Whole industries of making are collapsing into a single interface, and that is a real democratization: things that needed a crew and a budget are now open to anyone with an idea.

## The other edge

It cuts the other way too. There is no way that you, one person, know what all of those people knew: what the focus puller knew about depth of field, what the script supervisor knew about which hand held the cup, what the security engineer knew about how apps get broken. In a sense you are doing all of those jobs now. But are you? Do you know anything about them? Have you internalized anything? Are you getting better at anything? Or is what you know how to do just pull the lever on the video slot machine and wait to see if you won?

That is this week's question: **what do learning and skill look like after AI?**

## Two kinds of skills

We'll answer it in two directions at once.

1. **Skills you build in yourself.** Every field that trains people has a name for the small, repeated attempt that builds a skill: painters make studies, musicians play études, athletes run drills, actors rehearse, photographers shoot tests, writers do exercises. In these big production workflows we'll pick out the steps that matter to us, the ones we want to internalize, and practice them the way those fields do: isolate the part, repeat it with variation, get feedback, put it back into the whole.
2. **Skills you build for Claude.** Where we do take AI help, we don't want the slot machine either. We want Claude Code to perform those jobs the right way, and the way to teach it is a *skill* in the other sense: a folder of instructions, examples, and tools that captures how a job should be done. Writing a good one requires knowing what good looks like, which brings us back to the first kind.

The [concepts](/concepts) are the vocabulary for both: what each field calls its studies, how people learn, and what changes when a model can do the work. [Skills](/skills) is where we build the second kind, starting with how Claude skills work. [Explorations](/explorations) is where the studies go.
