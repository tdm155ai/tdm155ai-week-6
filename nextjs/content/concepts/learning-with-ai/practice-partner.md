title: Practice partner
description: The model as sparring partner, drill generator, and critic rather than answer machine; what each use trains.
author: claude

There are at least three ways to use a model that are not asking it for the answer. As a sparring partner: "argue the other side of my cut's structure," and you defend it. As a drill generator: "give me twenty shot descriptions and I'll pick the lens for each," and you do the twenty. As a critic: "here is my draft; tell me what you would change and why," after you have made your own list. Each use puts the attempt on your side of the table and the model on the other, which is the arrangement a sparring partner has with a boxer.

Each trains something different, and only if you hold up your end. Sparring trains the response under pressure, the thing you can say back when challenged. The drill generator trains whatever the drill targets, but the model has only made the drill; the reps are still yours, and a drill you skim is not a drill. The critic trains noticing, provided you looked first; read the critique before you have formed a view and it becomes the answer machine again. Two things go wrong. A model critic is agreeable by default and will find merit in most things; ask it to be specific and to rank, and tell it what you are worried about. And a model's drills reflect its idea of the skill, which may not be the thing you need to practice; check them against a teacher's or a book's.

The design principle, from the experiment in [the assistance effect](assistance-effect.md): help that makes you produce the step behaves differently from help that produces it. Build your prompts so that the partner cannot take your turn.
