Here is the one direct experiment this week leans on. Bastani and colleagues ran a randomized field study in high-school mathematics classes, published in PNAS under the title "Generative AI Can Harm Learning." Students practiced in one of three conditions: a standard GPT chat interface, a version the researchers called a tutor, built with teacher-designed safeguards that gave hints and would not hand over the answer, or no AI at all. During practice, both AI groups did much better than the control group. On the exam that followed, taken without any assistance, the standard chat group did worse than the students who had never had the tool. The tutor group's loss was mostly avoided, though they did not come out ahead on the exam either.

State it carefully, because it is easy to overstate. This is one experiment, in one subject, in one deployment, with one model. It does not show that AI prevents learning, and it does not show that hints always fix the problem. What it does show is that help which does the work for you can improve the session and leave you worse off when the help is gone, which is the [learning versus performance](../learning/learning-vs-performance.md) distinction with a model attached. And it shows that the design of the help matters: assistance built to make you produce the step yourself behaved differently from assistance that produced it for you.

For this week, the lesson is a design rule, not a verdict. For any skill you intend to keep, arrange the assistance so that the attempt stays yours: ask for a hint, a check, a critique, not the answer. For a skill you intend to delegate, test something else: whether you can direct, inspect, and repair what the model did.
