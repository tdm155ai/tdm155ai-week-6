The agent loop is iteration at machine speed: act, look at the result, adjust, act again, until a condition is met. A maker iterates too: version two of the cut, version three, each one trying something and keeping or discarding it. By version seven the cut is better. The question this week insists on is: who learned? If the agent ran the loop, the artifact improved and nothing about you changed; you have a better cut and the same eye. If you ran it, each version was a study, and the record of what each tried is the evidence of your learning. From the outside the two version sevens look identical.

This is the [learning versus performance](../learning/learning-vs-performance.md) distinction applied to a process instead of a session. A changed artifact proves that the arrangement that produced it works. It does not identify which component improved: the model's instructions, a script, your judgment, or none of them, since trial and error with enough trials improves things without anyone learning anything. The way to tell is the record: for each version, what it tried, why, who decided, and what the result changed in your view. Film, writing, and photography each have their own version of this, and each has had to learn the same lesson: a stack of drafts is not a stack of lessons unless someone wrote down what each was for.

In practice, slow the loop where you want to learn. Make a prediction before each version and compare after. Let the agent iterate fast where you have decided the output is what matters and your eye is not the thing being trained.
