title: Abstraction

A programmer writing in Python does not think about registers. An editor who says "cut to the reaction" does not think about frames. A director who says "take it from the top of the scene" does not say which line. Abstraction is working at a level that hides the level below it, and it is how all skill scales. Chunking in the Learning category is the same thing seen from inside the learner: once a sequence has become a unit, you can think in units and hold more of the task in mind. The expert's advantage is not that she sees more detail but that she works in larger pieces whose details take care of themselves. "Make me a thirty-second trailer" is an abstraction over several hundred decisions, and it is a legitimate one.

The cost shows up when the level below misbehaves. If you have only ever worked at the top, you have no handle on the thing that broke. The trailer is wrong in a way you can feel and cannot name, because naming it would mean descending to a level you have never visited. This is the abstraction's bargain: it buys speed and scope with a loss of recourse. Good abstractions are built so that the bargain is fair, with a contract you can check and a seam you can open; [information hiding](information-hiding.md) is the name for doing this well. Bad ones hide the judgment along with the detail.

The question for this week is not whether to work at a high level; you will, and so does everyone. It is whether you can move down one level when the task demands it, and the study for that is in [reopening the box](reopening-the-box.md).
