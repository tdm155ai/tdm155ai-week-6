When you use a calculator for 17 times 23, you have offloaded the arithmetic. When you follow turn-by-turn directions, you have offloaded the map. Autocomplete offloads the spelling, and then the next word. Cognitive offloading is the general name for handing a mental task to something outside your head. It is old: writing is offloaded memory, and a shot list is offloaded planning. Asking a model to structure your essay is the same move at a larger scale.

Offloading gives you capacity. The arithmetic you did not do is attention you can spend on whether the number makes sense. What it costs is the offloaded capacity itself, which stops being exercised, and sometimes the noticing that went with it. If you have arrived somewhere by GPS, you know the feeling: you got there, and you could not get there again. The task that was offloaded leaves no trace in you, and the skills around it (reading the terrain, catching the wrong turn) go unpracticed too, because you were never asked to look. None of this makes offloading wrong. It makes it a choice that should be made per task, and the right question is not "should I use the tool" but "which parts do I want to keep doing myself, and for how long."

Marlon's version for this course: you can get the whole animation from one prompt, so what is left to learn is whatever you decide not to offload. [Attempt first](attempt-first.md) is the rule that keeps a chosen skill in practice; [deskilling](deskilling.md) is what the choice looks like when nobody makes it.
