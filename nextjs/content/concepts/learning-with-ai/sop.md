Before there were skill files there were standard operating procedures: the pilot's pre-flight checklist, the studio's session setup sheet, the run-book an on-call engineer follows at three in the morning. An SOP is a written procedure for a repeated task, meant to be followed by someone who may not be the person who wrote it, in a state (tired, rushed, new) where memory is not to be trusted. A skill folder is an SOP whose reader is a model, and it inherits both the virtues and the vices of the form.

What SOPs fix: variance and forgetting. The steps that go the same way every time go the same way every time, and the one you always skip gets done. Checklists are valued in aviation and surgery for catching the omitted step that an expert was sure she never omits. What SOPs freeze: the decision at the time of writing. A procedure encodes a reason, and the reason can expire while the procedure stays. The mic goes on the left because of a cable run that was rerouted a year ago; the step is still on the sheet. Worse, an SOP can freeze the judgment out of a task, so that the person following it stops noticing the case where it should not apply. A technique in a field is an SOP the field has agreed on; a template in a studio is an SOP for a configuration; each is best when its users still know why.

When you write a skill for Claude, write it like a good SOP: short, with the reason beside the step where the reason matters, and with an instruction to stop when the case does not fit. Date it. Expect to revise it, which is the subject of [instructions vs. training](instructions-vs-training.md).
