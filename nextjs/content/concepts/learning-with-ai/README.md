Ask Claude for a thirty-second trailer cut from your footage and you can have one before lunch. Ask it for a storyboard, a color script, a podcast edit, a character turnaround: same. Every skill this course touches would take years to get good at, and a model can produce a passable version of the output from one sentence. So the question this week asks of every exercise is the one in [What are you learning?](what-are-you-learning.md): when you do the thing, what are you learning, and when Claude does it, what are you learning then? Are you learning to make the thing, or to type the sentence? And if the answer is the sentence, what would it mean to be better at that than the person next to you?

**The unit of practice.** In [Learning](../learning/README.md) the unit is the study, the drill, the étude, the take: a bounded attempt at one thing. Here the unit is the same bounded attempt, with one extra field in the record: who did it. A study in this category is an attempt where the [division of labor](division-of-labor.md) is written down, because that is the part the output hides. A finished cut looks the same whether you made twenty decisions or one. This week has two kinds of study, and they are the two deliverables. The first is a study for yourself: a repeated attempt at something you have decided to keep as your own skill, logged and played back. The second is a [study for an agent](studies-for-an-agent.md): a set of small tries that teach you what to put in an [agent skill](agent-skill.md), a folder of instructions you build for Claude so that a repeated task goes better next time.

**What gets repeated and what varies.** For your own skill, what repeats is the attempt and what varies is one feature at a time, with a prediction attached: this mic position will fix the room sound; this cut will make the point land. For the agent's skill, what repeats is the task and what varies is the instruction: you run the job, watch where it goes wrong, change a line in the [SKILL.md](skill-md.md), run it again on a case you have not used yet. Both are repetition. They improve different things. The first changes you. The second changes a file. The entry on [instructions vs. training](instructions-vs-training.md) is about keeping those apart, because "I taught Claude" can mean four different things and only one of them is learning in the sense the Learning category means.

**Where feedback comes from.** Three places, and you should know which. From the artifact: it works or it doesn't, and in a [harness](harness.md) you can see the tool calls and file changes that got there. From a test: an [evaluation](evaluation.md) for a skill, an unassisted attempt for yourself. And from the model as critic, which is the trickiest, because a model is agreeable by default and its account of why something works is a plausible story, not a diagnosis. The rule of [attempt first](attempt-first.md) protects the second source: if you form your own view before asking, the model's answer is something to compare against instead of something to absorb.

**What can't be drilled.** Judgment about what counts: which continuity error matters in this scene, which of two takes is the true one for the story. [Tacit knowledge](../learning/tacit-knowledge.md) resists being written into a skill file, and the entry on [tacit knowledge and skill files](tacit-knowledge-and-skill-files.md) is about where the writing stops. A skill can say "check continuity." It cannot make you see the mismatch. That capacity is built the old way, by looking at many cases with someone pointing at what to look for, and no amount of delegation produces it, because you are not in the loop when the work happens.

**What AI changes, and what it leaves.** It changes the cost of the output, which is now close to zero, and it changes the visibility of the work, which is now close to zero too. Both matter for learning. The one direct experiment we have, described in [the assistance effect](assistance-effect.md), found that students with an answer-giving model did better during practice and worse on the exam afterward, while a tutor version that withheld answers mostly avoided the harm. That is one study in one subject, and it does not say AI prevents learning. It says the design of the assistance decides what is left for you to do, and what is left is what you learn. Repetition and purposeful practice matter as much as they did; the literature on deliberate practice is disputed about how much of expertise practice explains, but no one in that dispute thinks practice is dispensable. What has changed is that you now have to choose, task by task, which skills stay in you and which go into a file. [Deskilling](deskilling.md) is the older name for that choice being made by someone else. This week it is yours.

So the two deliverables are two answers to the question. For a skill you keep: a log of attempts, with what you predicted, what happened, and what you changed, played back at the end so you can see the change in yourself. For a skill you give to Claude: a working skill folder, the studies that produced it, and a test on a case it has not seen. The point of making both in the same week is that they are not opposites. Writing a procedure down is one of the best ways to find out what you know, and discovering what you could not write down tells you what to practice next.
