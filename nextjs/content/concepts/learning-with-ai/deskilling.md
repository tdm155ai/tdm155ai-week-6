In *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), Harry Braverman described what happened to the machinist when the knowledge of how to cut a part moved out of his hands and into the program of a numerically controlled machine. The trade still existed. The skill had moved: conception was separated from execution, and the person at the machine was left loading stock and pressing start. Braverman called this deskilling, and argued it was the normal direction of work under management that wanted control of the process. The pattern is older than computers: the Jacquard loom took the weaver's pattern knowledge into punched cards.

His thesis has been argued over since. Some trades reskilled; new skills appeared around the machines; the picture was not one-directional. What survives the argument is the structural point: when a machine takes over part of a job, someone decides which human skills are kept and which are allowed to go, and that someone is usually not the worker. The skill does not vanish. It is relocated, into a program, a procedure, a file, where it can be copied and where it no longer belongs to anyone in particular. Tacit knowledge that lived in the hands is the easiest to lose, because nobody wrote it down before it left.

This is the frame for the choice this week asks you to make. A model can take almost any part of a media project. Deskilling is what happens when the split is made by default, by whatever the tool does best. The alternative is to make it on purpose: name the skills you are keeping, practice them, and put the rest into a [skill for Claude](agent-skill.md), where you can at least see it.
