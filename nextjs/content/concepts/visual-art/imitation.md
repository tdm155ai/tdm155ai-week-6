For most of the history of Western art, learning to draw began with copying. A student copied the master's drawings, then prints after famous paintings, then went to the museum and copied the paintings themselves; the Louvre has run a copyist program for centuries, and the easels are still there. Degas copied Italian paintings for years before his own work found its subject. The nineteenth-century academies made copying the first rung of a fixed ladder: copy flat plates, then draw from plaster casts, then from the living model. The logic was that copying put a good solution in front of the hand before the hand could find one on its own.

What copying trains is specific. Copying a drawing teaches you how its maker simplified a form, where the line thickened, what was left out, and it teaches these things through the hand rather than the eye alone, which is why looking at a drawing does not teach what copying it does. What it cannot train is the decision the original artist made before the first line: what to draw, and which relation in it mattered. A copy inherits those decisions. A student who only copies learns to render solutions and not to find problems, and the academic ladder's critics said it produced exactly that. The remedy was never to stop copying but to copy with a question: what is this line doing, and could I do it without the model?

A model trained on millions of images is the most thorough copyist ever built, and asking it for a drawing "in the style of" someone is copying with the hand removed. The question for your study is what, if anything, you took from the copy that would show up in a drawing of your own.
