title: Preparatory drawing

A preparatory drawing is any drawing made on the way to a specific work. For a Renaissance altarpiece the sequence could run from a first thought scribbled in pen, through compositional sketches that set where the figures stand, to studies of individual heads, hands, and drapery, to a more finished drawing of the whole design, and finally to the full-size cartoon that is transferred to the panel. The National Gallery's account of Raphael's *Saint Catherine of Alexandria* traces this chain: sketches that weigh a standing figure against a three-quarter one, a cartoon whose outlines were pricked and pounced onto the panel, and then, under the paint, infrared evidence that the head and some of the drapery were changed after the transfer.

What this sequence teaches is that planning is not a single decision followed by execution. Each drawing fixes some things and leaves others open. A compositional sketch decides where the figure goes and says nothing about the light; a drapery study settles the folds for one sleeve; even the cartoon, the most committed drawing of all, is overruled on the panel. The practice is in learning which decisions belong at which stage and in not forcing a choice the current drawing cannot make. The mistake is to treat an early drawing as a contract. What preparatory drawing cannot do is replace the painting; some problems only appear in paint.

The chain has an echo in working with a model. A rough prompt is a first thought; a reference image is a study; a detailed specification is something like a cartoon, and the generated image still departs from it. The useful question at each step is the same one Raphael faced: what survives the move to the next representation, and what gets lost?
