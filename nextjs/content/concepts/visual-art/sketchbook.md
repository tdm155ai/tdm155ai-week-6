title: Sketchbook
description: The book in which an artist's attempts, observations, and thinking accumulate.

A sketchbook is where the drawings that were never meant to be finished pile up. Leonardo's notebooks are the famous case: thousands of pages in which a drawing of a hand's tendons sits beside a note on water flowing around a rock, a design for a machine, and a reminder to ask someone about the measurements of a bridge. The pages were not made for anyone else. They are the record of a mind working, and because he kept them, we can watch him turn a question over across several sheets, draw the same shoulder from three angles, and reach a conclusion the finished paintings only hint at.

The sketchbook's value as practice is that it keeps the attempts. A single drawing shows what you can do today; a sketchbook shows what changed between March and June, which is the only evidence of learning that drawing offers. It also lowers the price of a bad drawing to nearly nothing, so you draw more. What goes wrong is the sketchbook that becomes precious: once you start making pages for a viewer, it stops being a place to fail and turns into a portfolio. The other failure is the opposite one, a book full of drawings nobody ever looks back through. A record only works if it gets played back.

This week your notebook is the sketchbook for whatever you are making, and it should hold the studies in order, including the ones that did not work and the ones a model made for you, labeled. Looking back through it at the end of the week is the exercise, not an afterthought.
