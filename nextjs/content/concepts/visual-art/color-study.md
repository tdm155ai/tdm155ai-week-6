---
title: Color study
description: An exercise or sketch made to see how colors behave next to each other.
author: claude
order: 14
---

Josef Albers's color course asked students to take the same small square of gray paper and place it on two different colored grounds. On one the gray looks lighter; on the other, darker; on a third it seems to take on a tint it does not have. Nothing about the gray changed. Albers's point, worked through a sequence of such exercises and published with student examples in *Interaction of Color* (1963), is that a color is not a property of a pigment but a relation to its neighbors, and that the only way to learn this is to make the relations and look. The name on the tube stays the same while the thing you see changes, and the exercise exists to make that discrepancy visible.

The older color study is the painter's: a small oil sketch made outdoors to catch the color of a particular light before it moves. John Constable's cloud studies of 1821 and 1822, painted fast with the time and weather noted on the back, are color studies in this sense. What both kinds train is the eye's independence from the name: seeing that the shadow is violet when you know the wall is white. Albers's exercises are a true training sequence, each building on the last, and they work because the comparison is controlled: one variable, two contexts. The outdoor study trains the same eye under the pressure of changing light. Neither trains what to do with the seeing; that is the painting's job.

This is the week's cleanest example of an exercise you cannot delegate. A model can produce a color scheme, but the exercise is in your eye, not the output, and a palette you did not see for yourself is a label, not a color.
