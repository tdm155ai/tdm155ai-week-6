A study is a piece of work made to find something out. Michelangelo's sheet of studies for the Libyan Sibyl, drawn in red chalk around 1510, shows the twisting figure of the Sistine ceiling drawn from a male assistant, and then, smaller, the hand, the foot, and the big toe drawn again. Those corners are the studies in the strict sense: each isolates one relation (how weight passes through the toe, how the hand turns) that the finished fresco has to get right. The word comes from the Latin *studium*, zeal or devoted attention, the same root as the musician's étude and the "studying" you do for an exam, and all three share the idea of attention applied to one thing.

A study is defined by what it does, not how it looks. It can be a quick pencil note or, like Degas's *Study of a Ballet Dancer* on pink paper in oil and watercolor, more worked than many finished pictures. The Lab's working distinction is between four jobs a study can do: prepare a particular work, train a capacity across works, explore a question that has not been posed yet, or record something seen for no project at all. The same sheet can do more than one, so do not read a drawing's purpose off its appearance. What a study trains is the habit of isolating a difficulty and keeping the attempts; what it cannot do is tell you which difficulty matters, which took Michelangelo decades.

This week your studies should start with the question written down: "can I make this hand look like it is holding weight?" and then the attempts, in order. If a model makes the hand, the study is of your prompt, not of your hand, and the record should say so.
