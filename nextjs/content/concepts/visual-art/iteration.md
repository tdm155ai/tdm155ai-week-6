title: Iteration
description: The successive versions of one work, and what each version changed.

In 1935 Matisse had a photographer record his *Large Reclining Nude* at more than twenty stages as he painted it, and the photographs show a figure that starts as a fairly naturalistic woman on a couch and ends as a set of flat shapes, the arm moved, the legs simplified, the grid of the couch redrawn again and again. Two years later Dora Maar photographed Picasso's *Guernica* as it went up, and the sequence shows figures appearing, moving, and disappearing. These are iterations: not studies made beside the work but versions of the work itself, each replacing the last. Without the photographs, only the final state would exist, and the pentimenti under it would be the only clue that there had been others.

Iteration is how a painting is made, and the practice in it is the practice of judging a version: looking at the state the work is in, deciding what is wrong, changing that and only that, and looking again. Each version is an attempt and a piece of feedback at once. What goes wrong is iteration without a reason, change for its own sake until the work is exhausted, and the opposite, where a painter cannot stop because no version is ever the last. Matisse's photographs are useful because they make the reasons visible: you can see which change answered which problem. A sequence of versions with no record of why is just a pile.

This matters for working with a model, where iteration is cheap and constant: a new prompt, a new image, a new prompt. A changed artifact is not evidence that its maker learned anything. Twenty generations show that the model varied; what shows that you learned is the reason written beside each one, and the one you chose.
