---
title: Decomposition
description: Breaking a figure or scene into simple forms that can be drawn one at a time.
author: claude
order: 12
---

A construction drawing starts with no outline at all. The head is a sphere with a jaw hung on it; the rib cage is an egg; the pelvis a box; arms and legs are cylinders joined at balls. Draw those, in perspective, and the figure is already standing in space before a single contour is drawn; the contour is then drawn around the forms rather than guessed. Andrew Loomis's drawing books taught generations of illustrators to work this way, and the method is older: Renaissance drawing manuals reduced the figure to blocks, and Leonardo's anatomical sheets take the body apart into views and layers so that the structure can be understood before the surface is rendered.

Decomposition into forms works because the parts are drawable and the whole is not. Nobody can draw "a man leaning on a doorframe" in one go, but anyone can draw a tilted box, then a cylinder, and the leaning emerges from how the boxes sit. It also makes errors findable: if the figure looks wrong, you can check whether the pelvis box is tilted the right way instead of staring at the whole. What is lost is the particular. A figure built from cylinders is every figure; the thing that made this person recognizable lives in exactly the details the construction left out, and a drawing that stops at construction looks like a mannequin. The forms are scaffolding, and the skill includes taking it down.

Michelangelo's Sibyl sheet is decomposition at another scale: not the figure into forms but the problem into sub-problems, and the toe turned out to be the one that mattered. That is the version of decomposition this week wants from you: not "learn to draw" but "make the weight visible," and then the three studies that would settle it.
