Around 1510 Michelangelo drew a sheet of a young man twisting at the waist, arms raised, and then, in the corners, drew the same figure's hand, foot, and big toe again, smaller. The sheet is a set of studies for the Libyan Sibyl on the Sistine ceiling. The toe matters because the figure's weight runs through it; if the toe is wrong, the whole body floats. That is what visual art's practice looks like at the top of the profession: not a finished picture, but a drawing made to settle one problem before the painting has to.

The unit of practice in this field is the [study](study.md), and the study is a stage in a workflow rather than a separate kind of work. A painter does not drill in the morning and paint in the afternoon. The [sketch](sketch.md) tries an idea; the study works out one part of it; the [cartoon](cartoon.md) carries the settled drawing to the wall; the painting finishes it, and the [pentimenti](pentimento.md) under the paint show that minds kept changing to the end. Drawing is where the thinking happens and painting is where it gets committed, which is why this category treats them as one [workflow](workflow.md), Leonardo's and ours.

What gets repeated is the subject and what varies is the question. Michelangelo draws the hand three times, each time asking something different of it. Monet paints the same grainstacks in different light, and the [series](variation.md) becomes the work. Albers has his students move the same scrap of colored paper from one ground to another until they can see that a color is a relation and not a name. A page of the [same thing drawn again and again](hundred-hands.md) can be a drill, but just as often it is one problem approached from several sides, and you cannot tell which from the page alone. The field's one true drill, the nineteenth-century sequence from copying prints to [plaster casts](cast-drawing.md) to the [live model](life-drawing.md), repeats the task of looking and varies only what is looked at.

Feedback in drawing comes first from the subject. Hold the drawing up beside the hand and the errors are there to see, which makes drawing unusual among the arts: the exercise grades itself, if you can see. The catch is that seeing is the skill being trained, so a beginner cannot yet see the error the drawing shows them. That is what the teacher in the [atelier](apprenticeship.md) adds: a line drawn over yours, a word about where to look. Over the centuries the field built tools to make the comparison honest, from the plumb line and the grid to the cast that holds still, the timed pose that forbids fussing, and the cartoon that can be laid over the painting to check it.

What can't be drilled is the choice of what to study. Nobody can hand you the question. Michelangelo could draw a hand because thirty years of looking had taught him which hand, on which figure, would decide whether the Sibyl held her weight. [Observation](observation.md) can be trained; knowing where to look, the thing Ingold calls an education of attention, is passed on by example and never fully written down. Nor can a drill produce the judgment that a study is finished, or that it is better than the painting it was made for and should be left alone. [Finish](finish.md) is a decision, not a stage.

AI changes the economy of the picture. An image model gives you a finished-looking painting in seconds, and the stages that used to come before it, the sketches and studies and underdrawing, do not have to happen at all. What that skips is not the painting but the looking. A study teaches its maker to see the relation it works out; a generated picture is a surface with no study under it, and the person who prompted it has learned nothing about how a foot bears weight. This is where the week's question lands hardest: when the model paints, what did you practice? Some of the field's tools translate directly. The [model sheet](model-sheet.md) that keeps a Disney character the same across hundreds of drawings is the same problem as keeping an AI character consistent across generated scenes, and the [storyboard](storyboard.md) is still the cheapest way to find out whether a sequence works before anyone makes it. What the model leaves is everything that was never in the paint: the decision what to make a study of, the eye that can tell a plausible hand from a convincing one, and the series of attempts, kept and compared, that is the only evidence that anyone learned anything.

So the invitation for visual art this week is to make studies the old way, with the question stated first and the attempts kept in order, and then to make the same study with a model and put the two side by side. One of them will have taught you to see something. Say which.
