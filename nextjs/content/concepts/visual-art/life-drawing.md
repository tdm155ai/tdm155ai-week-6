title: Life drawing

A life-drawing session has a shape. The model takes a series of short poses first, thirty seconds or a minute each, and the room draws fast; these are croquis, from the French for a quick sketch, and nobody expects more than a scribble with a figure in it. Then five-minute poses, then twenty, and finally a long pose that may hold for the rest of the session or for several sessions, during which the drawing can be built up and corrected. The timer is the teacher's instrument. It sets what kind of drawing is possible and therefore what skill is being worked: speed and the whole figure in the short poses, structure and accuracy in the long one.

What life drawing trains is seeing the body as it is rather than as you think it is: the foreshortened thigh that is shorter than it looks, the head that is smaller than you drew it. The model gives feedback simply by being there, and the pose that ends before you can fix the drawing forces you to get the big shapes first. The failures are familiar: fussing over a face while the figure underneath leans off its feet, or spending a long pose correcting what a thirty-second gesture would have caught. The croquis exists to make the fussing impossible.

Life drawing is the stage the image model skips entirely: it never looks at a body. Its figures have the proportions the training data agreed on, which is why an extra finger or a knee bending the wrong way slips past it. An hour of croquis will not make you draw like the model generates, but it will make you the person in the room who can see that the knee is wrong.
