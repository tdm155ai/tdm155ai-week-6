---
title: Pentimento
description: The underdrawing and the changes of mind that remain visible, or detectable, under a painting's surface.
author: claude
order: 6
---

A pentimento, from the Italian for repentance, is a change of mind preserved under the paint. A painter draws an arm in one position, paints it, decides it should be lower, and paints over it; years later the upper layer thins and the first arm shows through as a ghost, or an infrared camera, which sees through many pigments to the carbon of the underdrawing, reveals it outright. Technical examination of Raphael's *Saint Catherine* finds the pouncing marks of the cartoon and then departures from it in the head and drapery, which means the painter kept deciding after the plan was transferred.

Pentimenti matter to practice because they are the evidence that the finished work was not executed from a settled plan but revised in the making. The underdrawing is the last study and the painting is its iteration, and the two disagree. For a learner, this is permission: the painters whose work you are told to admire changed their minds on the canvas, and their first attempts were wrong in the same ways yours are. It is also a warning about what a finished surface hides. Looking at a polished painting, you see none of this; you have to go beneath the surface, or into the archive of drawings, to find the process. Learning from a finished work alone is studying a black box.

Generated images have no pentimenti. There is no underdrawing beneath a model's output, no record of a mind changing, and a sequence of generations from a revised prompt is the only trace of the process. That is why keeping your versions, with the reason for each change, is the modern equivalent of the layer under the paint.
