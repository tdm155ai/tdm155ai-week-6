A Renaissance painting was made in stages, and the drawings that survive let us read them. First the sketch, testing where the figures go; then studies of the parts that would be hard, a hand, a fall of drapery, a foot bearing weight; then a drawing of the whole design, and the full-size cartoon; then the transfer to the panel, the underdrawing, the paint, and the changes of mind that go on under the paint until the end. Leonardo adds a stage before all of these, the notebook, in which the question is found. Raphael's *Saint Catherine* was changed after its cartoon; Michelangelo went back to the toe.

Thinking of drawing and painting as one workflow rather than two arts changes what practice means. Each stage has its own unit of practice and its own question: the sketch asks "where?", the study asks "how?", the cartoon asks "exactly?", the painting asks "is it done?". A painter practices each by making many of it, and the workflow is the frame that tells you which stage you are at and so which kind of attempt you should be making. What goes wrong is confusing the stages: polishing a sketch, painting before the studies have settled the hard part, or treating the cartoon as a contract. And a representation changes at every handoff; the drapery study does not reach the painting unchanged.

Today's workflow runs mood board, thumbnails, reference images, studies, rough, clean, color, and a model can be inserted at any stage. The useful question is which. A model that makes the thumbnails leaves you the studies; a model that makes the finish leaves you the thinking. Put it where it takes the work you have already learned, and keep the stages where the learning still is.
