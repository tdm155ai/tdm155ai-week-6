title: Gesture
description: The whole movement of a figure caught in a few lines before any detail.
author: claude

A gesture drawing is the figure's action in a handful of lines: the curve of the spine, the lean of the weight, the swing of the arms, drawn in thirty seconds with no outline, no face, and no attempt at proportion. Kimon Nicolaïdes, whose *The Natural Way to Draw* (1941) built a whole course on it, told students to draw not what the figure looks like but what it is doing, and to keep the pencil moving the whole time. A good gesture drawing of a person stooping to lift a box reads as stooping and lifting even though it has no hands and no box.

Gesture trains the first decision in any figure drawing, the one that cannot be fixed later: what the whole body is doing. A drawing with a well-rendered head on a figure whose weight is not on its feet is wrong in a way no detail can repair, and gesture practice teaches you to get that right before you earn the right to draw a face. The time limit is what trains it; given five minutes you draw contours, given thirty seconds you have to draw the action. What gesture cannot train is accuracy, structure, or finish, which is why it opens a session and does not fill it. Its failure mode is the opposite of the cast's: the student who never moves past gesture produces lively scribbles with nothing holding them up.

Gesture is also a good way to think about what a generated image gets right and wrong. A model renders surfaces well and action less reliably: the figure is finished down to the eyelashes and the pose does not quite read. Thirty seconds of gesture over a generated image will tell you whether the body is doing anything.
