A model sheet is a reference drawing of a character: the figure from the front, side, and back at the same scale, a row of expressions, a few typical poses, often with notes on proportion ("three and a half heads tall"). At the Disney studio in the 1930s and after, artists drew a new character in dozens of exploratory poses and moods, and a selection of those drawings, approved by Joe Grant's model department, became the official sheet that every animator on the film worked from. The sheet is what lets two hundred people draw the same mouse and have it come out as one mouse.

The model sheet is the point where exploration becomes a standard. The drawings before it are studies in the full sense, asking what this character is like; the sheet records the answers and stops the asking, which is its job and its cost. What it trains, for the artist who makes it, is the ability to say what makes a figure itself: which proportions, which line of the brow, cannot change without the character becoming someone else. What it trains for everyone downstream is consistency, drawing from a reference rather than from invention. What it cannot do is animate. A sheet of poses is not a performance, and the animator still has to find the movement between them. Model sheet, study, and storyboard are three different jobs that one drawing can sometimes do; do not read the job off the look.

This is the course's own problem in its oldest form. Keeping an AI character the same across generated images is a model-sheet problem, and the sheet you make, reference images plus the written list of what must not change, is the part of the work that is yours.
