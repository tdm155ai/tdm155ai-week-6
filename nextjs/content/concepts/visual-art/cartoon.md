title: Cartoon
description: The full-size drawing made to transfer a finished design onto the wall, panel, or canvas.
author: claude

---

Before the word meant a funny drawing, a cartoon was a full-size working drawing, from the Italian *cartone*, a heavy sheet of paper. A fresco painter who had settled the design in small sketches and studies drew it again at the size of the wall, then transferred it: either by pricking holes along the outlines and dusting charcoal through them (pouncing), or by going over the lines with a stylus hard enough to score the wet plaster. The cartoon for Raphael's *Saint Catherine* at the National Gallery still carries the pricked outlines, and infrared examination of the painting finds the transfer marks below the paint. Leonardo's cartoon of the Virgin and Child with Saint Anne, also at the National Gallery, is a cartoon that was never transferred and survives as a work in its own right.

The cartoon is the last drawing before the irreversible stage, and its practice is the practice of commitment: everything movable has been moved, and the design is now a thing that can be handed to an assistant to transfer. That makes it the point in the workflow where the painter's thinking becomes a document someone else can execute, which is why it was also where workshop labor entered. The cartoon's limit is that it fixes only outlines. Color, modeling, and the final expression of a face are still open, and the Raphael painting differs from its cartoon in the head and the drapery.

For anyone making media with a model, the cartoon is the closest ancestor of the detailed specification: the point where your intentions are written out fully enough that another agent can act on them. It is worth asking what your cartoon fixes and what it leaves to the hand, or the model, that does the transfer.
