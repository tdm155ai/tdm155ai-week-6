---
title: Observation
description: Drawing as a way of seeing, and seeing as the skill that drawing trains.
author: claude
order: 11
---

Ask someone to draw their own hand and the drawing will show a hand they have never looked at: fingers of equal length, a palm as wide as it is long, a thumb attached in the wrong place. What was missing was not skill with the pencil but the ability to see what was there instead of what they knew. Drawing teachers have long said this; John Ruskin, in *The Elements of Drawing* (1857), wanted his students to recover what he called the innocence of the eye, seeing patches of color and shape before naming them as a tree or a face. Leonardo's anatomical drawings go the other way, through the surface to the structure, but the discipline is the same: look until the thing is seen.

Observation is a skill in the week's sense, a trained capacity to notice differences that matter, and drawing is its most direct exercise because the drawing shows you exactly what you did not see. The feedback is immediate and unforgiving: hold the drawing beside the hand. The trouble is that a beginner cannot yet see the error the comparison shows, which is why teachers correct with a line over the student's work and why so many drawing exercises are tricks to defeat naming: copy the photograph upside down, draw only the spaces between the chair's legs, draw the contour without looking at the paper. What goes wrong is substituting knowledge for sight, the symbol of an eye for this eye.

Observation is exactly what a generated image does not require of its maker. You can get a convincing hand without ever having looked at one. That is the trade the week asks you to notice: the picture arrives, and the eye that a study would have trained does not.
