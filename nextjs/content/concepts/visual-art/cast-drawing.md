---
title: Cast drawing
description: Drawing from plaster casts of sculpture, the academic step between copying plates and drawing the living model.
author: claude
order: 8
---

In a nineteenth-century academy, a student who had finished copying printed plates was set in front of a plaster cast: a foot, a hand, a head of a Greek statue, finally a whole figure, lit from one side and painted white so that nothing but form and shadow remained. The student drew it, often for weeks on one drawing, in charcoal, until the drawing matched the cast under the teacher's eye. Only then came the live model. Charles Bargue's drawing course, published with Jean-Léon Gérôme in the 1860s and 1870s and still used in some ateliers, lays out the ladder: plates, then casts, then life.

The cast is a drill in the strict sense, and its design is clever. It removes everything that makes a living body hard: it does not move, breathe, or tire; it has no color; its shadows do not shift as the afternoon goes on. What is left is the one skill the sequence wants to isolate, seeing the shape of a shadow and putting it in the right place with the right value. Hours on a cast train that skill as nothing else does. What the cast cannot train is everything it removed: movement, color, the way flesh differs from plaster, and the judgment of what to draw, since the teacher chose the cast. Students who stayed too long with casts drew living people as if they were marble, which was the standard complaint.

The cast is also the clearest model of good decomposition: a skill isolated by changing the subject, not by shortening the task. When you plan a study this week, ask what your cast is, the version of the problem with the distractions removed, and what you will have to add back afterward.
