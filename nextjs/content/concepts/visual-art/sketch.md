---
title: Sketch
description: A fast, exploratory drawing that is cheap enough to throw away.
author: claude
order: 2
---

A sketch is a drawing made quickly to try something, with no obligation to keep it. A painter planning a crowded scene might fill a page with twenty thumbnails, each the size of a postage stamp, moving the figures around until one arrangement holds. None of the twenty is a picture; together they are a search. Raphael's early sketches for his *Saint Catherine* consider a full-length figure before he settles on the three-quarter view the painting uses, and that abandoned option is the sketch doing its job: a decision made on paper for the price of a few lines instead of on a panel for the price of weeks.

The sketch differs from the study in what it is for. A study concentrates on one problem and works it through; a sketch opens options and discards most of them. Speed is the point, and so is cheapness: a sketch that cost too much to throw away stops being one. What sketching trains is fluency, the ability to put an idea down before it fades, and the habit of making many before choosing. What it does not train is accuracy. A sketch lets you skip what you cannot yet draw, and a sketchbook full of them can hide the thing you never learned, which is why the thumbnail page and the study sheet need each other.

With an image model the sketch is the easiest stage to reproduce: ask for twelve variations and you have a page of thumbnails in a minute. That is a fine way to search for a composition. It is not sketching, because the hand that would have learned where the figures go never moved. Keep the model's variations as what they are, a search you directed, and keep your own thumbnails beside them.
