title: Finish
description: The difference between a study and a finished work, and why the study is sometimes the better picture.

Seurat's *Study for "A Sunday on La Grande Jatte"* (1884), at the Metropolitan Museum, is the last of the studies for the large painting in Chicago, and the two are not the same picture at different sizes. The study is painted in patchy, open strokes; the finished work in the tight, dot-like touches Seurat called Divisionism. The study decided the composition and the light; the finished painting decided the surface. Many viewers prefer the study, and the same is said of Constable's full-size oil sketches for his large landscapes, which are looser and more alive than the paintings he exhibited. In the academic painting of the nineteenth century, "finish" meant a smooth, licked surface with every stroke hidden, and the Impressionists' refusal of it scandalized the critics.

Finish is a decision about what a work is for, and that is why it belongs in a reference on practice. A study is finished when it has answered its question; a painting is finished when it is ready for its audience; and the two can come apart, so that a study that answered everything is a better picture than the painting that was built on it. The practice is in knowing which you are making and when to stop. What goes wrong is finishing a study, polishing a drawing that was only meant to settle the pose, which wastes hours and often kills what worked. The opposite error is mistaking looseness for inquiry: a careless picture is not a study, and Degas's dancer on pink paper is a study despite being more worked than most paintings.

Generated images arrive finished. That is their most misleading quality for a learner: the polish suggests that a question was answered when none was asked. Ask of any picture, yours or the model's, what it decided.
