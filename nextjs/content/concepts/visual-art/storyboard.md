title: Storyboard
description: Sequential sketches that work out a sequence of events, shots, and staging before anything is made.

A storyboard is a row of small drawings, one per shot or moment, pinned up in order so that a sequence can be read like a comic. The form is usually credited to the Disney studio in the early 1930s, where story artists pinned sketches to corkboards and moved them around; the studio's story process today still describes thumbnails becoming refined drawings, with the artists thinking through expression, timing, staging, and framing as they draw. A storyboard for a chase might be thirty panels: the car, the turn, the close-up of the driver's eyes, the truck pulling out. Each panel is a sketch, and the board is where the sketches become a sequence.

What is easy to miss is that the storyboard is exploratory, not an illustration of decisions already made. Drawing the sequence is how you find out that the chase has no moment where the audience knows where the truck is, and panels get torn down and redrawn. As practice, storyboarding trains the specifically sequential skills, when to cut, what the viewer needs to see next, how long a beat lasts, that a single drawing cannot. What it cannot settle is duration and rhythm, which is why the next stage is the animatic, the board timed and cut to sound. Its failure mode is the beautiful board: panels so finished that nobody wants to move them, which defeats the point. A storyboard that cannot be rearranged is a set of illustrations.

For generated video, the storyboard is the stage that survives untouched. A model can render a shot but it cannot tell you what the next shot should be, and a board of rough panels remains the cheapest way to find out whether a sequence works before you spend a hundred generations on it.
