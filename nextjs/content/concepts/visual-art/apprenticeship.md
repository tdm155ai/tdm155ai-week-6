title: Apprenticeship

A boy entering a Florentine painter's workshop around 1400 did not start by painting. Cennino Cennini's *Il Libro dell'Arte*, a handbook written by a painter in that tradition, lays out the years: grinding pigments, preparing panels with gesso, learning to draw by copying the master's drawings, then drawing from nature, then being trusted with backgrounds and drapery in the master's pictures, and only after many years with the figures that carried the master's name. Leonardo learned this way in Verrocchio's shop, and the altarpieces of the period are workshop products, with the master designing, correcting, and painting the parts that mattered while assistants did the rest. The nineteenth-century atelier kept the structure with the labor removed: students drew under a master who came through to correct.

Apprenticeship works through proximity and tasks. The learner sees the whole process daily, including the parts nobody writes down (how thick the gesso goes on, when a drawing is finished), and is given work that is slightly beyond them, corrected on the spot, and then given more. The grinding is not a hazing; it is years of handling the materials before being asked to make decisions with them. What it cannot provide is independence: the apprentice learns the master's way, and the history of art is partly the history of apprentices leaving. It is also worth remembering that the workshop ran on labor and authority, and that who got in, and who got credited, was a matter of class, money, and gender, not talent alone.

Working with an agent reverses the roles: you are the master handing off the drapery, and the question is whether you know enough about drapery to correct it. An apprentice who never ground pigment could not run a shop.
