title: Editing
description: Working on a piece as its reader rather than its writer; cutting, ordering, and correcting as a skill separate from composing.
author: claude

---

Editing is working on a piece from the reader's side: cutting what doesn't earn its place, moving what's out of order, fixing what's wrong. Publishing splits it into levels. Developmental editing asks whether this is the right piece in the right shape; line editing asks whether each sentence does its work; copyediting asks whether it is correct and consistent; proofreading catches errors in the final. A writer editing their own draft cuts the first paragraph, which was a warm-up; shortens a sentence that was showing off; and catches that a name is spelled two ways. Editing and writing use different attention, which is why they are separate jobs anywhere that can afford two people.

What editing trains is the reader's eye, and it's learnable apart from writing, which is the useful fact: some excellent editors are ordinary writers, and the reverse is common. Editing your own work takes a trick, because you read what you meant rather than what's there. Time helps (put the draft away); so does reading aloud; so does changing the font. The failure modes are two. Editing too early kills the draft before it exists, which is what freewriting is designed against. Editing too lightly, polishing sentences while leaving the structure, produces a clean version of a broken piece. Editing follows revision: the developmental questions come before the commas.

Models edit well at the lower levels and quickly at the higher ones, and this is the one place in writing where delegating is often the right call, provided you can judge the result. The skill to keep is the judgment. Give the model a draft and ask for its cuts, then decide each one yourself, and keep the ones you refused along with the reason. Someone who can only accept edits has not learned to edit.
