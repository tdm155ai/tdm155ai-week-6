The writing workshop is a class in which the students' drafts are the text. A piece is circulated ahead of time; the group reads it; in the room, the readers say what they experienced, where they were confused, where they stopped believing, where they wanted more, and the writer, by the usual rule, stays silent until the end. The form was institutionalized at the Iowa Writers' Workshop, founded in 1936, and has been copied into most creative-writing programs since. Its one premise is that a writer can't see their own draft and a room of attentive readers can.

What the workshop gives is the gap between intention and reception. You meant the opening to be ominous; four readers found it confusing; that difference is the most useful information a writer gets, and it is unavailable alone. The failure modes are familiar to anyone who has sat in one. Vague praise ("I liked the voice") tells the writer nothing. Prescriptive rewriting ("I'd start with the second paragraph") tells the writer what the reader would have written. The report of experience, "I lost track of who was speaking here," is the useful kind, and staying in it is the reader's job. The writer's job is harder: separating the readers' diagnosis, which is usually right, from their proposed cure, which often isn't. Musicians have the master class and photographers the critique; the workshop is the same institution with the artifact on paper.

A model can play a workshop reader, and will, if asked for experience rather than a rewrite: where did you lose the thread, what did you expect after the first paragraph. It is one reader with no body in the room, and it can't tell you what a human audience felt. Use it before the workshop, not instead of it, and keep its notes separate from the readers'.
