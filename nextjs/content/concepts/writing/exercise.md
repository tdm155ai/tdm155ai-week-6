---
title: Exercise
description: A short writing task built around one move, done so the move can be practiced apart from a whole piece.
author: claude
order: 1
---

A writing exercise isolates one move and asks you to make it several times. Describe the same kitchen three times: from the doorway, from the floor, and from the point of view of someone who hates it. Write a paragraph with no adjectives. Take a forty-word sentence and split it into three; then take three short sentences and join them into one that still breathes. None of these is a piece anyone will read. Each trains something you need in every piece: control of distance, of modification, of sentence length. The word comes from the physical sense, and the comparison holds: a musician plays scales, a writer writes the same paragraph five ways, and neither is the performance.

The exercise works because it removes everything except the move. In a whole essay, a weak transition hides among forty other decisions; in an exercise built around transitions, it has nowhere to hide. That is also its blind spot. Doing the move well in isolation doesn't guarantee doing it when it has to fit everything else, and students who only do exercises can write excellent sentences and dead pages. The research on [part and whole practice](../learning/part-whole-practice.md) says to alternate: isolate the move, then put it back into a piece and see whether it survives. The other failure is compliance without purpose, a piece that meets the exercise's terms and says nothing.

This week, take the move from your own project rather than a textbook. If your voiceover keeps explaining what the picture already shows, the exercise is three voiceover lines for one shot, each telling the viewer something the shot can't. If you work with a model, give it the exercise after you've done it and compare the moves, not the wording; a model completes any exercise fluently, and fluency was not what you were training.
