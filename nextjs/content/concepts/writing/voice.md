---
title: Voice
description: The quality that makes a page recognizably one writer's; what practice is supposed to produce and can't drill directly.
author: claude
order: 16
---

Voice is the word writers use for whatever makes a page sound like one person and not another. Read two paragraphs about the same event by two good writers and you can tell them apart without a byline: from the rhythm, the length of the sentences, what gets noticed, what gets left out, how the writer treats the reader. It is the hardest term in this category to define and the easiest to recognize, and it is what the exercises are for: nobody wants to write transitions well as an end in itself.

Voice can't be drilled, and that is the thing to know before trying. Every other entry here trains a part (a move, a form, the capacity to produce, the capacity to cut), and voice is not a part. Writers who practice voice directly end up with mannerisms, a style assembled from the outside. What seems to produce it, as far as anyone can say, is years of choices made under feedback, a great deal of reading, and having something to say; the exercises make it possible, and then it arrives or doesn't. Stevenson, who analyzed prose technique as closely as anyone, warned that the analysis never accounts for the effect. Voice is also where tacit knowledge lives: a writer with a strong voice usually can't tell you the rules, because there are none to tell.

A model has a voice too, a fluent average, and it imitates other voices on request, including yours given enough pages. The risk for a student is not that the model will steal a voice but that drafting with it for a term will leave no room for one to form, since voice comes from the choices made in drafting and revising, the parts being delegated. The protection is plain: a share of your writing that is yours from the first word, kept, dated, and reread.
