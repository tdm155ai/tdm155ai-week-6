An outline is the piece reduced to its parts and their order: a list of what each section has to do, before any of it is written. For a 1,200-word story that may be six lines: open on the scene; the problem; who it affects; what has been tried; the complication; where it stands now. The outline makes arrangement a decision separate from sentence-making, and arrangement is a skill of its own. Classical rhetoric listed it, as *dispositio*, second among five canons, after finding the material and before putting it into words. Franklin practiced it deliberately: he scrambled his notes on a *Spectator* essay and, weeks later, tried to put them back in the best order before writing, then compared his order with the original's.

What outlining trains is the habit of seeing a piece as parts in relation, which is where most structural problems live and where sentence-level revision can't reach. It is the writer's decomposition, the cousin of the film's shot list and the actor's beats. What it can't do is tell you what you think. Outlines made before the first draft are often wrong, because the draft is where the subject gets discovered, and a writer who obeys the outline against the draft's evidence ends up with a tidy piece about the wrong thing. The reverse outline, made from a finished draft by writing one line per paragraph saying what it does, is often the more useful tool: it shows what the draft's order is, as against what you intended.

A model outlines well and will reorder anything you give it. The study that keeps the skill yours: outline first, draft, then reverse-outline the draft and compare the two; then ask the model for its reordering and judge it against both.
