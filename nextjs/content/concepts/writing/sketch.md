In writing, a sketch is a short, loose piece made fast and without much plan: a scene, a character seen once, a place at a particular hour. The word was borrowed from drawing, and the nineteenth century used it as a genre name; Dickens's first book was *Sketches by Boz*, Washington Irving published a *Sketch Book*, and Turgenev's *Sketches from a Hunter's Album* are short pieces of observed country life. A sketch of a person in a coffee shop, written in ten minutes, is not a story. It's a test of whether that person could carry one. The looseness is the feature: a sketch is cheap enough to throw away, so you can make several and keep the one with life in it.

The sketch trains two things, speed of observation and the willingness to commit words to a page before you know what they're for. What it doesn't train is structure or finish. A notebook full of sketches is not a draft, and the writer who only sketches never finds out whether a thing holds at length. The failure from the other side is judging every sketch as if it were finished and abandoning subjects that needed a second pass. Sketch and study differ in what they ask: a sketch asks whether there is something here; a study asks how a particular effect is made.

This week, sketch before you prompt. Three ten-minute sketches of one scene from your project (one in the present tense, one from another person's position, one that's mostly dialogue) will tell you which version is worth developing, and that choice is yours to make. A model can produce three variants in the time it takes to ask; the question is whether you would have known which one to keep.
