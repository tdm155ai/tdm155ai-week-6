In 1580 Michel de Montaigne published a book of prose pieces he called *Essais*, from the French *essayer*: to try, to test, to attempt. The word was his claim about what the pieces were: not treatises that settle a question but trials of his own mind on a subject (on friendship, on cannibals, on thumbs, on experience), following his thought where it went and reporting what he found, doubts included. Francis Bacon borrowed the title for English in 1597, and the form has carried the sense of a try ever since, underneath the school assignment that kept the name and lost the meaning. An essay in Montaigne's sense doesn't know its conclusion when it begins.

That makes the essay a study: a bounded piece made to find something out, where the finding-out is visible on the page. What it trains is thinking in writing, holding a question open for two thousand words and letting the sentences do the work of discovery. It is the opposite of the five-paragraph essay, which asks you to know the thesis first and prove it. What the form can't do is give you a subject or a reason to care about it, and its failure mode is the ramble that mistakes having no plan for having an open mind. Montaigne's essays wander and still arrive somewhere, and he kept revising them for the rest of his life, which is its own lesson about iteration.

A model will write an essay on any topic, and what it writes is the school version: a thesis, supported, concluded. The attempt is the part it can't do for you, because the attempt is your mind on the subject. Try one this week, on something in your project you don't understand yet, under one rule: you can't know the last paragraph when you write the first.
