title: Copywork
description: Copying out sentences by hand, word for word, to feel their rhythm and construction from the inside.

Copywork is the oldest writing exercise there is: you take a passage you admire and copy it out, by hand, word for word. Nothing is invented. Students learned their letters this way and scribes their trade, and writers still do it, often without telling anyone. Copy a paragraph by a writer whose sentences you envy and you find out things no reading reveals: that the long sentence is held up by a run of short clauses, that the paragraph turns on a one-word sentence in the middle, that the writer never uses the word you'd have reached for. Your hand is slower than your eye, and the slowness is the point. At the speed of handwriting, every comma is a decision you have to make again.

What copywork trains is attention. It is the writer's version of the painter's copy after a master or the jazz musician's transcription of a solo. What it can't train is anything of your own; a copy is a copy. Its failure mode is the obvious one: the hand keeps moving, the attention leaves, and you have produced a transcription without noticing a single choice. The remedy is a note beside the copy, one line per passage saying what you saw. If the note is empty, the copying was mechanical.

There is no version of copywork a model can do for you, which makes it an unusually clean study this week. Typing is faster than handwriting and loses some of the effect; dictating loses all of it. Copy a paragraph from a writer in the form you're working in (a script, a caption, a voiceover) and write your one line. Then, if you want the comparison, ask the model to describe the passage's construction and see which of its observations you had already made by hand.
