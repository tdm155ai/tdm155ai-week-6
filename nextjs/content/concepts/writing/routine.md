title: Routine

Routine is the decision to write every day, usually at the same time and for the same stretch, whether or not anything is ready to be written. Julia Cameron's morning pages in *The Artist's Way*, three handwritten pages first thing, are the best-known version; many writers describe a daily quota of words or hours and treat it as the job. A student who writes for twenty minutes every morning before class, on anything, has a routine. The reason it works is unglamorous: it removes the decision about whether to write today, and that decision was the thing getting skipped.

What routine trains is showing up, and it supplies what every other practice in this category needs: time distributed over days rather than crammed into one night, which the learning research on spacing favors for retention. What it doesn't do is make the writing better by itself. A routine is a container, and the container can hold the same mistakes for years; writers who log their hours sometimes mistake the log for progress. The distinction to hold is between the session and the learning. A routine guarantees sessions, and a session is not proof that anything changed. The remedy is to put a target inside the routine, one move to work on this week, and to check it against something beyond the hours: a reader, a comparison, a reread of last month's pages.

Routine is a part of practice a model can't supply and can quietly erode. If the daily writing becomes daily prompting, the sessions continue and the practice stops. Keep one daily stretch that is unassisted, and note in the log which stretches were which.
