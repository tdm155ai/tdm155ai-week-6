title: Revision
description: Changing a draft for a reason; rewriting as the main act of writing, not the cleanup after it.
author: claude

---

Revision is changing a draft because of something you noticed: the opening promises an argument the piece never makes, the third paragraph belongs first, the quotation is doing work the prose should do. Re-vision, seeing again. Most experienced writers say rewriting is most of writing and the first draft is the material for it. A student who writes a paragraph, reads it aloud, hears the rhythm working against the meaning, and rewrites two sentences so the stress lands where the idea is, has revised. A student who runs a spell-checker has not; that's proofreading, a different and smaller act.

What revision trains is the link between a reason and a change. The evidence worth keeping is three things: the version before, the version after, and the stated reason. Without the reason, "better" is an assertion. The habit from the learning research that fits revision is prediction: before you revise, write what you expect the change to do ("this should make the speaker's point clearer because..."), then compare the two versions and check whether it did. Failed predictions are the most useful entries in the record, since they show a criterion you held that turned out to be wrong. What revision can't do is rescue a draft with nothing in it, and its failure mode is cosmetic: moving words around while leaving the structure, which is usually where the problem is, untouched.

A model is a willing reviser and will rewrite anything you hand it, which makes the reason the thing to protect. Ask it for a diagnosis rather than a rewrite: what is this paragraph trying to do, and where does it fail? Then make the change yourself and keep before, after, and reason. If you take the model's rewrite, write down why, and whether your prediction about it held.
