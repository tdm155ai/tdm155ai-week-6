Benjamin Franklin, a teenager who wanted to write better, found a volume of the *Spectator*, Addison and Steele's essay paper, and made it his teacher. His *Autobiography* describes the method. He took short notes on the sense of each sentence in an essay, put the book away for a few days, then tried to write the essay again from his notes in his own words. He compared his version with the original, found his faults, and corrected them. To enlarge his stock of words he turned the essays into verse and back; to practice arrangement he scrambled his notes and tried to restore the order. That is imitation in the writer's sense: not copying words but rebuilding the thing, so that the gap between your rebuild and the model shows you what you don't yet know how to do.

Pastiche is the looser form: a page in the manner of Hemingway or Didion or a legal brief, written to find out from the inside what makes that manner. What imitation trains is the perception of choices; you can't write like Didion without noticing what she does with a list. What it can't train is the judgment of when to use it, and its known failure is surface mannerism, the short sentences acquired without the thing they were for. Two cautions: Franklin's account is his own, written decades later, not a controlled comparison; and the original is not the only right way to write the essay. A rebuild that differs from the model is sometimes an improvement.

Franklin's gap, between seeing a fluent model and generating a new instance, is the useful design, and a model closes it by producing the pastiche instantly. Keep it open: take notes on a paragraph you admire, put it away, rebuild it around a different example, compare, and only then ask Claude what structural difference it sees, with your own comparison already written down.
