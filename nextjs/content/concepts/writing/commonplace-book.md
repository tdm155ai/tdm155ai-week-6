title: Commonplace book

A commonplace book is a notebook for things found: passages copied from reading, overheard sentences, a word you want to use, a structural question you don't yet have an answer to, an opening you threw away. The form is old. Renaissance students kept them under headings so that a passage on friendship could be found when needed, and John Locke published a method for indexing one. Writers' notebooks are the loose modern version: Whitman's held trial lines, lists, and notes toward an American dictionary, and Virginia Woolf kept reading notebooks beside her own writing. The point is not storage. Collecting makes you choose, and choosing is attention; you can't copy a sentence into a notebook without deciding it was worth the copying.

What the commonplace book trains is noticing, and over months it becomes a record of what you noticed, which is a record of how your perception changed. It holds small units that aren't ready to be anything and lets them exist without proving progress. What it can't do is make you use them. The known failure is the pile of quotations never revisited, a notebook that is write-only, and the remedy is a habit of return: reread last month's entries and mark what's still alive, a kind of retrieval practice. An entry works better with a line of your own beside the passage, what it does or what you want to try. Without that, the notebook shows that you opened the book, not what you saw.

A model can organize your notebook, summarize it, and search it, and a tidy summary is a different object from your encounter with a passage. Keep the original entry in your words, and let the model work on top of it rather than instead of it.
