Writing is the field where the week's question is hardest to dodge. A model can produce a paragraph on any subject in a second, and the paragraph will be fluent. So what are you practicing when you write one yourself, and what are you practicing when you ask for one? Writers have been working on the first question for a long time, and their answers come in small units.

**The unit of practice.** Writers have never settled on one unit, which is itself informative. The [exercise](exercise.md) is the smallest: one move, isolated and repeated, like describing the same room three times with the narrator standing somewhere different each time. The [draft](draft.md) is the most common, because almost no piece of writing arrives in one pass; the draft is the whole attempt that exists so it can be changed. The [sketch](sketch.md) is the loose, fast version that tests whether a subject has anything in it. The [study](study.md) sits between exercise and finished piece: a bounded piece made to work out one effect, that can still be read as writing. Benjamin Franklin's account of teaching himself prose uses nearly all of these at once. He took short notes on an essay from the *Spectator*, waited some days, rewrote the essay from the notes, and compared his version with the original to find his faults. Then he scrambled the notes and tried to restore their order, to practice arrangement; then he turned prose into verse and back, to enlarge his stock of words. Each is a study with a target, and his *Autobiography* is a writer's account of his own training rather than a controlled experiment, which is worth keeping in mind whenever it gets quoted.

**What is repeated and what varies.** In writing, what gets repeated is almost never the words. [Copywork](copywork.md) is the exception, and even there the point is to notice the rhythm, not to reproduce it. What repeats is the move: the opening, the transition, the integrated quotation, the sentence that has to carry two ideas without a comma splice. What varies is the material. A good writing exercise keeps the move fixed and changes the subject, or keeps the subject fixed and changes the move, which is what the [constraints](constraints.md) of Raymond Queneau's *Exercises in Style* do, telling one trivial anecdote ninety-nine ways. The October project in this course loosely follows the old rhetorical sequence of invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery, and the writing studies of this week sit mostly in the first three: collecting material, ordering it, and working a sentence until it does what you want.

**Where feedback comes from.** Three places, in roughly the order a writer meets them. Rereading is the first and the one you control: putting the draft away and reading it later, or aloud, so that it arrives as a reader's experience and not as your memory of what you meant. The [workshop](workshop.md) is the second, and the institution the field built for it: a room of readers who tell you what they experienced, which is different from what you intended. The editor is the third, the professional reader whose job is to see what the writer can't. All three are reports of a reader's experience. The feedback that helps names where the reader was lost or bored; the feedback that doesn't is praise, or a rewrite in someone else's voice. The habit worth borrowing from the learning research is to predict before you reread: write down what you think the paragraph does, then check, and keep the predictions that failed, since they show which of your criteria was wrong.

**What can't be drilled.** [Voice](voice.md), or whatever the word is for the thing that makes a page recognizably one person's. Every exercise above trains a part. None of them produces the whole, and writers who try to drill their way to a voice usually end up with mannerisms. Voice seems to come from the accumulation of choices made under feedback over years, from reading, and from having something to say; the exercises make it possible, they don't cause it. Robert Louis Stevenson, who wrote some of the closest technical descriptions of prose we have, also warned that the analysis never accounts for the effect. Keep both halves of that.

**What AI changes and what it leaves.** The part of writing most easily delegated to a model is drafting, and that is the problem, because drafting is where most of the learning has been. The first draft is where you discover you don't know what you think; the second is where you find out. If a model writes the first draft, you skip the discovery and arrive at revision with nothing of your own to revise toward. The one field experiment this week leans on, the [assistance effect](../learning-with-ai/assistance-effect.md), in which students with unrestricted GPT help did better on practice and worse on the unassisted exam, comes from high-school mathematics, not writing, but the mechanism it suggests is the one writers would predict. What a model leaves to you, and can help with, is the rest. It can generate twenty openings for a paragraph you have already drafted, so you can see what yours is doing; it can play a merciless workshop reader if you ask for experience rather than a rewrite; it can set a constraint and check that you kept it; it can propose a cut and let you argue. All of those keep the draft yours. The rule for this week's writing studies is the rule for the course: attempt first, then compare. Write the paragraph, then ask for the model's version, then write down what the comparison showed you. That record is the evidence that you, and not the model, learned something.

Sources: Benjamin Franklin, *Autobiography*; Raymond Queneau, *Exercises in Style*; Robert Louis Stevenson, *Essays in the Art of Writing*.
