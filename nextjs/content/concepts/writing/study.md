title: Study
description: A bounded piece of writing made to work out one effect or operation, that can still be read as writing.

A writing study is a short piece made to work something out: how far a narrator can stand from the events, how much explanation a quotation needs around it, what happens to a paragraph when its information arrives in a different order. Take one interview excerpt and write three paragraphs around the same quotation: in the first, explain too little; in the second, too much; in the third, find the balance. None of those is the article. Each is a study, and the three together show you what the surrounding prose contributes, which you would never see from writing the balanced version once. The word carries its history: Latin *studium*, zeal, devoted application, the root shared by the painter's study, the musician's étude, and studying for an exam.

The definition is a proposal for this week rather than a term writers have always used this way; writers more often say exercise, draft, or sketch. What the study adds is a stated question. An exercise trains a move, a sketch tests a subject, and a study investigates an effect; it isn't finished until you can say what you found. That requirement is what makes it useful as evidence. A study with its question at the top, its attempts, and a sentence about what changed is a record of learning in a way a polished paragraph is not. A fine paragraph can be made without anyone learning anything, and an awkward study can be the opposite.

Pick a study from your October project. Three openings for one story (begin with a person, with a scene, with a claim), and before each one, predict what it will make a reader expect; then ask two readers what they expected. The model's turn comes after: once your three exist, ask it for a fourth and compare the functions, not the sentences.
