A constraint is a rule you write under: no letter e, fourteen lines, exactly a hundred words, every sentence a question. The Oulipo, the Paris group of writers and mathematicians founded in 1960, made constraints the whole method. Georges Perec wrote a novel, *La Disparition*, without the letter e; Raymond Queneau's *Exercises in Style* tells one trivial anecdote, a man on a bus and a button on his coat, ninety-nine times in ninety-nine manners. The second is the better model for a writing study, because it holds the material fixed and varies the move, which shows what each manner does. A sonnet is a constraint; so is a caption limit; so is a voiceover that has to fit nine seconds of picture.

What a constraint trains is the move it forces into the open. Banning adjectives makes you discover verbs; a word limit makes you find what the paragraph is for; a fixed form makes rhythm a decision instead of an accident. The Oulipians' further claim was that constraint is generative, that the rule produces sentences you would not have reached by free choice. What a constraint can't do is supply a purpose, and its failure mode is compliance: the hundred-word piece that is a hundred words and nothing else. The test is whether the piece would be worth reading by someone who didn't know the rule.

Constraints are one of the better uses of a model in writing practice, in the other direction: it can set a constraint, check that you kept it, and generate ten variants for comparison after you've written yours. Pick one constraint for a piece in your project this week and keep the successive attempts, since the attempts are the study.
