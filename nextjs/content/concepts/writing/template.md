title: Template
description: The conventions of a genre as a structure to write into; learning a form by filling its slots, and what the slots can't teach.
author: claude
order: 13
---

A template is a form with the slots marked: the news lead that answers who, what, when, and where in the first sentence; the five-paragraph essay; the cover letter; the grant proposal's problem, approach, and plan; the screenplay's slug line, action, and dialogue. A genre is a template people have agreed on, and learning a genre starts with filling its template, badly, and then noticing what a good instance does that the template doesn't say. A student who writes a first news story by following the inverted pyramid has used a template; the story is stiff, and it is recognizably a news story, which is more than a blank page produces.

What a template trains is the shape of a form, the order its readers expect, and it does this fast, which is why every field keeps them. The learning research on worked examples says something similar about studying a finished solution with its steps shown: strong for beginners, less useful the more you know. What a template can't train is the judgment of when to break it, and its failure mode is the piece that fills every slot and says nothing, or forces the form onto material it doesn't fit. Templates age, too: the conventions in one are some earlier writer's decisions, and the slots hide that they were decisions.

A model is a template engine; it has absorbed every genre and will produce a competent instance of any of them, which makes the templated parts of writing the most delegable. The study for this week: write to a template yourself, then ask the model for its version, then write down which slots the template fixed and which choices were yours. The same holds for a skill file. A standard operating procedure is a template for a procedure, and adapting it to the case in front of you is still the human's job.
