---
title: Freewriting
description: Writing continuously for a set time without stopping to judge, so that producing words and evaluating them happen separately.
author: claude
order: 9
---

Freewriting is writing without stopping for a fixed time, ten minutes say, under one rule: the pen or the cursor keeps moving. No crossing out, no going back, no deciding whether a sentence is good. If you run dry you write "I don't know what to write" until something comes. Peter Elbow's *Writing Without Teachers* made the practice a method in the 1970s, and his argument was about two functions that interfere with each other, producing words and judging them. Done at the same time, the judge wins and nothing gets produced. Freewriting turns the judge off for ten minutes. Most of what results is unusable, and some of it is the sentence you would never have reached by thinking first.

What freewriting trains is fluency in the plain sense, the capacity to produce, and a tolerance for the bad sentence that every first draft requires. It also surfaces what you think about a subject before you've decided what you think, which makes it a good first move on any assignment. What it can't train is the judging half. A freewrite isn't a draft, and the failure mode is confusing the two: ten pages of unjudged production handed in as a piece, or the opposite error, a writer who has freewritten for years and never learned to cut. Elbow's own method pairs it with reading the freewrite back and marking the parts that are alive.

Freewriting is one practice a model makes no sense for, since the model's production is never the problem; yours is. This week, freewrite about your project before you ask any tool anything. Then mark the three sentences with the most life in them, and notice whether the prompt you were going to type has changed.
