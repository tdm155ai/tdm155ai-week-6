A writing prompt is a given that starts a piece: "write about a time you were wrong"; "begin with the sentence 'The door was already open'"; "describe this photograph to someone who can't see it"; "a scene in which two people want the same chair." It supplies what the blank page withholds, a place to start and a boundary, and it gets twelve people writing in the same minute. The theater uses the word for the person in the wings who feeds an actor the forgotten line; the writing prompt does the same for the writer without a first one.

What a prompt trains depends on what it fixes. A prompt that fixes the subject trains invention under a constraint; one that fixes the form trains the form; one that fixes the first sentence trains continuation and voice. The good ones are specific enough to exclude most responses and open enough to allow a surprising one. What a prompt can't do is make the writing matter to the writer, and its failure mode is the dutiful response, the piece that answers the prompt and does nothing else, which is why writers learn to abandon a prompt halfway when something better turns up. Writing your own prompts is the advanced version, and a study of your own intentions: to write one for someone else, you have to know what you want the exercise to train.

The same word now names what you type to a model, and the two share more than a word. Both specify a constraint that starts a piece, and a good one in either case says what the result has to do. The difference is who writes next. This week's exercise: write the prompt twice, once for a classmate and once for Claude, and compare what each needs to know. The difference is a map of what you still have to supply.
