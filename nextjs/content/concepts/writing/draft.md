A draft is a whole attempt at the thing, made so it can be changed. The first draft of a thousand-word piece is a thousand words that get you from the beginning to the end, badly. The second draft knows what the piece is about, because writing the first one told you. A draft differs from an exercise or a sketch in that it tries to be the whole piece, and it fails as the whole piece in informative ways. Most writers speak of the first draft with some contempt and keep writing them, because there is no other way to find out what you think. An early draft exists to discover the subject; a late draft exists to be read.

Drafting trains the one thing nothing else in this category trains, getting to the end. Students who revise a first paragraph for a week never learn what the piece's problems are, because the problems live in the relation between the parts, and only a draft has parts. What a draft can't tell you is whether it's any good; you're too close, which is what rereading, the workshop, and the editor are for. The failure mode is stopping there and treating the first complete attempt as the piece. A draft polished sentence by sentence without being rethought keeps every structural mistake it started with.

Drafting is the part of writing a model does best and fastest, and that is the week's problem in one sentence. The first draft is where the learning was: the discovery that you didn't know what you thought, and then did. If a model drafts for you, you arrive at revision with nothing of your own to revise toward. Draft first. Then, if you want, ask for the model's draft and read the two side by side as a study of what yours is doing.
