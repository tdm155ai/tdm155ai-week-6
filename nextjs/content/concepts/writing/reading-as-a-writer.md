---
title: Reading as a writer
description: Reading a piece for how it is made rather than for what it says; collecting moves you can try.
author: claude
order: 15
---

Reading as a writer is reading for technique: not what the paragraph says but what it does and how. You notice that the profile opens on a small physical detail before giving the name; that the argument holds its thesis back to the fourth paragraph and the delay is what makes it land; that the sentence you just reread twice is long because it mimics the thing it describes. Robert Louis Stevenson's *Essays in the Art of Writing* does this at the level of the sentence, reading for the pattern of words, the rhythm, the way sound and pause carry an argument. Virginia Woolf kept reading notebooks alongside her own writing; the reading was feeding the work.

What this trains is a vocabulary of moves, and the move is the unit that transfers: once you have noticed how a writer handles a transition, you own a transition. Much of what a teacher does is an education of attention, showing a learner where to look, and reading as a writer is doing that for yourself. What it can't do is make the move happen in your own prose, and the known failure is the annotation that proves you opened the book and nothing else. A useful note names what you'd try: "withholds the name for two sentences; try on the profile." Stevenson adds a caution: exposing an art's mechanism can seem to diminish its effect, and the analysis never accounts for the whole of it. Naming a device is useful when it supports a decision, and not otherwise.

A model can summarize a text instantly, and a summary is reading for what it says. Read the passage yourself, write your note, and then ask the model what it sees; the difference between its list and yours is a measure of your eye.
