The assistant engineer arrives before the session. They select and test the microphones, run and label the cables, set up the tracks and inputs, and then watch: the engineer, the artist, the producer, the meters. When a hum appears, they find it. When a take is done, they write the log: which microphone, where, which take number, what was said about it. Over a year they hear thousands of decisions made before they are asked to make one. That is apprenticeship in audio, and its first content is operational reliability and observation; the judgments about sound arrive through those, not instead of them.

What it trains is [signal flow](signal-flow.md) by hand, the habit of preparation, the log, and the thing the anthropologist Tim Ingold calls an [education of attention](../learning/education-of-attention.md): being shown where to look and listen, rather than being told rules. What it does not require, whatever the folklore says, is low pay, long hours, or a hierarchy; a professional description of the role documents a working arrangement, not the conditions under which learning happens. A classroom can borrow the parts that matter, supervised responsibility and explicit explanation, and rotate the roles: one student records, one engineers, one logs, and they swap on the next take, so that everyone has been the person who writes down what happened.

With an agent the session log turns into something new. A skill file for Claude is close to an assistant's log rewritten as instructions, the [standard operating procedure](../learning-with-ai/sop.md) of a session, and it has the same limit: it holds what can be written down. Claude is an assistant who never sets foot in the room. It can prepare the template and the checklist; you still have to test the microphone.
