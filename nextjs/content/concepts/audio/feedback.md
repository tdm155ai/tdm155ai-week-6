title: Feedback

A presenter walks in front of the PA speaker and the room squeals. That is acoustic feedback: amplified sound leaves the speaker, re-enters the open microphone, gets amplified again, and loops, and the pitch of the squeal is whichever frequency the loop favors. Microphone placement, speaker placement, and system gain are the levers, which is why the fixes are to lower the gain, move the microphone, point it away from the speaker, or notch out the ringing frequency. It is not echo, not room reverberation, and not a critique, though all four get called feedback.

The other feedback is what the rest of this week is about: information about an attempt that lets you change the next one. In audio it comes from instruments (the meter and the analyzer give you an outcome, a number, with no diagnosis attached), from comparisons (the [A/B](ab-comparison.md) and the [reference track](reference-track.md), which tell you something is different and leave you to say what), and from people (the engineer who says "your mic is off-axis," which is a diagnosis and an intervention in one sentence). The [general account](../learning/feedback.md) sorts these as outcome, diagnosis, and intervention; the athletics version calls the first two knowledge of results and knowledge of performance. Each kind arrives at a different speed and needs a different amount of you to interpret it.

The two senses share a word because both are loops, output fed back into input. Their mechanisms and purposes have nothing else in common, and in a sound class you will hear both in one sentence. When someone says "we had feedback," ask which. If it is the squeal, learn the loop before you reach for the fader; if it is the note on your mix, write it in the log beside the version it was about.
