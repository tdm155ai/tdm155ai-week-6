Before you interview your roommate for the podcast, you record thirty seconds with the microphone on the desk, thirty with it on a stand, thirty with the window closed. You listen, and you decide. That is a test recording: a clip made to find something out rather than to use, the audio engineer's cousin of the photographer's [test shot](../photography/test-shot.md) and the cinematographer's [camera test](../film/camera-test.md). It costs almost nothing, which is why it is the most available study in this course, and why it is so often skipped.

What separates a test recording from fiddling with the gear is the record it leaves. A named question (does the stand get rid of the desk rumble?). One thing changed at a time. The settings written down: microphone, distance, gain, room, time of day. And all the alternatives kept, so that someone else can hear the comparison instead of only your conclusion. Two limits belong in the log rather than out of sight. A performance cannot be reproduced exactly, so some of what differs between two clips is the person, not the setup; and a laptop fan or a passing truck can turn up in one take only. Comparing clips at their raw levels confounds loudness with everything else, so keep the acquisition settings as one record and any playback adjustment as another. This is a proposed way of working, not a laboratory method, and the log should say so.

This week the test recordings are a deliverable alongside the interview: the set of clips, the log, and a paragraph on what the comparison showed. Claude can build the log and name the files; it cannot tell you which take had the truck in it unless you note it.
