For the podcast: you record the conversation (capture), choose the takes and cut the false starts and ums (editing and selection), balance the voices against the music and shape their tone (the mix), then bring the file to the platform's loudness spec and export it (release preparation, which in music is called mastering). In a studio these are often different jobs, a recording engineer, a mix engineer, a mastering engineer, each with a different question in front of the same files. You will do all of them, and the stage names are still useful, because they tell you which question you are supposed to be asking right now.

The stages matter for practice because they can be studied one at a time, and because they are not equally forgiving. A mistake in capture is the expensive one: you cannot un-record the room, the fan, or the microphone that was pointed at the wall. A mistake in the mix costs a saved version. The handoff between stages is where decisions disappear unless somebody logs them, which is why the [assistant's log](apprenticeship.md) exists. The flow also loops back: mixing is often where you learn that you need another take. What goes wrong is mixing while you are still recording, or mastering a rough cut, so that effort goes into a version that will be replaced.

AI tools now sit at every stage: noise removal and transcription-based editing at the front, automatic balancing in the middle, loudness normalization at the end. That makes the workflow the place to decide the [division of labor](../learning-with-ai/division-of-labor.md) stage by stage, and to write it down, since a finished file does not show who did what. Film and photography keep the same kind of stage list for the same reason.
