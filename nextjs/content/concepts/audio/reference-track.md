---
title: The reference track
description: A finished recording you compare your mix against, after matching levels, to study relationships rather than copy settings.
author: claude
order: 7
---

You are mixing a podcast intro with two voices, a music bed, and one sound effect, and you pull up an episode of a show whose sound you like. Switching between the two is using a reference track. The reference is almost always louder than your mix, because it has been mastered, so the first move is to turn it down to match; otherwise every comparison says "theirs is better" for the wrong reason. Then you listen for relationships rather than absolutes: how far the voice sits above the music, how much room is on it, where the effect lands, how the kick and bass share the low end in a song. The iZotope tutorial by mix engineer Nick Messitte lays out this method, and adds that your own earlier rough mix can serve as a reference too, preserving what you meant before you started adjusting.

A reference is the mixer's model for [imitation](../learning/imitation.md): something outside your head to measure against. What goes wrong is copying numbers instead of relationships (matching an EQ curve rather than a balance), choosing a reference from a different genre or made for a different platform, or forgetting that the reference includes a mastering stage your mix has not reached. A reference also cannot tell you what your piece is for. It shows what one finished thing sounds like; whether that is what you want is the judgment the comparison serves.

This week, pick one reference, write down three relationships you hear in it, change one relationship in your mix toward it, and save the version before and after. Claude can suggest references and describe what is typical of a genre. The comparison happens in your room, on your speakers, at a matched level, and that is the part you keep.
