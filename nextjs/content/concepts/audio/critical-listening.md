You have two versions of an interview you recorded, and one sounds muddy. Critical listening is the step where you work out what muddy means: a buildup of low-mid frequencies, too much room reflection, a microphone too far from the speaker. It is listening with a question, as against listening to follow the conversation, and its product is a description that connects what you hear to a decision about the recording. An engineer who says "the voice is sitting behind the music bed between 200 and 400 Hz" is doing it; a listener who says "it's kind of blah" is not yet.

Three questions hide inside most listening, and the practice depends on keeping them apart. Can you detect a change? Can you describe it? Do you prefer it for this purpose? The first two are trainable, with [ear training](ear-training.md) drills and with [A/B comparisons](ab-comparison.md) at matched levels; the third is taste, and taste is not detection. Formal listening tests make a related move: the ITU standard for assessing small audio impairments separates a familiarization phase, where listeners learn the material and discuss the artifacts, from the grading itself. Your class critique is not a standards-compliant experiment, but the order carries: learn what you are listening for, then judge.

This week, when you play back a take, write down what you heard before what you think of it: the time in the clip, the thing, and where it came from. A model can describe an audio file from the signal; it was not in the room, and the room is usually what you are deciding about. The noticing is the [skill](../learning/skill.md), in the old sense of the word: telling relevant differences apart.
