title: Takes and comping
description: Recording several takes of the same passage, keeping all of them, and assembling the best moments into one composite.
author: claude

---

You read a paragraph three times. The first take has the best opening, the third has the best ending, and the second has the one clean version of the sentence in the middle. In a DAW each take sits in its own lane under the track; you audition the lanes, select a stretch from each, and the result plays as one performance. That composite is the comp, and the verb is comping. The takes are kept, not overwritten; Ableton's manual documents the lanes, the auditioning, and the assembly, and most other DAWs work the same way.

Comping looks like error removal, and the study in it is about something else: phrasing and continuity. A comp that splices three different energies can be seamless at every boundary and still feel wrong as a whole, because the reader was in a different place in each take. So the exercise is to compare one complete take against a comp built from all three and say which is better for this piece and why, with a note at each boundary explaining the choice. Keeping the takes is what makes the choice inspectable: someone else can hear the alternatives instead of only your polished result. And the ear that picks "the best" moment is the ear being trained; treat its picks as attempts, not verdicts. A [take](../film/take.md) in film is the same unit, and comparing takes is documented editorial work there too.

Models can now read a paragraph cleanly from text in a synthetic voice, and can rank takes by a metric such as fewest hesitations. What a metric cannot know is which hesitation is the good one. If an agent does the ranking, log which boundaries were its and which were yours.
