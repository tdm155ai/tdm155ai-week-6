---
title: Ear training
description: Short drills that teach you to identify one specified change in a sound, such as a boosted frequency band or added reverb.
author: claude
order: 2
---

A drill plays a short clip twice: once flat, once with a boost around 3 kHz. Which band moved? You answer, the program tells you, and the next pair plays. That is ear training in the audio engineer's sense: a constrained identification task with an answer key. Harman's *How to Listen* program, built to train and select listeners for its product tests, works this way: it introduces simulated changes into recordings and asks listeners to identify, classify, or rate them. The band exercises cover peaks, dips, and filtering; the attribute tasks cover brightness, fullness, reverberation, noise, hum, and channel balance; the difficulty rises as you improve. The terms are the mixer's: a band is a region of frequencies, a boost raises it, a cut lowers it.

What it trains is detection and naming, worth having since you cannot fix what you cannot hear or ask for what you cannot name. What it does not train is the rest of mixing. A drill score records performance on that drill; recognizing a 3 kHz boost is not mixing expertise, and the program's own description does not claim it is. Whether a voice should be brighter for this scene is a different judgment, and whether drill skill [transfers](../learning/transfer.md) to the whole job is not established by the drill. It is a [drill](../learning/drill.md), with a drill's strengths and its blind spot.

You can build your own. Make five short excerpts with one clear change each plus one unchanged, name the files with numbers only, and have a classmate record three things separately: what changed, how confident they are, and whether they prefer it. Repeat on an unfamiliar excerpt a week later. Claude can generate the set and hold the answer key; the listening is yours.
