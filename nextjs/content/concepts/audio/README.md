Most of you will record a voice this month: an interview, a narration, a line for a video. Audio is the part of media-making where the gap between a study and the real thing is thinnest. A test recording costs thirty seconds, the file is small, and what you learn from it goes straight into the next one. It is also where the week's question is easiest to test, because the tools that can now do the work (clean the noise, cut the ums, balance the tracks, hit the loudness target) are good, and the one thing none of them can do is stand in your room.

**The unit.** The unit of practice in audio is small and comes in three shapes. The [test recording](test-recording.md) is the recordist's étude: a short clip made to answer one question about the setup (this mic or that one, this distance, window open or closed), with the settings written down and the alternatives kept. The take is the unit of performance: one attempt at the passage, numbered, saved, never overwritten, so that [comping](comping.md) and comparison are possible later. The [A/B](ab-comparison.md) is the unit of judgment: two defined versions of the same excerpt, at the same level, flipped back and forth. Everything else in this category, the mix, the revision, the reference, is built out of these three.

**What repeats and what varies.** Audio's version of repetition is one thing held still while one thing moves. The clearest example is the study this week asks of you: one microphone, one voice, one sentence, twenty [placements](mic-placement.md). The sentence repeats; the distance, the angle, and the height vary; each placement is logged and every file is kept, and at the end the whole set is played back in order. What repeats in the practice is the comparison itself: listen, name what changed, check the log, again. The sources add a caution worth keeping: a performance never repeats exactly, since nobody says a sentence the same way twice, and the raw levels differ from clip to clip, so the study carries an uncertainty that belongs in the log rather than hidden under a confident conclusion. Repetition in audio is not the same clip twenty times. It is the same question twenty times.

**Where feedback comes from.** Audio is unusually well supplied with feedback, and the kinds are worth keeping apart. The meter gives outcome feedback, instant and impartial: a peak, a loudness number ([metering](metering.md)). The [reference track](reference-track.md) gives you a comparison from outside your own head, once the levels are matched. The blind comparison, where a classmate flips the switch and you don't know which version is playing, tells you whether you can hear what you think you can hear. The assistant engineer's session log is the feedback that arrives a week later, when you can read what you did and compare it with what you remember doing. And then there is the other feedback, the howl when a microphone hears its own loudspeaker; the word covers both, and the entry on [feedback](feedback.md) keeps them separate. Something to know before you trust any of these: the meter cannot tell you whether it sounds good, the reference cannot tell you what your piece is for, and a sighted A/B is tilted toward the version you spent the afternoon on.

**What can't be drilled.** Two things, and they are different. The ear, partly. [Ear training](ear-training.md) can drill detection and naming, a boost at 3 kHz, a touch of added reverb, and that is worth doing. But a drill score is a score on that drill; recognizing a frequency band is not mixing, and the training programs that teach it don't claim it is. The ear that places a microphone well in an unfamiliar room comes from having been in unfamiliar rooms. And taste. Whether this voice should be brighter for this scene is not a detection question at all. [Critical listening](critical-listening.md) keeps three questions apart: can you detect the change, can you describe it, do you prefer it for this purpose. The first two can be trained in a month. The third is what the training is for.

**What AI changes, and what it leaves.** It changes a lot of the work. Noise removal, transcription-based editing, automatic balancing, loudness normalization, source separation that pulls a voice out of a finished mix, synthetic voices that never need a second take. Each of these sits at a stage of the [workflow](workflow.md) and does what an assistant used to do. What it leaves is the room. A model can describe a file and estimate how much reverb is on it; it was not standing where the microphone was, it does not know the refrigerator came on halfway through take two, and it did not hear the difference between take two and take three in the way that made you pick the one with the hesitation. For your projects this means something practical. Let the tools take the mechanical passes, and log what they decided. Keep the studies for yourself: the test recordings, the twenty placements, the blind flips. Those are where the ear gets made, and the ear is what you will still have when the next room is different.

A note on where this comes from. The entries lean on manufacturer guides (Shure, Focusrite, Genelec, iZotope), a DAW manual, and two standards documents (EBU R 128 and ITU-R BS.1116). Those document practices, technical relationships, and examples of training design. None of them measures how much a student learns by doing the studies described here, and the studies themselves are proposals for this week, not established methods.
