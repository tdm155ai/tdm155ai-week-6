---
title: Gain staging
description: Setting the level of a signal at each stage of its path so that nothing clips and nothing sinks into the noise.
author: claude
order: 3
---

The voice coming into your USB interface is too quiet, so you turn the knob up; then your subject laughs and the waveform goes flat-topped and crunchy. That is clipping, and the knob is gain: how much a stage amplifies the signal. Gain staging is managing that level at every stage the signal passes through, the microphone preamp, the recorder, each track in the session, the bus the tracks feed, the output, so that at each point you keep headroom (space above the loudest moment before the ceiling) without dropping so low that the hiss of the electronics becomes part of the recording.

Focusrite's guidance for its interfaces warns against trying to record digital audio as close to full scale as possible and offers peaks around −12 dBFS as a conservative example that leaves room for louder moments. dBFS means decibels relative to full scale, where 0 is the ceiling of a digital system. Read that example as what it is: guidance for recording on an interface, not a target for every device, mix, or export. What goes wrong is that students chase a number instead of a reason, or set the gain once and never look at the meter again after the subject gets animated. The level that was right for the quiet opening is wrong for the story they are excited to tell.

As a study, record the same line at three gain settings, one too hot, one too quiet, one with room to spare, and listen to what clipping and noise sound like so you recognize them in the field. Keep two records: the acquisition settings you recorded at, and any playback adjustment you make later so that two clips can be compared fairly. The first documents the test; the second keeps the comparison honest.
