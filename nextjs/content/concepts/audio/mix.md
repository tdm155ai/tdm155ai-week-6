---
title: The mix
description: Putting the recorded parts in relation to one another, in level, space, and tone, so that the whole serves a purpose.
author: claude
order: 8
---

Four tracks: the interview voice, your narration, a music bed, and a minute of room tone. Mixing is deciding how loud each is relative to the others, where each sits left to right, how bright or dull it is, how much room it has around it, and when all of that changes over the piece. It is not polishing each thing until it sounds good alone. It is putting things in relation, and the relation depends on what the piece is for: a mix for someone listening in a car at speed and a mix for someone in headphones on a quiet train are not the same mix.

In studios the stages are often different people. A recording engineer prepares and captures, a mix engineer assembles and makes the musical judgments, a mastering engineer does the final preparation for release; you will be all three, and the role names still help, because each asks a different question of the same files. What goes wrong is familiar to anyone who has done it once: soloing each track until it sounds great by itself and finding the sum is mud; mixing the voice too loud because it is yours; judging everything on one set of speakers. The practice that helps is to get a balance with the faders only before touching anything else, then work one relationship at a time, save versions, and compare them at matched level.

What cannot be drilled is the purpose. AI tools will now balance tracks, remove noise, and normalize to a loudness target, and they are reasonable starting points and poor final judges, because they do not know what the piece is for. For each one you use, write down what it decided, and ask whether you would have decided the same.
