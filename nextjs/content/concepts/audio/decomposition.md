title: Decomposition
description: Taking a recording apart by source, by route, by time, or by frequency, in order to work on one part at a time.

The voice is hard to hear, and there are four ways to take the problem apart. Solo the voice track, which separates by source. Listen to it before and after the compressor, which separates by route. Scrub to 1:12, where it drops out, which separates by time. Sweep a narrow EQ boost across the spectrum until you find the band where the music bed is sitting on top of it, which separates by frequency. These four partitions are related, and they are not interchangeable: a frequency band is not an instrument, because instruments overlap, and a track name is not a fixed sound, because the same track is a different signal before its effects, after them, and after the fader (DAW manuals distinguish exactly these tap points).

Each partition hides something. Soloing exposes a detail, and the question is always how the detail sounds in the ensemble, which is a different question; this is the general [problem of decomposition](../learning/decomposition.md), that the parts do not simply add back up. A word to define before you use it: stem. A raw track, a grouped export of several tracks, and an AI source-separated output are three different objects that all get called stems, and a conversation that treats them as one will go wrong.

As practice, this is the hand-alone of audio: work the voice by itself, then the voice against the bed, then everything, and write down what changed at each step. AI source separation, which splits a finished song into voice, drums, and bass, is a new kind of decomposition, and an impressive one. It hands you parts you never recorded, with artifacts in them, and learning to hear those artifacts is a listening study of its own.
