---
title: Templates and presets
description: A stored configuration, a session layout or a plugin setting, that saves the setup decisions but not the judgment that made them.
author: claude
order: 18
---

A podcast session template opens with two voice tracks, each with an EQ and a compressor already inserted, a music bus, a markers lane, and the inputs assigned to the interface. You open it and press record. A preset is the same thing at the scale of a single plugin: a saved setting called something like "vocal presence." DAWs let you save a template per kind of project, and the manual for Ableton Live describes template sets that store tracks, input and output settings, and devices. What a template saves is setup time and a set of decisions somebody already made.

It is tempting to call a template stored skill, and the phrase works only if you draw its edge. A template stores decisions and configuration; it does not store the perception that made them or the ability to adapt them. The preset that was right for yesterday's voice is a candidate for today's, nothing more, and a template someone else built is a [black box](../learning-with-ai/black-box.md) you have agreed to use without reading. The study that fits here is an exchange: trade session templates with another group and write up what their template assumes (which microphone, what kind of source, which room) and what would have to change for a different one. The assumptions are usually invisible to the person who made them.

The template is the audio cousin of the [agent skill](../learning-with-ai/agent-skill.md): a stored set of instructions and settings that tells a system how to proceed. Writing a skill file is making a template of your own procedure, and the same limit applies in both directions, since what you cannot say about your judgment does not go into the file.
