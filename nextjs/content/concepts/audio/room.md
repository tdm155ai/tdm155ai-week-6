---
title: The room
description: The space around the microphone and the space around the loudspeakers, each of which changes what gets recorded or heard.
author: claude
order: 13
---

Record the same sentence with the same microphone in your kitchen and then in a closet full of coats. The recording carries the room with it: the kitchen's hard surfaces reflect the voice back a few milliseconds late and give it a sense of distance and space, the coats absorb those reflections and leave the voice dry and close. On the recording side, the room is ambience, something to capture on purpose (a cathedral, a gym) or to reduce (closer microphone, soft surfaces, a blanket over the desk). The balance of direct sound and room sound is the main thing [mic placement](mic-placement.md) changes.

On the listening side, your room shapes what you hear from the speakers, and that is a different use of acoustics. Genelec's guidance on monitor placement describes early reflections from the desk and nearby walls, cancellations at certain bass frequencies caused by the speaker's distance from a boundary, and comb filtering, the hollow sound that results when a reflection combines with the direct sound; it notes that equalization alone cannot repair a cancellation caused by a wall reflection. So a mix made in a boomy corner or on a laptop on a bare desk may sound balanced there and fall apart in a car. What helps is to move the listening position, check on headphones and a second system, and learn the room with reference tracks you know well. Both rooms belong inside any study you write up: say where the microphone was and where you listened.

This is the entry the whole category rests on. A model can describe acoustics in general and even estimate the reverberation on a file. It cannot hear the room you are standing in, and the room is the thing that is different every time.
