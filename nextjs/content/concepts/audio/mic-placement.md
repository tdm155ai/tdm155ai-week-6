Same person, same sentence, microphone at five centimeters, at thirty, at a meter. Close, the voice is loud and intimate, with extra low end (directional microphones boost bass at close range, which engineers call the proximity effect) and very little room. Far, you hear more of the room than the voice: reflections off the walls, a thinner tone, a sense of distance. Distance changes the balance of direct and ambient sound, not only the level. Direction matters as much: a cardioid microphone hears what it points at and rejects what is behind it, so turning it a few degrees changes which part of the room it picks up. Shure's recording guide frames all this as relationships among source, microphone, and room rather than one correct distance.

This is the study Marlon has proposed for the week: one microphone, one voice, one sentence, twenty placements (or fifty), each logged with distance, angle, and height and a quick sketch, every file kept, the whole set played back in order at the end. The point of the number is that somewhere past the tenth placement you start to hear the room move before you check the log. Three cautions travel with it. The sentence never repeats exactly, so part of what differs between clips is the performance. The raw levels differ, so compare playback copies brought to similar loudness while keeping the originals. And hearing the difference is one skill; choosing a placement for a scene is another.

Claude can draft the log template, name the files, and even write a description of each clip from the audio. It was not in the room. The ear you are training with twenty placements is the one that will set the microphone for your interview next week, in a room nobody has described.
