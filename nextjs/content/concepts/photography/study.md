title: The study
description: "A set of photographs made to work something out, where the inquiry is in the comparison rather than in any one frame."

In photography a study is not a genre, the way a still life or a portrait is; it is a purpose. You make a set of pictures to find something out: how this room's light moves across a table in an afternoon, what a 24mm lens does to a face at arm's length, which of six framings of a doorway makes the door the subject. The example is ordinary. The same cup, photographed from the same spot, at nine, at noon, and at five. Not one of the three frames is the point. The point is what you can see once they are beside each other.

That is the property that separates a study from a snapshot or a finished picture. A finished photograph has to stand alone; a study has to be comparable, which means you hold most things fixed and change one, and you keep a record of what you changed. It can look like a drill (bracket the exposure in thirds) or like a question with no settled answer (what happens to the street at dusk), and the general account in [Learning](../learning/study.md) applies here as elsewhere: a piece of work made to work something out. What goes wrong is losing the comparison. Twenty frames with everything different in each one teach nothing, because nothing can be isolated; and a study you can't explain afterward, what the variable was and what you found, was just shooting.

This week the course asks for studies rather than finished work, so state the question before you shoot or generate, keep the alternatives, and bring the set, not the pick. Twenty generated images can be a study in the same sense: one thing varied, a sheet, a sentence about what you learned.
