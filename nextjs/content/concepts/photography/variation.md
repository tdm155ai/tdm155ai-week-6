title: Variation and the series
description: "Making one scene several ways, or many subjects one way; the series as a form of inquiry and, sometimes, as finished work."

Variation in photography comes in two kinds that are easy to confuse. The first is versions of one scene: the same doorway from three distances, at two times of day, with a person in it and out of it. The second is a typology, where the method stays fixed and the subject changes. Bernd and Hilla Becher spent decades photographing water towers, winding towers, and blast furnaces frontally, in flat light, from the same relative height, and hung the results in grids; the sameness of the procedure is what lets you see that no two towers are alike. Their *Winding Towers* (1966–97) is a work, shown in museums, not a pile of drafts toward one good picture.

As practice, the two kinds teach different things. Versions of one scene teach what a variable does, and they belong with [bracketing](bracketing.md) as controlled comparison. A typology teaches something closer to seeing: once the method is fixed, the subject's differences come forward, and you start noticing the category everywhere. The general case is in [Learning](../learning/variation.md), where varied practice beats identical repetition for later use. What goes wrong is calling everything a series. Six unrelated pictures are a folder; a series has a stated constant. And the Bechers' example cuts the other way too: a classroom typology of six campus doorways is a study because you made it to learn, and saying so does not turn their work into exercises.

Generated images make typologies cheap, and that is the trap: a model will give you fifty towers in a fixed style in a minute. The study is in deciding what to hold constant, saying why, and then looking.
