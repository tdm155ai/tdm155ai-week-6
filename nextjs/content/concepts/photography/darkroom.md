The darkroom is where film is developed and where prints are made from negatives, and the second job is the one that matters for practice. Printing is its own exposure: light through the negative onto paper, for a number of seconds, through a filter that sets contrast, and the print changes with every variable. The standard test is the test strip. You expose a strip of paper in steps (five seconds, ten, fifteen, twenty), develop it, and read off which exposure gives the tones you want. Ilford, which makes the paper, points out a flaw in the usual way of doing this: when the steps run across one strip, each step covers a different part of the picture, so you are comparing exposure and subject at once. Their alternative is several strips of the same representative region, each at one exposure. The design of the test decides what its result means.

What the darkroom teaches, even to students who will never enter one, is that capture does not finish the picture. The same negative can be printed light or dark, soft or hard, with the sky held back or let go, and choosing the negative is only the start. A print study therefore needs no new captures: take one frame and render it three ways. It is the clearest case in photography of the general point that the feedback loop can run after the attempt, on the artifact itself. What goes wrong is the same as in bracketing, changing two things between strips.

The digital darkroom keeps the lesson and loses the discipline; a slider is a continuous test strip with no record. Export three versions and name what differs.
