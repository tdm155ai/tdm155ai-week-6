title: Composition and framing
description: "Where things sit in the frame; the familiar guidelines, judged by what each one does to a picture rather than obeyed as rules."

Composition is the decision about what is in the frame and where. The guidelines are familiar: the rule of thirds, which puts the subject off center at one of four crossing points; leading lines, which pull the eye along a road or a railing into the picture; fill the frame; balance a heavy element with a lighter one; or center the subject and let symmetry do the work. A doorway photographed with the door at the center reads as a statement about the door; the same doorway with the door on the right third and a hallway leading into it reads as an invitation. Neither is right. They do different things.

That is the way to practice composition: as a set of effects to learn, not a set of rules to follow or to break. The guides that teach the rule of thirds say in the same breath that it is a guideline, and the slogan "learn the rules so you can break them" has its own trap, which is making the breaking the goal. The better question in front of any frame is what the viewer notices first and what relationships this arrangement creates, and the study that answers it is a comparison: the same subject framed three ways, laid side by side, with a sentence about what each framing does. Composition is also where imitation earns its keep. Copy a framing you admire, say exactly what you borrowed, and you find out whether the effect came from the framing or from something else.

Composition words are among the most reliable levers in an image prompt ("centered, symmetrical," "subject low in the frame, sky above"). Whether you know what the words do is a separate matter, and the three-way comparison is how you find out.
