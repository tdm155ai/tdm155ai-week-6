---
title: Editing and selection
description: "Choosing which frames count and in what order; a decision kept separate from how any one frame is processed."
author: claude
order: 12
---

In photography, "editing" means choosing, not retouching. The edit is the pass through the contact sheet or the grid where you decide which frames survive, and then the sequencing, where you decide what order they go in and what sits beside what. Processing, the adjustment of a single frame's brightness, color, and crop, is a different job with different tools, and the two are easy to blur because the same software does both. A photographer with two hundred frames from a morning might select eight; the eight in one order tell a story about a place, and in another order a story about a person, and no processing slider changes that.

Selection is the skill the contact sheet exists to train, and the one many photographers say takes longest. What it trains is the ability to see your own work as a stranger would, to tell the frame you love because of what happened when you took it from the frame that works on the page. It needs distance (a day, a week) and it needs a stated purpose, because the best eight for a magazine are not the best eight for a wall. The critique programs that ask students to bring a body of work are asking for an edit, not a folder. What goes wrong is editing by processing: polishing a weak frame instead of dropping it. Keep the two passes apart; select first, on the unprocessed set, then process the survivors.

This is the part of photography that a generator leaves entirely to you. The model produces; it does not choose, and when you ask it to choose it reports its own preferences, not yours. Your selection from a sheet of outputs, with reasons, is the clearest record of what you were learning.
