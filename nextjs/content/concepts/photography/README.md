Photography is the discipline where the cost of an attempt fell first. A painter's study takes an afternoon; a photographer's takes a sixtieth of a second, and a phone will make a hundred of them before lunch. That makes photography the clearest case in this week's collection of a question the whole week asks: if attempts are nearly free, what is practice? The answer the field worked out over a century is that the picture was never the expensive part. The judgment is, and judgment is built by making attempts comparable.

The unit of practice in photography is the frame, and above it the test shot and the assignment. A frame is one attempt: this subject, this light, these settings, this moment. A [test shot](test-shot.md) is a frame made to answer a named question (is the window blowing out? is a sixtieth fast enough for her hands?), and it is only a study if you can say what the question was. An [assignment](assignment.md) sits above both: a constraint set for a week or a term that turns a pile of frames into an inquiry. One lens, one city block, twenty frames. The course proposes this, and it is a proposal rather than a documented tradition, but the shape is the one photography schools have used for decades: hold most things fixed so that the thing you vary can be seen.

What photography repeats is the procedure; what varies is one setting, one position, one moment. [Bracketing](bracketing.md) is the strict form: the same framing exposed in steps above and below the meter, so the only difference between neighbors is the one you chose. [Variation](variation.md) is the loose form, and it runs from three framings of a cup to Bernd and Hilla Becher's grids of water towers, where a fixed method applied to different structures makes their differences legible. That example matters because it shows the series can be finished work, not a stack of drafts. A study in photography is not a failed photograph; it is the comparison a set of photographs makes possible.

Feedback comes from laying attempts side by side. The [contact sheet](contact-sheet.md) printed a whole roll on one page so the photographer could see the frame they chose next to the frames they didn't, with the caution that a sheet shows the frames it holds, not the ones left on other rolls or deleted. The [crit](critique.md) adds other eyes: someone says what they notice first, and the useful version ties that observation to what you were trying to do and to the next experiment. The [print](darkroom.md) is the third source, and the least obvious: the same negative rendered three ways teaches that capture is not the end of the decisions, and the test strip, exposed in steps, is bracketing moved into the darkroom. Each of these turns your attempts into evidence, which is the difference between taking pictures and studying.

[Seeing](seeing.md) can't be drilled, or not directly. Exposure can be: aperture, shutter, and ISO have effects you can memorize and then feel. Composition guidelines can be tried until you know what each does. But noticing that the light has changed, that the subject has relaxed, that the picture is in the reflection and not the window, is not a setting. Ansel Adams's word for a related skill was visualization, imagining the finished print before the exposure, which is different again from noticing. Neither comes from repetition alone. They come from repetition with attention, from looking at the sheets afterward, and from someone pointing at what you missed. The drills exist to free attention for what the drills can't reach.

Students in this course mostly generate images rather than expose them, and it would be easy to conclude that none of this applies. The opposite is closer. A prompt that says "a portrait" gets the model's average portrait; a prompt that says "window light from camera left, shallow depth of field, the subject's eyes on the lower third" gets a specific one, and the words in that second prompt are the vocabulary a photographer builds by bracketing, by light studies, by reading contact sheets. A generator makes the attempt free. It does not make the question free, and it does not make the [selection](selection.md) free: the model produces, and when you ask it to choose it reports its own preferences, not yours. The stated question of a test shot, the edit across a sheet, the reasons kept in an [archive](archive.md), are what you still have to supply, and they are also what you would have to write into a skill for an agent. The model has seen more photographs than any of us. It has not seen the one in front of you, and it does not know what you were trying to do.

So this week's photography studies are proposed as the ones they always were, done with a camera or with a prompt, with one addition that the whole week asks for: say which frames you made, which the model made, what changed between them, and why.
