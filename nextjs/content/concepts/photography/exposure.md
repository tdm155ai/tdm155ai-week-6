Exposure is how much light reaches the sensor, and three settings control it. Aperture, the size of the lens opening, also decides how much of the scene is in focus: open wide and the background melts, stop down and everything is sharp. Shutter speed, how long the sensor is exposed, also decides whether motion blurs or freezes. ISO, the sensor's sensitivity, also decides how much noise the picture carries. The textbook name is the exposure triangle, and the lesson beginners miss is that these are not three ways to make a picture brighter. Each one buys brightness with something else. A student who wants a moving hand sharp has a different problem from a student who wants its motion visible, even if both pictures are correctly exposed.

Exposure can be drilled, which is why it comes first in most courses. Manufacturers' guides recommend a priority mode so that you vary one setting at a time, and the sensible study is exactly that: the same scene at f/2 and f/11, then at 1/15 and 1/500, with a record of what changed. After a few weeks the tradeoffs stop being a diagram and become something you feel at the dial. But technical fluency is one capacity among several. Exposure does not tell you where to stand, what to include, when to press, or how to light the subject, and a phone's computational camera makes some of these decisions for you in ways its documentation may not explain. Know the triangle; don't mistake it for photography.

For a prompt, the exposure vocabulary is a lever. "Shallow depth of field," "long exposure," "high ISO grain" are terms a model has seen attached to millions of images, and using them precisely is one way a prompt stops being average.
