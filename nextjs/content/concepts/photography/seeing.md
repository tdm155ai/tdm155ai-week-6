Two things get called "seeing" in photography, and they are worth telling apart. The first is observation: noticing that the light on the far wall has gone warm, that the woman at the bus stop is mirrored in the window behind her, that the shadow of the railing makes a second railing on the pavement. The second is what Ansel Adams called visualization. Standing in front of Half Dome in 1927, he imagined the print he wanted, a black sky and a monumental face of rock, and chose a red filter to get it; he first called this pre-visualization, and visualization is the word he settled on. Observation is about the scene. Visualization is about the picture that doesn't exist yet.

Neither can be drilled the way exposure can, but both can be trained indirectly. Observation grows from constraint and return: the same block for a week, the same window every morning, which is what the assignment and the daily photo are for. Visualization grows from the print. Only once you have made enough pictures to know what a scene becomes on paper can you predict it, and the test is explicit: say what the picture will look like before you take it, then compare. Drawing's version of the claim is in [observation](../visual-art/observation.md), that making is a way of looking. What goes wrong is substituting the camera for the eye, shooting first and looking at the screen, so that the noticing happens to a thumbnail instead of to the world.

With a generator, visualization is the whole job and observation is the thing most at risk. A specific prompt is a visualization written down. The question for the week is where the specifics come from, if not from looking.
