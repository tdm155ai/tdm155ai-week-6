title: The assignment
description: "A constraint set in advance that turns a pile of frames into a question; one lens, one block, twenty frames."

An assignment is a constraint given before the shooting starts: a subject, a limit, a number, a deadline. Photography schools run on them. The International Center of Photography's curriculum has students practicing framing with an empty slide mount, then shooting portraits and still lifes to a brief, then editing and discussing the prints; the assignment is what makes the shoot comparable to the crit that follows it. The course's own version, proposed for this week, is one lens, one block: a single fixed focal length, a single city block, twenty frames, and then fifty or a hundred if the first twenty taught you something. That formula is ours, not a documented tradition, but its shape is the standard one: fix most of the variables so the ones you leave free can be seen.

What an assignment trains is attention under constraint. With a 50mm lens you cannot zoom, so you walk, and after twenty frames you know what that lens sees before you raise it. With one block you run out of obvious pictures by frame eight, and the next twelve are where the looking starts. A difficult lens, as this course's assignment puts it, is a lens whose limits force decisions: a long lens that makes you stand across the street, a wide one that makes you step uncomfortably close. What an assignment can't do is substitute for the review. Twenty frames with no contact sheet and no crit are twenty frames, and the number matters less than the record: twenty with notes beat a hundred without.

For generated work the same move applies. Fix the model, the aspect ratio, and the subject; vary only the prompt's description of the light; make twenty; keep them all. The constraint is what makes the twenty a study instead of a scroll.
