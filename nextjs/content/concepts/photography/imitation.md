Photographers learn by copying, as painters do, and the useful form of it is precise. "In the style of Walker Evans" is a mood; "a frontal, centered facade in flat light, the camera level, nothing moving" is a list of operations you can carry out and check. ICP's curriculum has an example in which students, after looking at Shirin Neshat's photographs, make their own images with writing on them, borrowing a specific move rather than an atmosphere. The exercise is to choose one picture, list the decisions in it you can see (distance, height, light, what's centered, what's cut by the edge), and remake those decisions on your own subject.

What imitation trains is decomposition: to copy a picture you have to work out what it is made of, and the ones that resist copying show you where the decisions you can't see are. The general account is in [Learning](../learning/imitation.md), and the painter's version, copying the masters, is in [visual art](../visual-art/imitation.md). What it can't train is why that photographer made those decisions. The failure mode is borrowing the surface, a grain and a color cast, and calling it the style. Name what you took. If you can't name it, you haven't learned it yet; you have only matched it.

This is also where the generator is strongest and the student most at risk. "In the style of" is the prompt a model answers best, by averaging thousands of that photographer's frames, and it produces an imitation you did no decomposition for. The study is to write the operations, not the name, and see whether you and the model agree about what the style consists of.
