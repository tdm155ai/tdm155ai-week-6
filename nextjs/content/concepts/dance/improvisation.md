---
title: Improvisation
description: Practice without set steps, moving from a score, a rule, or a partner's weight instead of from a phrase.
author: claude
order: 15
---

The teacher says "cross the room, and don't let your feet lead," and twenty people find twenty ways to do it. Or two dancers lean into each other until neither is standing on her own, and the dance is whatever keeps them from falling; that is contact improvisation, which Steve Paxton began in the early 1970s and which is still taught as a form. William Forsythe recorded a set of improvisation tools in the 1990s, ways of extending, folding, and dropping an imagined line through space. In each case there is no phrase to learn. What is given instead is a score: a rule, an image, a partner's weight, a constraint, and the dancer's job is to answer it in real time.

Improvisation trains what a set phrase cannot: deciding while moving, listening to a partner's weight before it arrives, and finding movement outside the vocabulary your technique gave you, which is why many choreographers generate material this way and then set it. It does not train a specific execution. An improviser becomes wide, not reliable, and the two kinds of practice complement rather than replace each other. The common failure is that the body, left to itself, improvises its habits, and twenty minutes of "free" movement turns out to be the same six moves. A good score is one that makes the habit impossible, and improvisation, done seriously, is practice under constraint rather than its absence.

A model is a reasonable improvisation partner: give it the score and let it answer, or let it set a score for you. What it cannot do is share weight. The listening that contact improvisation trains has no text version.
