Opening night. The stage is wider than the studio, so the spacing is off by a step; the lights make the front row disappear; the costume catches on a lift that was clean for three weeks; the audience is breathing. A dance that was finished in the studio is suddenly new, and the dancer finds out what rehearsal did not cover. A run is the series of performances that follows, the same program night after night, and the dancer's job in it is a strange one: to repeat the thing exactly and make each repetition look like the first time, which is the theater's problem too.

What the studio cannot supply is the list above, plus adrenaline, no stopping, no mirror, and fatigue of a kind only a run produces, eight shows a week on a different body each day. Performance trains what rehearsal cannot: committing fully, performing for someone, and recovering in real time, the fall that is covered so that only the other dancers saw it. Dress rehearsals and previews exist to get some of that early. The distinction between [learning and performance](../learning/learning-vs-performance.md) is useful here in both directions. A clean final run in the studio does not mean the dance is learned, and a good opening night does not either; the test is the second week and the second cast. By the second week a run has changed the dance, deepened it or let it go stale, and a rehearsal director's work during a run is noticing which.

This week's exercises stop short of performance on purpose, and that is worth remembering when judging them. A study that goes well in the room is evidence of a session, not of a skill. What you can do when it counts is found out when it counts.
