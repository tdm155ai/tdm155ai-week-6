title: Rehearsal
description: Learning repertory, the work of getting a particular dance into particular bodies by a particular date.
author: claude

---

After morning class, a company spends its afternoons in rehearsal, and the difference between the two is the difference between training a capacity and training a piece. In rehearsal a twenty-minute dance is learned phrase by phrase from a rehearsal director over a few weeks: dancers mark it, then do it full out, then with the music, then run a section without stopping, then run the whole thing, then take it to the stage. Nothing learned in rehearsal is thrown away at the end of the day, which is the opposite of class, and everything learned in rehearsal is aimed at a date.

Because it is finite, rehearsal is a schedule of decisions about what to stop for. Stop-and-go work fixes one transition at a time; a run-through finds out whether the fixes survive being strung together, and whether the dancers have the stamina for the piece. Theater's [scene work](../theater/scene-work.md) and [run-through](../theater/run-through.md) are the same pair. Rehearsal also trains things class never touches: spacing (where in the room each body is on each count), partnering, entrances, and the counts of a specific score. What it does not reliably train is adaptability. A dancer can become fluent in one sequence without becoming better at learning the next, and the one general skill rehearsal does build, being a quick study, comes from learning many pieces rather than from polishing one.

The week's exercises are mostly class, not rehearsal: small, repeated, thrown away. It is worth knowing which you are doing. A rehearsal makes a thing; a class makes a maker.
