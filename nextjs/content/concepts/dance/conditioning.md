---
title: Conditioning
description: Training the body, its strength, range, and endurance, as distinct from training the skill, the step itself.
author: claude
order: 17
---

A dancer whose jumps are low does not only practice jumping. She does calf raises, plyometrics, and core work in a gym, and the jumps get higher without a single jump being rehearsed. Pilates was taken up early by dancers in New York for exactly this use; stretching, strength training, and swimming are now ordinary parts of a dancer's week. None of it is dancing. Conditioning raises what the body can do; skill is doing a particular thing well with that body. For a long time class was assumed to supply both, and it does not: class trains the step, and a body that lacks the strength for the step cannot learn it by repeating it.

The distinction matters because the two fail differently. If the limit is strength or range, more repetitions of the step make it worse, not better, through fatigue and compensation; if the limit is skill, more strength does nothing, and a strong body with no technique is not a dancer. Conditioning follows the logic athletics calls [overload](../athletics/overload.md): the body adapts to a load slightly beyond what it is used to, provided it gets the rest in which the adaptation happens. Dancers are known for skipping the rest, and overuse injury is the field's occupational disease. Conditioning also does not transfer on its own. A stronger calf does not jump higher until the jump is practiced again with the new calf, so the two kinds of training alternate rather than substitute.

The split applies beyond dance. Sitting through a four-hour edit, holding a camera steady for a minute, and keeping attention on a sixth take are conditioning; the cut, the move, and the judgment are skill. Before practicing a thing harder, ask which limit you are at.
