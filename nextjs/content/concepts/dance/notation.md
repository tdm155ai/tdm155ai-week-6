A page of Labanotation is read from the bottom up: a vertical center line stands for the body, columns on either side for the limbs, and symbols whose shape gives direction, whose shading gives level, and whose length gives duration. Rudolf Laban published the system in the 1920s. Benesh Movement Notation, developed in the 1950s and long used by the Royal Ballet, writes the body onto a five-line stave like music. Both can record a whole ballet precisely enough that it can be restaged decades later by people who never saw it. Few dancers read either; notators are specialists, and since the 1970s video has become the working memory of most companies.

Notation gives dance what a score gives music: a record independent of any body, a way to reconstruct, and something to point at in a dispute about the real version. What it loses is also what a score loses. The quality of the movement, the effort, the intention, the way one dancer's weight sits differently from another's, are not on the page, and a notated ballet still needs a répétiteur to bring it back. Video loses different things. It is one angle, one cast, one night, so one dancer's errors become the dance and a dancer learning from the tape learns that dancer rather than the choreography. The research for this week puts it plainly: a written outline and a video are records of a practice, not substitutes for a teacher's observation.

Motion capture, pose estimation, and video generation are new notations and inherit the old limits. The question to ask of any of them is the question to ask of a skill file: what is in the record, what is not, and who in the room still has to know the difference.
