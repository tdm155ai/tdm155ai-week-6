---
title: Decomposition
description: Breaking a phrase into steps, counts, and body parts so each can be learned on its own.
author: claude
order: 3
---

A rehearsal director teaching a new phrase says "just the feet," and the room does the footwork with arms hanging. Then "now add the arms." Then "with the head." Then "with the music." The phrase has been taken apart along one seam, the body, and reassembled a layer at a time. It can be cut along other seams too: by counts ("the first eight, then the second eight"), by step (a pirouette becomes a preparation, a relevé, a spot, a landing, each practiced alone), or by difficulty, pulling out the one hard transition and doing just that transition fifty times while the rest of the phrase waits.

Decomposition gives each piece attention it would never get inside the whole. It is also how dancers learn fast: a phrase that is too much to take in at once is learnable as four small things. What it loses is everything that happens between the pieces. The arms learned separately sit on top of the legs like a borrowed coat until they have been done together many times; the transition between two steps is its own skill and not the sum of the steps; timing inside the music does not survive being counted out dry. Reassembly is a stage of practice in its own right, not a formality, and the [research on part and whole practice](../learning/part-whole-practice.md) says the same: parts help most when the parts are independent, and least when the skill is in the joins.

This is the move the whole week leans on, and dance shows its price clearly. A student who has decomposed a problem for a model has done the first half of the work; the second half is noticing what fell between the parts.
