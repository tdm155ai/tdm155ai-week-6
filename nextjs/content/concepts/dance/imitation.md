---
title: Imitation
description: Learning by watching, from the teacher's demonstration, from the front row, from the dancer who already knows it.
author: claude
order: 10
---

The teacher demonstrates the combination once, marking it, saying the counts, and the class copies it. There is no second demonstration and nothing is written down. In a crowded studio the back rows watch the front row, which is why the front row is where the strong dancers stand and new dancers are told not to. Repertory is learned the same way: a company member who knows the role stands in front, and the new dancer does what she does, a beat behind, until the beat closes. Dance is passed on by imitation to a degree few other fields match, and a dancer's first skill is learning to see a phrase quickly enough to do it.

What imitation trains is seeing movement in units and turning what you see into what you do, which is harder than it sounds: the demonstration is marked rather than full, is seen from the front or the back, and is reversed if the teacher faces you. What it cannot give is the inside of the movement, the weight, the effort, the timing of the breath. You copy the shape, and the teacher corrects the rest. It also copies more than intended, a dancer's errors along with her phrase, and a company's house style along with its steps. Ingold's point about skill applies: the teacher shows you where to look, not what to do, and the [education of attention](../learning/education-of-attention.md) is what the demonstration is for.

A generated clip of a dancer is a demonstration with no inside and no one to correct what you copied from it. It may show a movement no body can make. Watching the front row at least guarantees the front row did it.
