title: Automaticity
description: Muscle memory in the dancer's sense, a phrase the body can run without being told, so attention can go elsewhere.
author: claude

---

A dancer learns a phrase on Monday, counting every step. By Friday she can do it while watching the person beside her, listening for a cue, and thinking about dinner. Dancers call this muscle memory. The memory is not in the muscles, but the name captures how it feels: the phrase has become something the body does rather than something the head directs, and the dancer rides it the way you ride a bicycle, steering without thinking about balance.

The [general account](../learning/automaticity.md) describes three stages, from working everything out, through smoothing, to automatic, and in dance the third stage is the goal of rehearsal, not a side effect. A dancer who is still thinking about the next step cannot perform, because performing needs the attention that thinking uses: for the music, the partner, the space, the audience. Two things go wrong. What becomes automatic is whatever the body did most, errors included, so a phrase rehearsed wrong is now reliably wrong. And once automatic, a phrase resists change: a choreographer alters two counts and the body keeps doing the old version for a week, a problem every dancer who has learned a second production of the same ballet knows. Ericsson has argued that automaticity is where improvement stops, which is one reason class never lets an exercise settle into a fixed combination.

A model's fluency is not your automaticity. A task you have delegated every time never reaches the third stage in you, and never frees your attention for anything, because it never occupied it. That is sometimes the right trade and sometimes not; the point is to know which stage you are at, and in what.
