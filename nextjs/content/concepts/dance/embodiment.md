---
title: Embodiment
description: Knowledge that lives in the body, what a dancer knows that cannot be said, shown on video, or handed to anyone else.
author: claude
order: 16
---

A dancer in a balance knows where her weight is. She cannot tell you, and if she tried the words would be useless; the video cannot show it either, since two balances that look identical from outside can be a held one and a falling one. The only way to know is to stand there and fall off it a few hundred times until the body stops falling. Polanyi's phrase for this is that we know more than we can tell, and the [general account of tacit knowledge](../learning/tacit-knowledge.md) covers it; dance is the case where the balance between what can be said and what cannot tilts furthest toward the second.

For practice this means the body is both the instrument and the record. It learns by doing, under correction, over time, and the inside of a movement, the weight, the effort, the breath, does not transfer by description, diagram, or watching. A dancer's proportions, injuries, and habits are part of what she knows, so no two dancers know the same thing. It is why dance needs répétiteurs instead of scores, and why the mirror and the video are treated with suspicion: they show the outside, which is not where the knowledge is. A recording does not exhaust what a mover learned, and a generated moving body may look plausible from one angle while being impossible from another. Judging such a clip takes movement knowledge; generating it does not show that anyone can perform it.

This makes dance the week's clearest case of a skill that cannot be delegated, and the place to ask its question. When you do it, you learn something in the body. When the model does it, you learn to judge a picture of a body. Both are skills. They are not the same one.
