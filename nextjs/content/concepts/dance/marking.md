---
title: Marking
description: Doing a dance at reduced size and effort, to remember it, to check it, or to save the body.
author: claude
order: 4
---

During a break in rehearsal a dancer stands at the side of the room, moving her hands and a little of her feet, lips counting. She is marking: doing the dance small. The shape, the order, and the timing are there; the height of the jump, the full extension of the leg, and most of the effort are not. Often the hands stand in for the feet, two fingers walking the steps. Teachers mark when they demonstrate a combination, so the class learns to read a marked phrase as a full one. Dancers mark in a tech rehearsal to spare their legs, and in the wings before an entrance to make sure the sequence is still there.

Marking is a memory tool and a test at the same time: you find out whether you know the phrase without paying the cost of dancing it. David Kirsh, a cognitive scientist who worked inside Wayne McGregor's company, has argued that marking is a form of thinking with the body, and his group reported that dancers who marked a phrase between run-throughs remembered it at least as well as those who practiced it full out. What marking cannot do is train the execution. The balance, the height, and the stamina are not rehearsed by a two-finger version, and a dancer who has only marked a section is surprised by it at full size. Timing drifts too, since a marked step takes less time than a real one, which is why the music is put back as soon as possible.

Marking is the dancer's sketch: fast, cheap, and done to find out what you know. Before you ask a model for something, try the same move. Describe the steps in your own words. Whatever you cannot mark, you do not yet have.
