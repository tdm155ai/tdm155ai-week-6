A ballet class begins at the barre, the wooden rail along the studio wall, with pliés: knees bending and straightening, slowly, in each position of the feet. Then tendus, the foot brushing out along the floor and back; dégagés, the same with the foot leaving the floor; ronds de jambe, frappés, fondus, and finally grands battements, the leg thrown high. Then the class leaves the barre for the center of the room: adagio (slow balances), pirouettes, petit allegro (small quick jumps), grand allegro (big traveling jumps), and the révérence, the closing bow. Ninety minutes, the same order in every studio. What changes is the combinations: the teacher sets new sequences of these steps each morning, learned on the spot.

The fixed order is the point. Because nobody has to think about what comes next, attention goes to the body: the hips over the feet, the turnout, the finish of the foot. The barre isolates (a hand on a support takes balance out of the problem, so legs and placement can be worked alone) and the center puts it back together. Class is warm-up and training at once, and not for beginners: professionals take it nearly every day for a whole career, and the principal never graduates from the tendu. What goes wrong is routine without attention; the teacher's job is to make yesterday's exercise a new problem today. Other techniques have their own set shapes; the Cunningham Trust describes its classes as built around torso and legwork, spatial awareness, and rhythmic accuracy.

Nothing in class can be delegated, which makes it a model for everything else this week. The form is copyable even where the content is not: a fixed order that frees attention, a daily return, and an [exercise](../learning/exercise.md) repeated while what it sits in varies.
