Walk into a ballet studio at ten in the morning and you will see roughly what you would have seen a century ago: dancers at a wooden rail along the wall, bending their knees in time to a piano. This is [class](class.md), and it is the first thing to understand about how dance is learned. A professional dancer takes class nearly every day of a career, and the class does not change much. Pliés, tendus, dégagés, and up the ladder of barre exercises to grands battements; then the center: slow adagio, turns, small jumps, big jumps, and the bow. Nobody graduates from it. The principal stands at the same barre as the apprentice, doing the same tendus.

**The unit.** The unit of practice in dance is the combination: a short sequence of steps, usually eight to thirty-two counts long, set by the teacher that morning and never written down. The teacher demonstrates it once, [marking](marking.md) with hands and half-size steps; the class watches and copies; the pianist plays; the room does it. Then the room does it on the other side. A class is twenty or thirty of these. A rehearsal is made of longer ones, the [phrases](phrase.md) of an actual dance. Everything in the training is built from pieces this size: small enough to hold in the body all at once, large enough to be dance rather than a position.

**What repeats and what varies.** The exercise repeats and the combination varies. Every day there are tendus; the tendu combination is different every day. This is the system's trick. The thing being trained (the tendu itself, the turnout, the hips placed over the feet) is done thousands of times, but the dancer never gets to do it on autopilot, because the sequence it sits in is new, and attention has to stay on. The combination is thrown away when the music stops, and it has done its job if the tendu is a little better than yesterday. And every combination goes left and right. The second side is the first variation of every exercise, and it keeps the body honest, since nearly everyone has a side they prefer. Rehearsal reverses the proportions: the phrase stays fixed, because it is the choreography, and what varies is the dancer's hold on it, first marked, then full out, then with the music, then in costume, on the stage, under lights. [Repetition](repetition.md) here means the same thing done again, and the question each time is whether it is the same thing.

**Where feedback comes from.** Dance studios have a feedback device built into the wall. The [mirror](mirror.md) shows a dancer the shape she cannot feel accurately, and it is the first thing dancers reach for and the thing teachers spend years weaning them from, because it works only facing front and there is no mirror on stage. The second source is the correction: a word, or a hand that presses the hip down an inch. [Corrections](feedback.md) are given to one dancer and meant for the room; everyone applies them. In rehearsal the correction comes from the [répétiteur](repetiteur.md), the rehearsal director who carries the dance in memory and checks each body against it. Video came later and is a mirror with memory, with the same limit: it shows the outside.

**What can't be drilled.** The range of a body can be trained, but each body has limits, and conditioning is not the same as skill. Beyond that, the things that make two dancers doing identical steps look different (phrasing, musicality, the quality dancers call presence) are not drilled, though they are prepared for. And the most important thing a dancer knows cannot be said at all: where the weight is in a balance, how much is enough. You find it by falling off it a few hundred times. That is [embodiment](embodiment.md), and it is why the field has a profession of people who carry dances in their bodies and teach them into other bodies, rather than a shelf of scores.

**What AI changes, and what it leaves.** Dance is the discipline where the knowledge most obviously lives in the body, which makes it the clearest case the week has. A model can describe a dance, count it, cut the video, suggest a phrase, and generate a clip of a body doing something. None of it moves your leg. The generated dancer may look right from the front and be impossible from the side; judging that takes movement knowledge that generating it does not give you. What AI changes is the record: notation, video analysis, the choreographer's sketching and recombining of phrases, the production of demonstrations with no inside. What it leaves is class, which cannot be delegated, and the question is the week's question in its bluntest form. What are you learning when you do it? Something in the body. What are you learning when Claude does it? How to judge a picture of a body. Both are skills; they are not the same skill, and dance makes that hard to confuse.
