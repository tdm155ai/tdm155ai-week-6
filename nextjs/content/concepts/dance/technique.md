A tendu means the same thing in every ballet studio in the world, and so do the other few hundred French words that name ballet's positions and steps. That shared vocabulary is what a codified technique is: a system of movement with a name for each part, a class with a set order, and a standard to correct against. Ballet is the oldest. The twentieth century added others, each built out of one choreographer's body and needs: Graham technique, organized around the contraction and release of the torso and work on the floor; Horton technique, with its flat backs, lateral stretches, and a sequence of named studies; Cunningham technique, which the Cunningham Trust describes as centered on torso and legwork, spatial awareness, and rhythmic accuracy, with phrases that change orientation and shift meter and tempo.

Codifying buys a great deal. A technique can be taught by someone other than its inventor, drilled every morning, corrected against a shared standard, and written about. It also costs something. Each technique is a record of particular bodies' solutions, and not every body fits it; a dancer can learn one perfectly and dance dully; and techniques are not interchangeable, so a ballet dancer learning Graham spends months unlearning. The written outline is not the technique either. The Cunningham Trust's classes are taught by former company members and authorized teachers, and a video of a 1984 class is a record of the practice, not a substitute for a teacher who can see and correct.

Technique is the dance world's skill file: what a field has managed to write down so it can be taught. What it leaves out is the subject of the [embodiment](embodiment.md) entry, and of the week.
