title: Counts
description: The numbers dancers say under the music, the scaffold that holds a phrase in memory.

"Five, six, seven, eight," and the room moves. Choreography is taught in counts, nearly always in eights: each step lands on a number, "and" marks the half-beat between, and a phrase is "two eights" or "a six and a two." The counts are a grid laid over the music. A dancer can learn a phrase before she knows the music at all, run it in a silent hallway, and tell another dancer exactly where to come in ("you enter on seven of the third eight"). Merce Cunningham's dancers often rehearsed with no music, since the score was made separately and sometimes heard for the first time at the performance; the counts, and at times a stopwatch, were what held the dance together.

Counts work because they chunk. Eight steps become one eight, a dance becomes a list of eights, and memory can hold the list where it could not hold the steps. What they train is timing and recall; what they cannot carry is quality, the way the weight moves between three and four, which is why a phrase learned only by counts looks mechanical. Dancers describe the failure as "dancing the counts instead of the music," and some of them count visibly, with the face. Choreography built on breath, or on irregular music, resists eights altogether, and even in classical work the last stage of learning a phrase is throwing the counts away and letting the music carry it.

Counts are a notation that lives in speech, and an abstraction in the week's sense: a layer that hides the one below so that something larger can be handled. The thing to know before using one is what it hides.
