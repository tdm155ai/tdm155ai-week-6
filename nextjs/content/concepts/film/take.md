"Scene twelve, take three." A take is one recorded attempt at one shot, slated so it can be found again. Five takes of a single line are five separate recordings of the same frame, the same blocking, and the same words, with the performance and the timing varying between them. Comparing them is the editor's work, not the director's memory: the ScreenSkills checklist for editors lists reviewing takes, comparing them, and constructing alternatives from them, and a line in the finished film is sometimes the first half of one take and the second half of another.

The thing to know before calling for another take is that repetition is not improvement. Take seven may be worse than take two. Another take gives the editor an option without necessarily changing what the actor can do, and a set that goes again and again without saying why is collecting, not practicing. The useful habit is to name what should differ this time ("hold the look a beat longer before you answer") and to notice what is being held fixed. That note, more than the count, is what makes a take an attempt in the sense this week cares about. The general account of what repeating does and does not do is in [repetition](../learning/repetition.md).

A generation is a take. Run the same prompt ten times and you have ten takes; choose among them and you are editing. Keep them all, label them, and write down what you changed between runs, because the selection is where the judgment lives.
