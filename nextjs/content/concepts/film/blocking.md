title: Blocking
description: Deciding where the people and the camera move in a scene, and when.

Blocking is the plan of movement: who crosses to the window on which line, where the second person is standing when the first one turns, where the camera is for each of those moments. In the rehearsals Martha Pinson describes with Sidney Lumet, the actors worked out their staging on a taped-out floor first, and the camera positions were designed afterward around what they had found; the blocking was recorded as it changed, and the camera diagrams were redrawn to match. The order can run the other way, with the camera fixed first and the actors placed for the frame, but either way the blocking decides what the shots can be.

That dependency is what makes blocking a useful thing to study. Change one movement and some of the planned shots stop working: the over-the-shoulder no longer has a shoulder in it, the insert is now behind someone's back. Theater blocks for an audience that sits in one place; film blocks for a frame that moves, which means the same scene has to be staged for each shot and kept consistent across them. The study proposed for this week is the direct version: rehearse a short exchange on a floor plan, decide who moves and why, choose the camera positions, then change one move and find out which shots need revising. Keep both plans.

For a generated scene, blocking is the thing you most often fail to specify. A floor plan with arrows is a better prompt than an adjective, because it tells the model where people are, and it tells you what has to match from shot to shot.
