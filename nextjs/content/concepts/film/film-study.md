Film study here means watching a film closely enough to take it apart, with a making question in hand. The British Film Institute's analysis of *12 Angry Men* in twenty-five frames does this for one room: it compares framing, camera height, lens, lighting, and cutting across selected frames to show how Lumet keeps a single enclosed set visually alive and how the changing camera position builds tension. François Truffaut's book-length interview with Hitchcock does it film by film, asking at each turn how a particular effect was made. In both, the viewing is organized by a question a filmmaker would ask.

What this trains is noticing: that the camera is lower in the second half than the first, that the reaction shot comes before the line rather than after. What it cannot show is that you can do any of it. The National Film and Television School pairs film culture and research with practical projects for this reason; analysis alone is not evidence of production skill, and a student who can describe every cut in *12 Angry Men* has not thereby learned to cut. The bridge is the practical question. "How does he keep the room interesting for ninety minutes" becomes "how do I keep my room interesting for ninety seconds," and the study ends in an attempt, not a paragraph. Athletes use the same term for the other direction, watching themselves, and you should watch your own cut the way the BFI watches Lumet.

A model can describe the shots in a sequence quickly and fluently. Check it against the frames. Where its description exceeds what is on screen, you have found something about the tool and something about your own eye.
