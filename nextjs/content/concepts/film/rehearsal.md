Rehearsal in film is practice for a scene before it is recorded. Script supervisor Martha Pinson describes Sidney Lumet's version from eight films with him: two or three weeks of it, starting with reading and discussion around a table, then staging on a floor taped out to the set's dimensions. The cinematographer came to the final run-through, and the shot design and lighting followed what the actors had found; Pinson wrote down the blocking, the dialogue changes, and the timings while the camera diagrams were revised and passed around. Lumet's own book, *Making Movies*, describes the same commitment to rehearsing before shooting.

Know that this was his method, not the norm. Many productions rehearse on the day, on the set, with the camera waiting, and some directors avoid rehearsal because they think it flattens what the actor would have found on the first take. What rehearsal buys is that the actors find the scene before the clock is running and the crew learns the moves before it has to follow them. It also separates two kinds of practice that a shoot otherwise mixes: rehearsing the performance, which is the actors' work, and rehearsing the coordination, the camera move and the focus pull, which is the crew's. Theater keeps these apart as scene work and tech; on a set they are often the same unrecorded takes.

For a generated scene, the rehearsal is the cheap pass: a stills version, a walk-through of the space, scratch audio under the animatic. Run it before you spend credits on the real thing, and note what the cheap version taught you.
