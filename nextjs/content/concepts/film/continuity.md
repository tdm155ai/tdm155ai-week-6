title: Continuity
description: The records and checks that keep costume, props, appearance, and action consistent across takes and shooting days; AI consistency is a comparison, not the same process.

Continuity is the reason the glass that is half full in the wide shot is still half full in the close-up recorded two hours later. Scenes are shot out of order and in pieces, so someone has to keep the record: which hand held the glass, where the coat was unbuttoned, what the actor said when the line changed. On a professional set that someone is the script supervisor, whose trainees, in the ScreenSkills description, take continuity photographs and keep technical logs under supervision. Martha Pinson, in that role for Lumet, recorded the blocking, the dialogue changes, and the timings of every take. Hitchcock, in 1937, credited his wife Alma with handling the technical continuity of his films.

What continuity trains is noticing, at the level of the cuff and the glass, and the habit of writing down what you noticed, because the editor three weeks later interprets the footage through those records and cannot ask the room. What goes wrong is simple: nobody wrote it down, and the error is discovered in the cut when the set is gone. The editor's checklist pairs reviewing footage with reviewing the continuity records for exactly that reason.

For AI-assisted video the problem reappears as keeping a character the same across generated shots, and it is worth saying carefully that this is a comparison, not the same process. On a set, consistency is kept by records and a person watching; in generation it is kept by reference images, repeated prompt language, and checking the output against the reference, and the kinds of drift are different. The course's own version of the problem is in [character consistency](../learning-with-ai/character-consistency.md), and the animator's reference document, the [model sheet](../visual-art/model-sheet.md), is the older tool for it.
