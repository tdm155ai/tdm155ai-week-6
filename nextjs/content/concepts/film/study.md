A film study, as this week uses the term, is a small piece of work organized around a question you can answer: can a viewer follow this action from these four shots; does the camera move keep two people in the same relation; does the cut make a reaction feel immediate or delayed? The object can be a storyboard, a rehearsal, a lighting test, a recorded scene, or two edits of the same footage. The scene recorded once in one shot and once in four is a study. So is a character walking through a door, generated three times with one word changed. This is a teaching definition, not an established genre; filmmakers make all of these objects, but for production reasons rather than to learn.

What separates a study from a short is that the purpose is stated. Film schools build their teaching from such pieces: the National Film and Television School's directors' course combines script analysis, work with actors, and staging exercises with a directed scene that is then edited, reviewed, and given feedback. But a short can be a complete work, a training exercise, or a demonstration for a larger project, and nothing about its length tells you which. Do not assume every short is practice for a feature, and do not call something a study if you cannot say what it was for. A study cannot make a film; it answers one question and leaves the rest. The general account of the word, from the painter's study to the musician's étude, is in [the study in general](../learning/study.md).

For your Unit 2 work, state the question before you make the thing, then write what you found beside the file, saying which part you did, which part a model did, and what you were learning in each case.
