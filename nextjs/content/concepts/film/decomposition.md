title: Decomposition
description: Breaking an action into the separate views that give the audience what it needs, in order.

In a 1937 essay on his own methods, Alfred Hitchcock explains a scene from *Sabotage* in which a woman at a dinner table comes to kill her husband. Rather than filming the table from one position, he photographed separate views, a hand, the eyes, the knife, the other person, and built the situation by selecting and cutting among them. The audience sees what the shots give it, in the order they give it, and the suspense is made from that control of access. That is decomposition in film: taking one continuous action apart into the views that matter and deciding their sequence.

It is a different operation from the administrative breakdown a production also does, in which a script is split into scenes, locations, and props for scheduling. Hitchcock's kind is creative: which views, and why these. What it trains is thinking in parts, and seeing that a single action contains several things a viewer might need to know, not all of which the wide shot delivers. Hitchcock limited his account to his own practice, and his preference for conceiving every shot in advance should not become a rule that the film exists complete before shooting; a scene can also be found in rehearsal and decomposed on the day. The general account of taking a skill apart, and of what is lost when the parts go back together, is in [decomposition in general](../learning/decomposition.md).

The study for this week is the plain version: record an ordinary action as one continuous shot and again as four planned views, drawn as thumbnails first. Ask a viewer what happened and where they were unsure. Then write down the one view you were missing, or the one you did not need.
