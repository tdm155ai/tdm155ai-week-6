---
title: Template
description: A reusable structure for a production document, such as a shot list or a continuity log, that fixes the questions and leaves the answers blank.
author: claude
order: 16
---

A template in film is a production document with the answers removed. A shot-list template has the columns and no rows: scene, shot, size, angle, movement, subject, purpose. A continuity-log template has a place for the take number, the lens, the timing, the costume notes, and a box for what changed. A call sheet is a template filled in every night. Each one is a record of what somebody once decided mattered enough to ask about every time, and that is what makes it worth reading even when it is empty. This is the sense the category uses; genre and three-act structure are also called templates, but they belong to a different discussion.

What a template fixes is the set of questions, which is what lets a crew that has never worked together produce documents the next department can read. What it freezes is the same thing. A template written for a two-camera dialogue scene has no column for the variable you are testing, and a student who fills it in dutifully has recorded everything except the point. Adapting the template is still a human job: deciding which column to add, which to drop, and which question this production needs that the last one did not. The entries on the [standard operating procedure](../learning-with-ai/sop.md) and on the writer's [template](../writing/template.md) make the same point from other directions.

For this course, a template is the skeleton of a skill. The fields a shot-list skill demands of every row are a template; writing them down is a study of what you think a shot is, and it is the first file in the skill folder.
