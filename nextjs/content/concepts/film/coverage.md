---
title: Coverage
description: The set of shots recorded so that a scene can be assembled in the edit, with alternatives if the plan fails.
author: claude
order: 5
---

Coverage is the material a scene is built from. For a conversation at a table it might be a wide master of both people, an over-the-shoulder on each, a close-up on each, and an insert of the thing on the table, so that the editor can cut between them in any order the scene needs. For the climactic fight in *Only God Forgives*, cinematographer Larry Smith describes covering the action from static positions with three cameras and changing lenses between setups. Coverage is a shooting strategy; the scene is made from it later.

The caution is that more angles do not guarantee usable options. Coverage can be a way of postponing a decision rather than making it, and a crew that shoots everything has often not decided what the scene is. The ScreenSkills checklist for editors describes the real test: reviewing the footage against the continuity records, comparing takes, building alternatives, and flagging pickups, with the set kept standing until the assembly shows that nothing is missing. Martha Pinson, writing about Sidney Lumet's rehearsal-heavy method, acknowledges the other side of the trade: his designed shots left limited coverage, and the cost of that was argued about. Hitchcock planned every view in advance and shot little else. Neither is the rule.

Plan your coverage from the storyboard, not from a list of standard sizes. After the cut, write down the one shot you lacked and the ones you never used. That pair of notes is the study.
