A film crew is organized by department, and each department trains its own. The ScreenSkills checklist for a script supervisor trainee shows what that looks like from the bottom: the trainee keeps technical logs and takes continuity photographs under supervision, is expected to ask why things are done the way they are, practices the skills they have watched and gets feedback on them, and is asked to evaluate their own learning as they go. Responsibility grows as judgment is trusted. The trainee who photographs the table on day one is the one checking the table against the photograph by week three.

What an apprenticeship trains is specific. It is the department's craft, learned by standing next to someone doing it, in the setting where it is done, which is why it carries the parts of the skill nobody has written down, where to look and what counts as a problem. What it cannot be assumed to provide is a ladder. The familiar stories, from production assistant to assistant director or from loader to cinematographer, skip the department-specific responsibilities that each of those jobs involves, and this category does not repeat them as a rule. Watching is not enough either; the trainee checklist insists on practicing with feedback, which is the difference between an apprentice and an observer. The general account, including the atelier and the cognitive apprenticeship, is in [apprenticeship in general](../learning/apprenticeship.md).

If you are on a crew this term, even a crew of two, keep a note of what you were allowed to do each day and what you were only allowed to watch. The line between them moving is the record of the apprenticeship.
