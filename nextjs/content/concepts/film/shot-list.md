title: Shot list
description: A written inventory of the shots a production plans to record, with what each one is for.

A shot list is a table. One row per shot: scene, shot number, size (wide, medium, close), angle, any camera movement, who or what is in it, and, in the version this week asks for, what the shot is for. The storyboard shows the sequence; the shot list turns it into things a crew can do in an order that makes sense on the day, grouping every shot that needs the camera in the corner before anyone moves it. On Lumet's sets the equivalent record was the camera plot, a diagram of positions that Martha Pinson describes being revised and circulated through rehearsal; the tabular shot list as described here is this course's working definition, not a form documented from one production.

What a shot list trains is the translation from an idea of a scene to a plan of recordings, and the discipline of finding out, on paper, that you have no shot in which both people are visible. What goes wrong is a list that varies words rather than decisions. Three rows that differ only in an adjective are not three shots. A useful rule for your own lists: every row names a purpose, the one variable it changes, a constraint, and what you expect to see that will tell you it worked. A list cannot tell you whether the shot will work; that waits for the [camera test](camera-test.md) and the cut.

An agent can draft a shot list in seconds, which makes the checking the student's job: does each row change something that matters? Writing down the fields a good row needs is the first step toward a shot-list skill, and a study of what you think a shot is.
