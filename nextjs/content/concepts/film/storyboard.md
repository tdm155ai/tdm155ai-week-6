---
title: Storyboard
description: A sequence of drawn frames used to explore and communicate framing, staging, and narrative before anything is shot.
author: claude
order: 1
---

A storyboard is a row of small drawings, one per shot, that shows a sequence before it exists. If you are planning a thirty-second video of someone unlocking a bike, the board might be a wide shot of the rack, a close-up of the hands at the lock, and the face looking up when it opens. Each panel is a framing decision; the row is an editing decision. At Walt Disney Animation Studios, story artists work from thumbnail sketches of the script up to refined drawings, and the studio describes the work as sorting out emotion, timing, staging, and framing together with the writers and editors. The board is where those decisions get made, not a picture of decisions already made.

That is the thing to know before drawing one. A board treated as an illustration, with panels decorated after the sequence is settled, tests nothing. A board treated as an exploration, with the same beat drawn three ways, is the cheapest place to discover that a sequence does not work: the audience will not know where the lock is, or the cut to the face comes too late to read as relief. What the storyboard trains is thinking in shots and in cuts. What it cannot show is duration, because a static panel has no length, and that is what the [animatic](animatic.md) is for.

For AI-assisted video the storyboard is the study that survives. A video model generates one shot at a time, so the sequence has to be decided somewhere, and the board is where you find out which shots you need and what has to stay the same across them. Draw it fast and badly; its job is to be changed.
