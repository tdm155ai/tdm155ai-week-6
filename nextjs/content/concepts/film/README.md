Film is a strange place to look for practice, because almost nothing on a set is done for the sake of getting better at it. A second take is recorded to give the editor an option, not to train the actor. A camera test is run to choose a camera. Dailies are watched to catch a focus problem while the set still stands. Each of these has a production purpose that has nothing to do with learning. And yet, together, they make up one of the most developed systems anywhere for testing a piece of work before it is finished, and that system is what a student making AI-assisted video can borrow.

## The unit

The smallest unit is the [take](take.md): one recorded attempt at one shot. Above it sits the shot, which is a decision about what the audience sees; above that the scene, assembled from shots in the [rough cut](rough-cut.md). But the unit of practice, the thing a filmmaker makes in order to find something out, is the test: a [storyboard](storyboard.md) panel drawn three ways, an [animatic](animatic.md) cut to length against scratch audio, a [camera test](camera-test.md) comparing two capture choices, a [rehearsal](rehearsal.md) on a taped-out floor. Each is a cheap version of the expensive thing, made to answer one question. The [study](study.md), as this week uses the word, is a short piece of film work organized around a question you can answer, with the question written down.

## What is repeated and what varies

Between takes, the frame, the blocking, and the lines stay fixed and the performance varies. Between camera tests, the subject and the light stay fixed and the camera varies. Between two cuts of the same footage, the footage stays fixed and the order or the duration varies. The discipline is in deciding, before you go again, which of these you are doing. "Again" is not a plan. A director who says "once more, and this time don't look at her until the second line" has named the variable; a director who says "once more" has recorded another option without learning anything. The same holds for a storyboard redrawn, an animatic re-timed, or a prompt re-run: say what changed.

## Where feedback comes from

Film's feedback arrives in stages, and the stages are what make it teachable. [Dailies](dailies.md) show the recorded material while shooting can still respond to it; Larry Smith projected the dailies of *Only God Forgives* on a large screen because a small monitor hides the focus and lighting faults that a cinema reveals. The rough cut shows, for the first time, whether the shots make a scene, and an assembly that reveals a missing shot can send the crew back for a pickup. A test screening shows whether an audience understands what the makers thought was clear. The lesson in each is to review early enough for the observation to change the next attempt, and to review under the conditions in which the work will be seen. "Version two is better" is weak evidence; "the listener's reaction now comes before the answer, so viewers see the hesitation" is a finding.

## What can't be drilled

A take can be repeated, but whether take seven is better than take two is a question, not a rule; a performance does not improve by count. [Coverage](coverage.md) can be increased, but more angles do not guarantee a usable cut, and a crew that shoots everything may have postponed the decision rather than made it. Alfred Hitchcock, in his own account of his methods, planned every view in advance; Sidney Lumet rehearsed for weeks and let the camera follow the actors. Neither approach is the norm, and no exercise produces the judgment that chooses between them. What film can drill is the mechanics: the slate, the continuity log, the shot list, the cut. What it can only prepare for is the moment when the material on the screen is not what was planned and someone has to decide what the scene now is.

## What AI changes and what it leaves

For this course, the question is which of these studies survive when the shots are generated rather than shot. The storyboard survives, and becomes more important: a video model makes one shot at a time, so the sequence has to be decided somewhere, and the board is where you find out which shots you need and what has to stay the same across them. The animatic survives as the cheapest way to test duration before paying for a clip. The test survives almost unchanged: same prompt, two models; same reference, two settings; a declared variable and a prediction. [Continuity](continuity.md) changes the most. On a set it is kept by a script supervisor's photographs and logs and by a person watching; in generation it is kept by references, prompts, and checking, and the two are a comparison, not the same process. A character who drifts between shots is the generative version of the glass that is full in the wide and empty in the close-up.

What AI leaves is the editorial work. A generation is a take. Ten runs of one prompt are ten takes, and choosing among them, comparing, cutting, noticing that the reaction comes too late, is what the editor's checklist has always described. A changed cut, whether you changed it or an agent did, is not evidence that you learned; the evidence is whether you can say what the change fixed and why. Keep the two versions. Write the question down first. That is what a film study is.
