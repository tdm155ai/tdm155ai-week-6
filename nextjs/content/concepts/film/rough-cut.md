A rough cut is the scene put together for the first time: every shot in order, roughly trimmed, with no sound mix, no color, and temporary music if any. It is the point at which the sequence exists as something you can watch rather than something you planned. The vocabulary around it varies by workflow. Some editors speak of an assembly first, then a rough cut, then a fine cut, then picture lock; others use the words differently or skip a stage, so treat the labels as local. What they share is that the cut is provisional and is made in order to find things out.

What it finds out is usually that something is missing or in the wrong place: the reaction comes too late, there is no shot in which the two people are seen together, the insert of the lock is needed and was never recorded. The ScreenSkills checklist for editors puts this to practical use, describing assemblies built while shooting continues so that pickups can be flagged before a set is struck. The mistake is to polish it. A rough cut that has been color-graded is a cut you will be reluctant to change, and its whole value is that it can be changed. Save each version rather than overwriting it, because the difference between two cuts is the evidence a study produces.

This week's editing study is the rough cut made twice: from the same clips, two versions that differ in where a reaction sits or how long a shot holds, shown to viewers who are asked the same question without being told what to look for. Revise once, and write down why.
