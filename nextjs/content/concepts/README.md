This is the week's reference: a map of the words people use when they practice. Every field that trains people has its own name for the small, repeatable piece of work that builds a skill. Painters make studies. Musicians play études. Athletes run drills. Actors rehearse. Photographers shoot tests. Writers do exercises. Audio engineers make test recordings. The names differ, but the thing they name is the same: an attempt made in order to get better at making, not in order to make the finished thing.

The first two categories are the foundation. **Learning** is the general account of how a skill is acquired: what repetition does, why doing well in a session is not the same as having learned, what a teacher adds, and why some of what an expert knows can't be written down. **Learning with AI** is the week's problem. When a model can do the work, what are you learning when you do it yourself, and what are you learning when it does it for you? The answer is not "stop using the model." It is that you need to know what you are building in yourself and what you are building for the model, and those are two different deliverables.

The nine categories after them are the evidence: music, visual art, writing, theater, dance, athletics, film, photography, and audio engineering. Each one's essay answers the same five questions. What is the unit of practice here? What gets repeated, and what varies? Where does feedback come from? What can't be drilled? What does AI change in this field, and what does it leave alone? Read the essays across fields and the pattern shows: every discipline has found a way to isolate a part, repeat it with variation, get feedback on it, and put it back into the whole, and every discipline has something at the center that practice can prepare for but not produce.

Many words appear in more than one category. Repetition means one thing to a pianist running a passage, another to a dancer doing the combination left and right, another to an actor in a Meisner exercise. Each entry defines the term as its field uses it and links up to the general account in Learning. When you find the version of a word that fits the thing you are making this week, that is your study.
