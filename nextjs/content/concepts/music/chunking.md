---
title: Chunking
description: Grouping notes into phrases, chords, and patterns that memory and the hands can hold as single units.
author: claude
order: 7
---

Nobody memorizes a sonata note by note. A musician holds it as phrases, chords, and patterns: "a descending sequence in thirds," "the opening figure, up a step," "four bars of the same arpeggio with the top note changing." A chunk is a group of things the mind treats as one. A beginner reads a scale as seven separate notes; an experienced player reads it as one shape and has attention left over for how to play it. The idea comes from memory research (Miller's limit of about seven items; Chase and Simon's chess players, who could recall real board positions far better than random ones because they saw patterns, not pieces), and the same thing shows up in studies of how skilled musicians read notation.

Chunking is what makes a piece memorizable and playable at speed, and music theory is in large part the vocabulary for it: knowing that a run is a dominant seventh arpeggio turns twelve notes into one name. What goes wrong is at the boundaries. Memory slips in performance tend to happen where one chunk ends and the hands must find the start of the next, so musicians practice "starting points," the ability to begin from any phrase in the piece rather than only from the top. A chunk can also be too big to be reliable, which is where decomposition comes back in: break the chunk down, rebuild it, and the rebuilt version is more secure.

Every medium has chunks. A shot-reverse-shot is a chunk; so is a fade-under-voiceover. Part of what this week asks is which chunks you own, in the sense that you can call them up and vary them, and which ones a model is calling up on your behalf.
