A jazz student puts on a recording of a saxophone solo, plays four seconds, stops, finds the notes on the horn, plays the four seconds again, and keeps going until the whole solo can be played along with the record. This is transcription, and it has long been how jazz is learned: from the record, by ear, phrase by phrase. The Suzuki method teaches small children the violin the way they learned to talk, by listening and copying before reading a note. Most music in the world is passed on this way; notation is the exception. Even the notated tradition learned by copying: Bach copied out other composers' scores by hand and arranged Vivaldi's concertos for keyboard, and copying was how he studied them.

Imitation trains the ear first, because you cannot copy what you cannot hear. It builds vocabulary: the phrases you transcribe become phrases you can play. And it carries what notation cannot: time feel, tone, the way a phrase leans. What it does not train is deciding what to play; a player who has only copied solos can sound like a mosaic of other people. The common failure is copying the surface, the notes, without the thing that made them work, which is why teachers insist on transcribing in the fingers and the ear rather than only onto paper, and on taking a copied phrase through all twelve keys until it is yours.

A language model is an imitation machine, and the examples you put in a prompt are a kind of transcription you hand to it. What a musician learns from transcribing is in the transcribing: the hours with the record, the ear slowly catching up. That is the step a model does for you; notice when you have handed it over.
