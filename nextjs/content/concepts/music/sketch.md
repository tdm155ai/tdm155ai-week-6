Beethoven carried small sketchbooks in his pocket and kept larger ones at his desk, and filled both with fragments: a theme tried in four forms, a few bars of counterpoint, a reworking of a passage that had been finished the week before, alternatives crossed out and rewritten. The Beethoven-Haus in Bonn, which holds much of this material, distinguishes the desk books from the pocket books, has reconstructed leaves that were scattered over two centuries back into the books they came from, and matches sketches to the works they fed. A performer's practice vanishes as it happens; a composer's leaves a paper trail, and the trail contains the alternatives that were not chosen.

A sketch is for trying without committing, keeping without finishing, and comparing. The sketchbook is where the composer's version of repetition happens: the same theme, again, slightly different. Two cautions. An archive of surviving marks is not a transparent recording of a mind at work; it is evidence of what was written down and survived, organized by scholars long afterward. And fragments that lose their relationships become unreadable, which is the practical lesson for anyone keeping a record of their own studies: give each sequence of attempts a name, keep the attempts in order, and mark which is the original and which is a later comment. That rule is a proposal borrowed from the archive's troubles, not a claim about how Beethoven meant his books to be read.

Working with a model, the ten variations it produces are sketches. The sketchbook is the record of which one you chose and why, kept with the ones you didn't, and a before-and-after pair with one sentence on the decision between them is often enough.
