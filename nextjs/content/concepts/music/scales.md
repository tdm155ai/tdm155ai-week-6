title: Scales and arpeggios

A scale is the notes of a key played in order, up and back down. An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time instead of all at once. They are the first thing a music student learns and the last thing a professional stops practicing, because tonal music is built out of them: a hand that knows all the major and minor keys already knows most of the shapes in a score. Juilliard's pre-college violin audition asks for them by name, alongside an étude and concerto movements, which shows how an institution thinks about musicianship: as components, none sufficient alone.

Scales train evenness, intonation, fingering patterns, and the ability to move through any key without thinking. They are also the easiest practice to do badly, as a mindless warm-up that occupies the hands while the mind is elsewhere, which is why teachers give a scale a target: a rhythm, a crescendo to the top, a particular articulation, a tempo one notch faster than yesterday. A cellist and a trumpet player practice different scales differently, but both are drilling the raw material of the pieces, and in both the aim is for the pattern to become one shape rather than seven notes, done while attention goes elsewhere.

Every medium has its scales: the operations you should be able to do without thinking before you can make anything with them. For a video editor it might be a clean cut on action and a level audio bounce; for someone building a tool for Claude it might be the handful of commands the skill assumes can be run. Naming yours is the first step of this week's work, because they are what a model can do for you most easily, and what you lose most quietly.
