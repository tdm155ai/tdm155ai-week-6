---
title: The master class
description: A public lesson in which one student plays and is corrected in front of others, who learn by watching.
author: claude

---

A master class is a lesson with an audience. One student plays a prepared piece for a visiting artist while a hall of other students watches. The artist stops them, works on a single passage, sings it, plays it, asks for it again, and the student tries to change something on the spot. The format is often traced to Liszt's teaching in Weimar in the mid-nineteenth century, and it is now a fixture of conservatories and summer festivals. Everyone in the room is there to learn from one person's correction.

For the student on stage, the class trains performing under correction: taking a note in public and trying it immediately. For the watchers, it trains hearing what the master hears. "Did you hear that?" aimed at the room is the master class's main teaching move, a way of directing everyone's attention to one thing in the sound, and the whole session works as a worked example of a lesson, with the teacher's reasoning spoken aloud. What goes wrong is that it is also theater. The master performs; the correction can be a display of the master's taste rather than a diagnosis of the student's problem; the student cannot practice the change properly until later, so what the room sees is a first attempt; and the watchers may come away with the words rather than the sound.

Critique in a media classroom is a master class in this sense, with the same strengths and the same risk that the room learns the teacher's vocabulary without learning to see. When a model's notes on your work are read aloud for everyone, the same thing applies, with one difference: the model has no ear in the room, and its "did you hear that?" is a guess about something it did not hear.
