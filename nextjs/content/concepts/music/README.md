Music is the discipline that has thought longest, hardest, and most publicly about how to practice. Its vocabulary is the one the other fields borrow: the étude, the scale, the lesson, the rehearsal, the master class. A conservatory is an institution built around the question of what to repeat, and every musician you have ever heard spent years in a small room doing something that was not performing. That makes music the clearest place to see what practice is, and the clearest place to ask what happens to it when a model can do the work.

## The unit

The unit of practice in music is small and bounded. It is the passage: four bars of a real piece that are not yet working. It is the [exercise](exercise.md): a pattern with no musical purpose, built to isolate one mechanism, like the sixty Hanon patterns that no one has ever performed. And it is the [étude](etude.md): a piece built around one technical problem that is still a piece of music. The whole field's genius is in that middle category. Chopin wrote training pieces that are played in concert halls, and the word he used for them is the French for "study," from the Latin for devoted attention, the same root as the English word. The painter's study of a hand and the pianist's étude are one idea. Under all three sits the shared vocabulary of [scales and arpeggios](scales.md), the raw material a hand has to know before any piece makes sense to it. The first decision a musician makes every morning is which unit to work on, and that decision is a skill of its own.

## What is repeated and what varies

The passage is what gets repeated. What varies is nearly everything else: the tempo (the metronome moved one notch at a time), the hands (right alone, left alone, together), the rhythm (the same notes in a dotted pattern, then reversed), the size of the unit (the bar, the phrase, the page), and the day. Musicians found out by trial what the learning research later measured: identical repetition installs whatever is repeated, including the errors, and a passage practiced ten ways holds up under pressure better than one practiced the same way a hundred times. The theme and variations, the form in which a composer keeps the bass line and changes everything else thirty times, is this principle turned into music. And when the player joins a group, the unit changes again: what a quartet repeats in [rehearsal](rehearsal.md) is not notes, which each member already has, but the relationships between them.

## Where feedback comes from

Three places. The first is the teacher, at the weekly [lesson](lesson.md), who hears what the student cannot yet hear and says where to listen. The second is the recording: since every phone became a recorder, the playback has been a second teacher, returning the attempt the way a listener would receive it rather than the way it felt from inside. The third is the ear, the student's own, which the other two exist to train. Music is the one discipline with a required class in perception, and the design of the whole system, lesson by lesson, is to make the student's ear good enough to replace the teacher's. The master class puts this loop in public so that a room can learn from one person's correction; the practice log writes it down so that it can be read.

## What can't be drilled

[Interpretation](interpretation.md) is the clearest case: the score fixes the notes and leaves open the tempo, the shape of a phrase, which voice to bring forward, and there is no exercise for making those decisions well. It is learned by listening to many performances, by years with a piece, and by a teacher asking "do you hear what you are doing there?" The ear is partly drillable (intervals and chords can be named with practice) and partly not: hearing what is wrong with the shape of a phrase comes by exposure and by having it pointed out. Performance can be practiced but not drilled, because its first rule, never stop, is the opposite of the practice room's. And the choosing of what to practice next is the teacher's skill, the one that transfers to the student last.

## What AI changes and what it leaves

A model can now write the exercise, generate the ten variations, transcribe the solo, and give notes on the recording. The repetition still happens, in a second, but not in you, and the thing a musician learns by transcribing a solo was in the transcribing. The model can also be a teacher who is available every day instead of every week, which changes the loop's rhythm; but it hears only what you give it, and the lesson's point was always to put the teacher out of a job by training your ear. So the week's question lands with unusual precision in music: for each attempt, what did you do, what did the model do, and what can you now hear that you couldn't last week?

What AI leaves is the ear, the hands (no model will practice your scales), interpretation, the performance you have to give without stopping, and the plain fact that you can judge only what you have practiced judging. The model for the week's work is the concert étude: a study, built around one problem, that somebody else would want to watch.
