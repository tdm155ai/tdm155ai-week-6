In a conservatory ear-training class the teacher plays two notes and the students write down the distance between them (the interval); plays a chord and they name its type; plays a melody and they write it out (dictation); hands them a line of notation and they sing it at sight (solfège). None of this trains the hands. It trains perception. Most students start unable to tell a major third from a minor one; by the end of the term they can, and only the ear has changed. Relative pitch, hearing distances between notes, can be trained in nearly everyone. Absolute pitch, naming a note with no reference, is much harder to acquire after childhood, and whether adults can learn it at all is debated.

The ear is the skill that makes every other kind of practice possible. A student who cannot hear that a note is out of tune cannot fix it, however many times they repeat the passage, and the lesson's "listen to the bass" assumes an ear that can find the bass. Only part of it can be drilled. Interval and chord identification respond to flashcard-style practice; hearing what is wrong with the shape of a phrase comes by exposure and by a teacher pointing, an education of attention rather than a set of right answers. And it cannot be handed off. A tuner tells you the pitch, but the tuner is not on stage with you.

Audio engineers train the same way, with the same split between drillable identification and learned judgment. For anyone working with a model that can also judge the output, the ear is the thing worth protecting: a tool that evaluates for you does not train your evaluating, and your ear is what you are left with when the tool is gone.
