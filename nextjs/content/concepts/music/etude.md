An étude is a short piece of music built around one technical problem. Chopin's Étude Op. 10 No. 1 sends the right hand up and down the keyboard in wide broken chords, over a slow bass, for about two minutes; nearly every bar asks the same thing of the hand, and the point is to make that one motion reliable. But Chopin's études are played in concert halls, because they are music: they have a shape, a harmony that goes somewhere, a feeling. The word is French for "study," from the Latin *studium*, devoted attention, the same root as the English "study." A painter's study of a hand and a pianist's étude are cousins: a piece of work made to work one thing out.

What separates the étude from a plain exercise is that it has to communicate. An exercise can be pure mechanism. An étude asks you to solve the technical problem and make it sound like something at the same time, a harder and more useful target, because that is what a real piece demands. The cost is that an étude is less isolated: when the passage goes wrong, the music around it can hide where. So teachers pair the two. Scales and exercises isolate the mechanism; the étude puts it back into a musical situation; the repertoire piece puts it among a hundred other demands.

This week the étude is the model for a study you can make: a short piece of media built around one operation (one cut, one camera move, one transition) that still has to say something to a viewer. The test of whether you have made an étude rather than an exercise is whether someone could watch it without knowing what you were practicing, and still get something from it.
