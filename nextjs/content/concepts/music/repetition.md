title: Repetition
description: Running a passage again and again; what that fixes, what it bakes in, and how musicians keep it from going stale.

The basic act of the practice room is playing the same bar again. A pianist takes a four-bar run, plays it, plays it again, and again, until it comes out right, and then plays it right ten more times so that right becomes the default. The saying in practice rooms is that practice does not make perfect, it makes permanent: the hands learn whatever they repeat, including the wrong fingering, the rushed third beat, and the tension in the shoulder. A passage repeated twenty times with an error has been practiced as an error twenty times.

Repetition helps when it comes after you have found the right way, in units small enough to control, with attention on each pass. It hurts in three familiar ways. Playing the piece from the top every time means you practice the parts you can already play and skid through the parts you can't. Repeating at speed before you can do it slowly repeats the mistake faster. And repeating with the mind elsewhere installs whatever happened to come out. Musicians also know what the learning research later measured: identical repetition feels productive and yields less than repetition with something changed, which is why a good practice session turns the same passage over in different rhythms, tempos, and groupings rather than running it unchanged.

Working with a model, the repetition can happen without you. Ask for ten versions of a cut and you get ten in a minute; the tool has repeated, but nothing has been installed in your hands or your ear. The question for every repeated thing this week is where the repetition is happening, and whether that is where you wanted the skill to end up.
