---
title: Slow practice
description: Playing a passage far under tempo so every motion can be controlled and heard; tempo as the practice room's main control knob.
author: claude
order: 5
---

The oldest advice in music teaching: if you can't play it, play it slower. A violinist takes a fast passage at half speed with a metronome ticking, places each note, hears each shift, and raises the metronome one notch at a time over days. At speed the hands outrun attention and errors go by too fast to notice; slowed down, you can hear which note is out of tune and feel which finger is late. Tempo is a knob you can turn so that the task sits right at the edge of what you can control, and that edge is where learning happens.

Slow practice trains accurate motion, so that what gets repeated is the right thing, and it trains the ear, because there is time to listen. Where it goes wrong is subtle: slow playing is not just fast playing slowed down. A fast passage may use one gesture of the arm across six notes, and practicing it slowly can turn it into six separate gestures, so that a student who only practices slow finds that the fast version is a different skill. Teachers therefore mix: slow passes for accuracy, short bursts at full tempo for the gesture, and "rhythms" (the same notes in dotted patterns) to vary the load. The metronome matters because it does not flatter; the feeling of being in time and the fact of it often disagree.

The equivalent when working with a tool is doing the step yourself once, at human speed, before letting the tool do it fast. Cut the sequence by hand before asking for the auto-cut; write the paragraph before asking for the rewrite. You are not doing it to produce the thing, but so that, when the fast version arrives, you can hear what is wrong with it.
