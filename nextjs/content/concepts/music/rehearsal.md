---
title: "Rehearsal"
description: "Practice for a group: coordinating parts, cues, and interpretation, which practicing alone cannot supply."
author: claude
order: 13
---

An orchestra rehearsal brings together eighty players who have each practiced their own part and asks them to play the piece under a conductor, stopping to fix balance, entrances, and the places where the sections drift apart. A string quartet spends an afternoon on a passage that every member can already play. What gets practiced in rehearsal is not the notes but the relationships between them: who leads and who follows, how the tempo bends and who bends it, how four players tune one chord together, what happens when someone comes in early. The sectional rehearsal (just the violins, just the brass) is decomposition applied to a group.

Rehearsal trains listening, anticipation, and recovery: the skill of staying together when someone else goes wrong, and of adjusting to a cue that came a little late. It also builds a shared interpretation, the one reading of the piece that the group will give. What goes wrong is using rehearsal to learn parts, which is the conductor's oldest complaint ("learn it at home"), and assuming that competence alone transfers to competence together; it does not, because the unit of skill has changed from one person's control to the coordination of several. A group can also sound fine while hiding who has not learned what, which is a problem for anyone trying to tell what each member can do.

A film shoot is a rehearsal problem in this sense: the camera operator can practice the pan alone, but the pan has to answer an actor, a sound recordist, and a director's cue. So is a workflow in which one person's generated asset becomes another person's input, where the failure tends to happen at the handoff even when every individual output looks competent. That parallel is a proposal, not a finding.
