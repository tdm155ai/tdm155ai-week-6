title: Interpretation

Glenn Gould recorded Bach's Goldberg Variations in 1955 and again in 1981. The second is slower and quieter, different in character almost everywhere, and both are correct with respect to the score. That is what interpretation means. A score fixes the notes and leaves a great deal open: how fast, how loud relative to what came before, where a phrase breathes, which of three simultaneous voices to bring forward, how long to hold the silence before the last chord. Interpretation is the set of decisions a performer makes inside that space, and it is what people mean when they say two pianists played the same piece and it was not the same piece.

It cannot be drilled directly. There is no exercise for taste. It is learned by listening to many performances of the same passage and describing the differences without calling one right, by living with a piece for years, and by a teacher asking "do you hear what you are doing there?" until you do. But it rests on technique: you can only choose the phrasing you can execute, so every technical gain widens the space of readings available to you, and that is the real reason to practice scales. What goes wrong: "it's my interpretation" as a cover for a passage that is not under control, and the imitation of a famous recording mistaken for a reading of one's own.

This week, try the exercise the music research suggests: a short score, two performances of the same passage, and a description of what differs, with a model's description included as one more reading to evaluate rather than the answer. A model can offer you a plausible interpretation of anything. Yours is the one you can account for, decision by decision.
