title: Performance
description: Playing for an audience, where you never stop to fix anything; the mode whose rules are the opposite of the practice room's.

In the practice room you stop at every mistake. On stage you never stop. The wrong note is already gone, and the only thing that matters is the next one. Performance is a different activity from practice, with different rules, and musicians practice it as a separate skill: run-throughs with no stopping allowed, playing for friends, recording a take in one pass, a dress rehearsal in the hall where the concert will be. The hall, the lights, the adrenaline, and memory under pressure are all conditions the practice room does not supply, and the first performance of any piece teaches things no amount of practice could.

What performance trains is continuity, recovery, and communication: keeping going, covering the slip, playing to someone. What goes wrong runs in both directions. Students who practice only by running through the piece are performing in the practice room, and never fix anything; students who only ever stop and fix are practicing on stage, and freeze at the first slip. The learning research has a warning that rhymes with this. Soderstrom and Bjork distinguish learning from performance, using "performance" for how well you do during the session itself; their point is that a good run today is not evidence that you have learned the piece, and musicians know this version too: the run that went well on Tuesday and fell apart on Saturday.

In media the performance is the take, the live stream, the demo in front of the room. A generated artifact is a kind of performance with no practice underneath it, which is why it can be polished and still teach you nothing; the week's question is what you can do without stopping, when the tool is not running.
