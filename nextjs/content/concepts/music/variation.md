Mozart wrote twelve variations on the tune we know as "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star." Bach's Goldberg Variations are thirty pieces on one bass line; Beethoven's Diabelli Variations are thirty-three on a small waltz. In each, every variation keeps something (the harmony, the bass, the number of bars) and changes something else: the rhythm, the texture, the key from major to minor, the speed, the mood. The form is a study in its own right, an answer to the question of what one piece of material can become, and composers have used it for centuries as a way of working out their own technique in public.

The practice room has its own version. A hard passage is played "in rhythms" (long-short, then short-long), with different articulations, at several tempos, transposed into another key, hands swapped. The point is the same one the learning research measured: repeating a thing identically gives less for later use than repeating it with something changed, and musicians will tell you that a passage practiced ten ways is more reliable under pressure than the same passage practiced one way a hundred times. What goes wrong is varying before the plain version is secure, so that you have ten unstable versions instead of one stable one, and using variation as a way to avoid the one version that is hard.

A model will produce ten variations of anything in a few seconds. The composer's variations were the work; the model's are the output. What is left for you is the choosing, which is where the skill was all along: knowing which of the ten to keep and being able to say why.
