---
title: The lesson
description: The weekly meeting with a teacher who hears what the student can't; the feedback loop at the center of musical training.
author: claude
order: 10
---

Instrumental music is taught one-to-one. A student practices alone for six days and on the seventh plays for a teacher, who listens, stops them after eight bars, says "again, and this time listen to the bass," and at the end assigns the next week's work. The lesson is short compared with the practice it directs, and that is the design: its job is not to practice but to steer. The teacher diagnoses (the fourth finger is collapsing; you are not hearing the second voice), demonstrates, picks the next task, and sends the student back to the room. A good teacher is prized for what they can hear more than for what they can play.

The lesson is the clearest instance of a feedback loop in any discipline: observe, diagnose, intervene, re-test a week later. What the teacher adds, in Tim Ingold's phrase, is an education of attention: not instructions so much as showing the student where to listen, until the student can hear it unprompted. What goes wrong: the student practices for the lesson rather than for the piece, polishing a performance for Tuesday instead of fixing the problem; the teacher corrects everything and the student retains nothing, which is why experienced teachers pick one thing; and the student who cannot hear a mistake until the teacher points it out has not yet learned the part of the skill that matters most.

A model can play the teacher who is available every day instead of every week, and that changes the rhythm of the loop. But it hears only what you give it, and the aim of the lesson was always to make the teacher unnecessary. If you ask a model for feedback, ask it to withhold the answer and name only where to listen.
