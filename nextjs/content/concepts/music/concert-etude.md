title: The concert étude

Before Chopin, études mostly stayed in the practice room: Cramer's, Czerny's, Clementi's *Gradus ad Parnassum*, useful and rarely performed. Chopin's Op. 10 (1833), dedicated to Liszt, and his Op. 25 (1837) changed the category. Each piece still trains one technical figure, and each is also a complete work that a pianist would program in a recital. Liszt pushed further: his *Trois études de concert* say it in the title, and his *Transcendental Études* (final version 1852) are display pieces first and training pieces second. The line continues through Debussy's *Études* of 1915, dedicated to Chopin's memory, to Ligeti's, begun in 1985.

What the concert étude shows is that the study can be the art, and that the constraint (one figure, held to for the length of the piece) can make the music rather than merely the technique. The difference between an exercise and a piece turns out to be a matter of what is asked of the material, not the material itself. The concert étude is still a study: pianists use Chopin's to train, and the usual risks apply. A student can perform an étude before it is solved, so that the audience hears the problem instead of the piece, or can forget what the étude was for and treat the technical figure as decoration. The Lab's working distinction, which this form illustrates, is that an exercise trains a limited operation while a study makes that operation artistically consequential.

The concert étude is the model for this week's deliverable. The exercise isolates; the étude communicates; the concert étude is a study somebody else would want to watch. That is the target: a small piece built around one operation, where the operation is visible to you and invisible to the audience, and the piece works anyway.
