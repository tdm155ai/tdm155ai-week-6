---
title: Decomposition
description: Breaking a piece into the bar, the hand alone, the one hard shift, so each part can be practiced by itself and then put back.
author: claude
order: 6
---

A pianist learning a Bach fugue does not start by playing it. She plays hands separately, then one voice at a time, then two voices together, then the bar that goes wrong, then that bar with the bar before it. A cellist isolates a shift, the moment the hand slides up the string to a new position, and practices just the two notes on either side of it, back and forth, fifty times. Decomposition is the practice-room habit of cutting the problem down until it is small enough to solve: the piece into sections, the section into bars, the bar into one hand, the hand into one movement.

It works because a small problem gives clear feedback: you can hear whether the shift is in tune in a way you cannot when it is buried in a phrase. What goes wrong is that the parts, put back together, do not add up to the whole. Hands practiced separately can fight when combined; the seams between practiced chunks are where memory slips happen; and a skill that lives in coordination (the balance between two voices, say) cannot be found in either part alone. The research on part-whole practice says roughly this: practicing parts helps when the parts are fairly independent and hurts when the skill is in how they fit. Musicians handle the seams by overlapping units, starting a beat before the hard bar and ending a beat after, and by returning to the whole often.

Decomposition is also how you write instructions for a tool: break the job into steps a model can follow. What you learn from decomposing a piece is which step is the hard one, and that knowledge is lost when the decomposing is done for you.
