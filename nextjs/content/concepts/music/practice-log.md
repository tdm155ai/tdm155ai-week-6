title: The practice log
description: A record of what was practiced, toward what goal, with what result; the device that makes practice visible to the student and the teacher.

---

Many teachers send a student home with a notebook. For each day: what you practiced, for how long, what the goal was, what happened. The simplest version is a count of minutes, and it is nearly useless. The useful version records a target ("the shift in bar 12 in tune five times in a row"), the attempt, and what to change tomorrow. Conservatoires such as the Royal College of Music tell their students to set goals at several timescales and to make the short-term ones specific and measurable. The log turns practice, which disappears as it happens, into something that can be looked at the next day and brought to the lesson.

What the log trains is noticing, and a certain honesty: the difference between what was practiced and what was merely played through becomes visible on the page. What goes wrong is familiar from every kind of record-keeping. Minutes get counted as if minutes were learning. Students become fluent at writing plausible reflections without improving the passage. The logging can take longer than the practicing. And the written entry can lie in ways a recording can't, which is why the phone recording of the attempt is the other half of the log: evidence the entry has to answer to.

This week the log is the spine of the deliverable. For each attempt, record the goal, the attempt, your prediction of how it would go, the feedback you got, and the next change. Then record who did what: you, a peer, the teacher, the model. A log in which the model's contribution is unmarked is a log of the model's practice, and it will read as yours.
