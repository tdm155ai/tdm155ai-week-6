title: Automaticity
description: Muscle memory: a passage the hands can play while the head does something else, and the price of getting there.

A pianist who has memorized a piece can often play it while holding a conversation, and will sometimes find that thinking about the fingering makes it fall apart. Musicians call this muscle memory, though the memory is in the brain, not the muscles: a sequence repeated enough times that it runs without conscious control. Fitts and Posner described three stages of getting there. In the cognitive stage you think through each step; in the associative stage the steps smooth into each other and errors drop; in the autonomous stage the sequence runs by itself. Any professional's scales are autonomous. So is the drive home.

Automaticity is what practice is for: it frees attention. When the hands know the notes, the mind can attend to the sound, the phrasing, the other players. The price comes in two forms. What is automatic is hard to change, so the wrong fingering learned early stays for years, and relearning costs more than learning did. And an automatic sequence has no map of where it is: when a memory slip breaks the chain in performance, the conscious mind does not know what bar it was in, which is why musicians build a second, deliberate memory of the piece (the harmony, the starting points) as a backup to the hands. Ericsson added a third cost: once a skill is automatic you stop attending to it, and improvement stops, so that getting better means deliberately pulling the automatic thing back into attention.

A model's fluency looks like automaticity with no stages before it. For your own skills the question is which have reached the autonomous stage, and which have been skipped: delegated to a tool before the hands ever learned them.
