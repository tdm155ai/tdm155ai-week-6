In 1993 Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe, and Clemens Tesch-Römer asked violin students at a Berlin music academy to estimate how many hours they had practiced alone since they began. The students their teachers rated best reported the most, on the order of ten thousand hours by age twenty, and the authors argued that what mattered was not time with the instrument in general but *deliberate practice*: effortful work aimed at a specific weakness, designed with a teacher's help, not enjoyable in itself, and sustained for years. The paper gave skill research its vocabulary. It is also the source, by way of Malcolm Gladwell's *Outliers* (2008), of the "10,000-hour rule," a number Ericsson himself later said was never meant as a threshold.

The claim is disputed, and any use of it should say so. Macnamara, Hambrick, and Oswald's 2014 meta-analysis found that measured practice accounted for a minority of the differences in performance across domains, about a fifth in music; a 2016 analysis of sports found 18 percent overall and about 1 percent among elite performers. Ericsson and Harwell replied in 2019 that most of the studies pooled had not measured deliberate practice as originally defined, so the meta-analyses were counting something looser. Both sides agree that practice is not dispensable; what is in question is whether it is close to sufficient, and whether the strict kind can be measured at all. The original hours were retrospective estimates, which is one more reason to hold the numbers loosely.

For this week the working term is purposeful practice: a target, an attempt, usable evidence, another attempt. The musician's version of that loop is the lesson plus the practice room, and it is the loop to keep intact when a model offers to do the attempt for you.
