Open Hanon's *The Virtuoso Pianist* (1873) and you find sixty exercises that are almost not music: a five-finger pattern played up the keyboard and back down, then the same pattern starting one note higher, for pages. Nobody performs Hanon. It exists to make the fingers even and independent and makes no attempt to be anything else. That is the exercise in its pure form, a task set only in order to train, the physical sense of the word before writing teachers borrowed it. Scales and arpeggios, long tones on a wind instrument, a drummer's rudiments (the paradiddle), bowing patterns on an open string: all of these belong here.

The strength of an exercise is isolation. With nothing musical to attend to, all your attention goes to the mechanism: is the fourth finger as loud as the second, is the shift in tune, is the stroke even. Feedback is immediate and hard to argue with. The weakness is the same thing. An exercise trains a movement out of context, and a student who sounds like a machine on Hanon can still fall apart in a sonata, because in the piece the movement has to happen while you are thinking about something else. Teachers disagree about the dose. Some skip published exercises and extract their own from the repertoire, turning the hard bar of a real piece into an exercise by repeating it; others swear by a daily half hour.

When you set yourself an exercise this week, say what mechanism it isolates and what it leaves out. The [étude](etude.md) is the next step, where the mechanism has to serve something, and that is the step a model's output skips: it hands you the finished sound without the hours of mechanism underneath.
