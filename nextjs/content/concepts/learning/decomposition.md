title: Decomposition
description: Breaking a skill into parts that can be practiced alone, and what is lost when the parts go back together.

"Make a documentary" cannot be practiced. "Make the interview intelligible" is closer but is still several tasks. "Decide whether microphone placement or room sound is the main problem in these clips" can be practiced, checked, and practiced again. Decomposition is breaking a skill, or a task, or an area of expertise into parts small enough to work on alone. Every discipline does it: the pianist practices one hand, the dancer counts the phrase into steps, the athlete isolates the footwork, the illustrator builds the figure from cylinders and cubes. Hitchcock's breaking of an action into shots is the film version. It is the move that turns an impossible whole into a list of possible exercises.

The parts are easier to practice, easier to check, and easier to hand to a model. What they lose is the connection between them. Timing between parts, the coordination that makes the whole a whole, and the judgment about when each part is wanted are not located in any part and do not come back on their own when the parts are reassembled. A student who can do each move cleanly may still be unable to do the scene. This is why decomposition needs a return trip: isolate, practice, reassemble, and practice the reassembled whole, which is the subject of [part-whole practice](part-whole-practice.md). It also needs an honest map. One usable slice of a skill, plus a clear account of what remains outside the slice, is a stronger learning claim than either "I made a film" or "the model made it."

This week, decompose your own project into the skills it needs, pick one slice to practice, and write down which parts you are delegating. The map is a deliverable.
