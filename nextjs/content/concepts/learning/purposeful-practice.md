You want a smoother handheld push-in. You set the target (the foreground object stays steady while the background reveals the doorway), you shoot it, you watch the take and see that it drifts at the end, and you shoot it again with one change. That loop is purposeful practice: a target, an attempt, usable evidence, another attempt. It is the week's working term because it names what distinguishes practice from making. Making a film produces a film; purposeful practice produces a changed capacity, and the film is the by-product. The term is deliberately less strict than [deliberate practice](deliberate-practice.md), which in Ericsson's expertise research requires tasks designed by a teacher at the edge of the learner's ability, and more demanding than [repetition](repetition.md), which needs no target at all.

Each of the four parts does work. Without a target, you cannot tell whether an attempt improved. Without an attempt, there is nothing to learn from. Without usable evidence (a recording you can review, a comparison you can make), you are relying on how the attempt felt, which is unreliable. Without another attempt, the evidence changes nothing. The most common failure is skipping the third part: students make a second version without looking closely at the first. Ericsson and Pool's *Peak* uses the same phrase for practice that has goals, focus, and feedback but lacks an expert teacher's designed tasks; this week uses it in that spirit, without the claims about hours.

With AI the loop can be run by the model: it produces the attempt, judges it, and produces another. Purposeful practice for you requires that at least one of the four parts stays yours, and the one to keep first is the evidence: look at the attempt yourself before you ask the model what it thinks.
