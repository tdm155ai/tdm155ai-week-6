Benjamin Franklin taught himself to write by taking notes on essays from the *Spectator*, setting them aside for a few days, rewriting the essays from the notes, and comparing his version with the original. Jazz musicians learn solos by ear, note for note, off the record. Art students have copied in museums for centuries; dancers learn the combination by watching the front row. Imitation is learning by copying a model, and it is the oldest method in every discipline here. Its logic is that the model contains the skill in finished form, and copying forces you to attend to every decision in it, including the ones you would not have noticed by looking.

What copying trains is attention and a repertoire: you come out able to see what the original did and able to produce its moves. Franklin's method adds the step that matters, the comparison, which turns the copy into feedback about the difference between the model and you. What copying cannot train is choosing. The copied solo does not tell you what to play over the next tune; the copied sentence does not tell you what to say. Copying also fails when it is mechanical, when the hand moves without the attention, which is the difference between [copywork](../writing/copywork.md) and typing. And a learner who copies only one model acquires its limits along with its strengths.

A model that produces an image "in the style of" has done something like imitation without the learning. For you, the useful version is to copy something you admire by hand, compare, and name what you borrowed, which is also the honest way to use a reference with a generator.
