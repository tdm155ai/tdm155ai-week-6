A pianist practices scales; a doctor has a practice; a photographer keeps a daily practice; everyone is told that practice makes perfect. The word covers all of these, and they are related. "To practice" is to do a thing in order to become able to do it, as against doing it because it needs doing. "A practice" is a thing done habitually, a routine, and in the older professional sense the ongoing exercise of a craft (a law practice, a medical practice). The thread is the Greek root behind the word, *praktikos*, fit for action: practice is the doing side of knowing. For a student the first sense is the one that matters: the attempt whose purpose is the next attempt.

Practice comes in kinds, and the kinds train different things. [Repetition](repetition.md) of a fixed action builds speed and reliability. [Variation](variation.md) builds the ability to adjust. A [drill](drill.md) isolates a move; an [exercise](exercise.md) sets a task that trains one; a [study](study.md) tries to work something out while training; rehearsal puts the parts together under conditions close to performance. What they share is that each produces evidence (a recording, a drawing, a draft) that can be checked against a target. Practice without a target or without checking is time spent, and time spent is a poor predictor of skill. The strongest version of this claim, [deliberate practice](deliberate-practice.md), is disputed in the research literature; the weaker claim, that practice has to be aimed and checked to do much, is not.

The question this week asks of every kind of practice is who is doing the attempt. A model can run the routine, but it cannot practice for you: the capacity built by practicing stays in whoever made the attempts.
