A dancer runs the combination again, on the left side this time. A pianist plays the bar ten times. A camera operator does the same slow pan twenty times across the same room. Repetition is the plainest form of practice and the one every discipline in this reference has a version of. What it does is make an action cheaper: faster, more consistent, needing less attention, until the hands or the eye do it without being told. That is the mechanism by which the parts of a skill become [automatic](automaticity.md), which frees attention for the parts that can't be.

Repetition is indifferent to what it repeats. Do a thing wrong twenty times and you will have a reliable wrong thing, harder to fix than it was the first time, because it is no longer being chosen. This is why the disciplines surround repetition with checks: the music teacher who stops the student at the bar, the coach who watches the form, the recording that gets played back. It is also why identical repetition is weaker than it feels. Research on motor and cognitive learning finds that repeating with something [changed](variation.md) each time, or mixing the action with others, produces worse sessions and better retention than blocked repetition of the same thing. And repetition has a ceiling: once a move is automatic, more repetition at the same level maintains it without improving it, which is the [plateau](plateau.md).

This week's assignments ask for twenty or fifty repetitions of a single AV maneuver, recorded, so they can be seen side by side. The number is not the point; the comparison is. Look at the first and the twentieth and say what changed.
