title: Sketch
description: Fast, cheap, exploratory; what a sketch is for and how it differs from a study.

Ten thumbnail frames in two minutes to see whether the scene reads better from the doorway or the window. A paragraph dashed off to find out whether the essay has a shape. Forty bars of a melody in a pocket notebook. A sketch is fast, cheap, and exploratory, and all three properties are the point. Because it costs little, you can make many; because you can make many, you can compare; because it is exploratory, it is allowed to be wrong. The word comes from Italian *schizzo*, with the sense of something done on the spot. Beethoven's sketchbooks and Leonardo's notebooks are the famous archives, and they show the form at work: alternatives tried and discarded, the thinking visible because it was never cleaned up.

A sketch and a [study](study.md) are both small and both come before the work, but they answer different questions. The sketch asks "which?" (which framing, which order, which opening) and explores a space; the study asks "how?" (how does the hand hold the cup) and goes deep on one problem. The sketch trains fluency and range: the ability to generate options quickly and see them side by side. What it cannot train is finish, and what goes wrong is treating a sketch as a draft to be polished rather than a question to be answered. Sketching also fails when it becomes precious: the moment a sketch takes too long to throw away, it has stopped doing its job.

Generative models are sketch machines; they make many cheap variants fast. That makes the selection among sketches the part that stays yours, and selection is a skill practiced only by selecting.
