Before painting a figure, a painter draws the hand alone, three times, from different angles, to work out how the fingers hold the cup. That drawing is a study. So is a thirty-second video made to find out whether one cut can carry a change of mood, and so is Chopin's Op. 10 No. 3, an étude (French for study) built around one technical problem. A study is a piece of work made to work something out. It differs from a [drill](drill.md) because it has a question, not just a target, and from a [sketch](sketch.md) because it is sustained: you stay with the problem until you have learned what you came for.

The root ties the senses together. Latin *studium* means zeal, eagerness, devoted application; *studere*, to be eager, to apply oneself. English *study* came through Anglo-French *estudie*; French *étude* descends from the same Latin. So the painter's study, the musician's étude, and the student's studying share a word for sustained attention, even though the practices differ. What a study trains is judgment about a specific problem; its product is knowledge you can use in the next piece, and sometimes the study turns out better than the finished work it was for. What goes wrong is a study without a question: a piece that is small and preparatory but is not trying to find anything out, which is only a small piece. An [exercise](exercise.md) can get by on a target; a study needs the question.

This week is named for studies. Each one you make should be able to state its question in a sentence and its result in another, whether the model made the artifact or you did.
