In 1993 Anders Ericsson and colleagues asked violin students at a Berlin academy to estimate how much they had practiced alone since childhood. The best students reported the most: around ten thousand hours by age twenty. From this and similar evidence Ericsson argued that expert performance is explained less by talent than by deliberate practice: effortful, structured work on tasks just beyond current ability, designed by a teacher, with feedback and repetition. Malcolm Gladwell turned the number into the "10,000-hour rule," which is not something Ericsson claimed. The hours in the study were an estimate and an average, not a threshold.

The claim has been disputed, and any entry that leans on it should say so. Macnamara, Hambrick, and Oswald's 2014 meta-analysis found that measured deliberate practice explained a modest share of the differences in performance across domains; Macnamara, Moreau, and Hambrick's 2016 analysis of sports put it at about 18 percent overall and close to zero among elite performers. Ericsson and Harwell replied in 2019 that most of the studies counted were not measuring deliberate practice as he had defined it. The disagreement is partly about definitions and partly about what a correlation across people can say about the practice of any one person. What survives the dispute: there is no recipe of hours, and practice is not therefore dispensable. Structured, checked practice remains the only known route to skill; how much of the difference between individuals it explains is the open question.

For this week, the strict term is a reference point and the working term is [purposeful practice](purposeful-practice.md). Use "deliberate practice" when you mean teacher-designed tasks with feedback, and hedge when you cite the numbers.
