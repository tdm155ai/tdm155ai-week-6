A sound recordist who hears, before anyone else in the room, that the dialogue will be unusable because the refrigerator is humming has a skill. So does the editor who sees that a cut lands one frame late, and the photographer who knows which of forty frames to print. In each case the skill is not only the action (moving the mic, trimming the cut) but the noticing that comes before it. The word keeps that order. English *skill* comes from Old Norse *skil*, distinction, discernment, and the related verb *skilja*, to separate, to tell apart, to understand. Knowing was telling things apart before it was doing things well. The working definition this week uses: a developed capacity to notice relevant differences, make decisions, and act competently within a particular task or situation.

Defining skill this way changes what [practice](practice.md) is for. If a skill were only execution, a tool that executes would replace it. But the noticing is built the same way the hands are, by attempts that are checked, and nothing else builds it. A student who has a model produce twenty cuts has not practiced seeing which one is right unless they do that part themselves. This is what [cognitive offloading](../learning-with-ai/cognitive-offloading.md) threatens: not the output, which gets better, but the discernment, which only grows in the person doing the discriminating. A skill is also particular. Being able to judge a mix does not make you able to judge a storyboard; the differences that matter are different ones.

This week, name the skill you are practicing at the level of a noticing: not "editing" but "hearing when a cut interrupts a gesture." Then ask what in your exercise trains that noticing, and what the model does instead of you.
