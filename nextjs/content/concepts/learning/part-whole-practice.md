A swimmer drills the kick with a board, then the arms with a pull buoy, then swims. A film crew rehearses the camera move alone, then the actor's cross alone, then the two together. Part-whole practice is the question of how to combine these: whether to break a task into parts and practice them separately, practice the whole from the start, or follow some sequence of the two. The research on part-task training, much of it from flight and military training, gives a rough answer. Part practice helps when the parts are relatively independent and the difficulty lies in the parts; whole practice is better when the task is highly organized and the difficulty lies in the timing and coordination between parts, because that coordination can only be practiced whole.

Wightman and Lintern distinguished three ways to make a part: segmentation (practice the first section, then add the next), fractionation (practice two components separately that normally occur together, like the hands in piano), and simplification (practice the whole with something made easier, like a slower tempo). Fractionation is the risky one, because the components may interact in ways that practicing them apart does not prepare for. The general rule: when you isolate a part, plan the reassembly, and expect the first whole attempt after part practice to be worse than you hoped. A theater company's schedule encodes the answer, alternating scene work (parts) with run-throughs (the whole).

For an AI-assisted project, the parts you delegate and the parts you practice yourself are a [decomposition](decomposition.md) too, and the whole you have to be able to do is the integration. Practice that.
