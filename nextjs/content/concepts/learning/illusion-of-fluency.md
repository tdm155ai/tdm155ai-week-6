---
title: Illusion of fluency
description: Why rereading and watching feel like learning; recognition is not recall.
author: claude
order: 26
---

You watch a tutorial on color grading twice and it all makes sense. You open the software and cannot do the first step. You reread your notes until every line looks familiar, and the exam asks you to produce what you can only recognize. The illusion of fluency is the feeling that you know something because it is easy to process when it is in front of you. Familiarity is mistaken for ability. Rereading, highlighting, and watching demonstrations produce it reliably, which is why Dunlosky's review rates rereading and highlighting as low-utility techniques despite their being the most popular: they make the material fluent without making you able to produce it.

The underlying confusion is between recognition and recall. Recognizing the right answer when shown it is a weak test that almost anything passes; producing it from nothing is the test that predicts what you can do later. The illusion persists because the feeling of knowing is generated by ease of processing, and ease is exactly what rereading creates. Bjork's group calls the family of such mistakes illusions of competence, and the remedy is a test that cannot be passed by familiarity: close the material and produce it, wait, and try again under changed conditions ([retrieval practice](retrieval-practice.md)). The illusion also explains why [desirable difficulties](desirable-difficulty.md) get abandoned; they feel like not learning, and the smooth session feels like learning.

A model's output is maximally fluent. Reading a good explanation it produced will feel like understanding, and the test is the same as always: can you reproduce the reasoning, or the move, without it?
