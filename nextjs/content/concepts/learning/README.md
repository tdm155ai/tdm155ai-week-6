Every field in this reference has its own word for the small, repeatable piece of work that builds a skill: the étude, the study, the drill, the take, the combination. This category is the account underneath them. It is about how a person comes to be able to do something they could not do before, and it is written for a week in which that question has a new edge. A model can now produce a documentary cut, a storyboard, a working script, or a 3D scene from one sentence. If the work can be delegated, why practice at all? The answer this category gives is that practice was never only a way of producing the work. It is how the capacity to notice, decide, and act gets built in a person, and that capacity does not transfer by delegation.

## The unit of practice

The unit is an attempt small enough to repeat and inspect. A [skill](skill.md), in the sense this week uses, is a developed capacity to notice relevant differences, decide, and act well inside a task, and nobody acquires one by doing the whole task once. They take a slice (one camera move, one transition, one paragraph opening) and do it again under conditions where the result can be checked. The slice goes by different names: an [exercise](exercise.md) when the training is the whole point, a [study](study.md) when the attempt is also meant to work something out, a [drill](drill.md) when one move is repeated until it is reliable. What makes any of them practice rather than production is that the point of the attempt is the next attempt.

## What is repeated and what varies

[Repetition](repetition.md) is the mechanism, and it is more complicated than doing the same thing many times. Repeating an action makes it faster and less effortful, which is what you want for the parts that should become automatic, and exactly what you don't want for an error, which repetition bakes in just as efficiently. The research on motor and cognitive learning points the same way from several directions: practice that [varies](variation.md) something each time, that [mixes](interleaving.md) problem types, that is [spread out](spacing.md) rather than massed, tends to produce less impressive sessions and more durable skill. The most useful distinction of the week is Soderstrom and Bjork's, between [learning and performance](learning-vs-performance.md): how well you do during practice is a poor guide to what you will be able to do next week, and the conditions that make a session feel smooth often do less for the skill than the conditions that make it feel hard.

## Where feedback comes from

Nothing improves without information about the result, and the kinds of [feedback](feedback.md) differ in what they require. The outcome (the shot is shaky, the mix is muddy) is often visible to the learner. The diagnosis (you rushed the pan; the mic was too far away) requires someone who can see the cause, and the intervention (try it slower; move the mic and compare) requires knowing what to change. Much of what a [teacher](teaching.md) or [coach](coaching.md) adds is the second and third kind, plus something harder to name: pointing the learner's attention at the difference that matters, which Ingold calls the [education of attention](education-of-attention.md). A learner can also feed back to themselves, by predicting a result before the attempt and comparing afterward, which is the practical form of [metacognition](metacognition.md) this week asks for, and by keeping a [practice log](practice-log.md) that can be played back.

## What can't be drilled

Drills isolate. That is their power and their limit. A skill broken into parts ([decomposition](decomposition.md)) can be practiced part by part, but the coordination between the parts, the timing of the whole, and the judgment about when to use the move at all are not in any of the parts. Some of what an expert knows cannot be written down even by the expert ([tacit knowledge](tacit-knowledge.md)); it is passed along by working beside someone who has it. And no amount of repetition on one task guarantees [transfer](transfer.md) to another: what you practiced in one piece of software, one scene, one medium may or may not show up elsewhere, and the honest claim is the specific one.

## What AI changes and what it leaves

AI changes who does the attempt. When a model produces the cut, the student gets a result without the attempt, and the feedback loop goes slack: there is an outcome to judge but no action of one's own to diagnose. The deliberate-practice literature is disputed (Ericsson's 1993 study of violinists, the meta-analyses by Macnamara and colleagues, Ericsson's reply), and this category does not lean on any recipe of hours. What is not disputed is that skill builds through attempts that are checked and tried again, and that the capacity to judge an output is itself a skill built the same way. Repetition, purposeful practice, and skill development still matter when a model can do the work, because they are how you become someone who can tell whether the work is good, say why, and do the part that should stay yours. The question to carry into every exercise this week, taken up in [Learning with AI](../learning-with-ai/README.md): what are you learning when you do it, and what are you learning when Claude does it?
