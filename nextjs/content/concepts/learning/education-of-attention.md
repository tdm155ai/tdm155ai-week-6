---
title: Education of attention
description: Ingold's account: skill is passed on by showing where to look, not by transmitting instructions.
author: claude
order: 30
---

A sound engineer stands behind a student at the console and says, "Hear that? Right there, when the vocal comes in." The student didn't hear it. Then, after a few more times, they do, and can't stop hearing it. Nothing was transmitted; no rule was handed over that the student could have read instead. What happened is what the anthropologist Tim Ingold, borrowing the phrase from the psychologist James Gibson, calls the education of attention. Skill, on this account, does not pass between generations as a stock of representations copied from one head into another. It is grown in a person through engagement with an environment, under the guidance of someone who shows where to look and what to notice, until the learner can perceive differences they could not perceive before.

This changes what [teaching](teaching.md) is. The teacher's main work is not stating rules but directing attention: pointing, demonstrating, setting up the situation in which the relevant difference becomes visible. It also changes what counts as evidence of learning. The sign is not that you can repeat the instruction but that you notice something new in a new case (a mismatch between a cut and a gesture, a shift in implied certainty, a transition that doesn't hold) and can act on it. Ingold's account is a challenge to any written procedure, including a skill file. A rule that says "check continuity" directs attention in words; it does not make the learner able to see which continuity problem matters in this scene. Receiving the procedure is a different event from acquiring the perception.

A useful question for every study this week: what did it make perceptible that you could not see before? Annotate a new case to find out.
