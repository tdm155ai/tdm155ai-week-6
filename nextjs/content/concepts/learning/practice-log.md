After each attempt, a line: the time, the target, what help you had, which version this is, what you noticed, and what you'll change. After a sequence, a short note on what you can now see or do that you couldn't. That is a practice log. Music teachers have assigned them for generations, and conservatory guidance still asks that practice have a goal and a criterion rather than a count. The log's first job is to be an external memory: attempts blur together within an hour, and a learner cannot compare the first take to the twentieth without a record of what the first take was for.

Its second job is to be evidence that can be played back. A log that holds predictions and results makes learning inspectable: you can find the moment a diagnosis changed ("I thought it was timing; now I think it was the camera angle"), which is the clearest evidence of [regulation](metacognition.md) there is. The log goes wrong in two ways. Over-instrumentation: if logging takes longer than practicing, the record becomes a performance for the instructor, and one representative comparison with a short explanation beats fifty checkboxes. And false certainty: a system that rewards only correct predictions teaches students to rewrite the record. Preserve the wrong guesses. A log should also keep attribution. Student observation, teacher suggestion, and model proposal should stay distinguishable, or the student will later read the model's explanation as something they understood themselves.

This week's assignments build staged [repetition](repetition.md) that you log, and the record is played back to you, so you can see a change that was hard to notice while making it. The log is a deliverable, and the attribution column is its most important one.
