A math textbook shows a problem solved step by step before asking you to solve one. A film class watches a scene with the director's shot list beside it, so you can see the decisions and their result together. A worked example is a finished solution with its steps shown, studied rather than attempted. Sweller's research on cognitive load found that novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving equivalent problems, because solving a problem you don't know how to solve spends attention on search that could go to understanding the method. The example gives the method directly.

The effect reverses with expertise. Kalyuga and colleagues found that once learners know the method, worked examples become redundant and problem solving teaches more; the step-by-step walkthrough that helped the beginner slows the intermediate down. So the worked example is a stage, not a method, and the useful sequence is examples first, then faded examples with steps missing, then problems, which is [scaffolding](scaffolding.md) in miniature. A worked example also only teaches if it is studied: reading a solution and nodding is the [illusion of fluency](illusion-of-fluency.md), and the check is to close it and reproduce the steps. In the disciplines the form is everywhere: the master class, the annotated draft, the side-by-side edit, the solved étude on a recording.

A model's output is a worked example of a kind, a finished solution, but usually without the steps. Asking for the steps, then closing the output and reproducing it, turns it into one. The prompt-side version, examples placed in the prompt for the model to learn from, is [few-shot](../learning-with-ai/few-shot.md).
