title: Technique
description: The codified way of doing a thing that a field has agreed can be taught and drilled.

Ballet has five positions of the feet. Audio engineering has gain staging. Cinematography has the 180-degree rule, and film editing has the cut on action. Each is a piece of technique: a way of doing a thing that a field has worked out, named, and agreed can be taught to anyone willing to drill it. The word is Greek *technē*, craft, the knowledge of how to make. Technique is what makes a field teachable at all. Because it is codified, it can be demonstrated, broken into parts, practiced out of context, and checked against a standard; a teacher can say "your elbow is dropping" because the field has decided where the elbow goes.

Technique is also where the arguments are. Every codified technique was once one person's way of doing it, and a field's techniques carry its history and its exclusions (ballet's positions, Graham's contractions, and Cunningham's torso are three codifications of what a body is for). Technique trains reliability and gives a shared vocabulary; what it cannot train is what to do with it. In music the gap has a name: [interpretation](../music/interpretation.md) is what remains after the technique is solved. And technique can be overrated. A coach's "form" can become a target in itself, pursued past the point where it helps the result, and a student can learn to produce a textbook move that doesn't fit the scene in front of them.

Models have absorbed a great deal of technique, which is why their output is competent by default. What you learn from [drilling](drill.md) technique yourself is not only the move but the standard: the ability to see when the model's competent default is the wrong choice here.
