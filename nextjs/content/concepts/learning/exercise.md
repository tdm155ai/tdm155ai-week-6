A writing teacher asks for a paragraph with no adjectives. A piano teacher assigns Hanon. A photography assignment: fifty frames with one fixed lens on one city block. An exercise is a task set in order to train, and the setting is what distinguishes it from work that happens to train you. Someone (a teacher, a book, or you) has chosen a constraint so that one capacity gets worked while the rest is held still. The word comes from Latin *exercere*, to keep at work, to drive on, and the physical sense is still the plainest: an exercise works a muscle by giving it a load it would not otherwise meet. The cognitive and artistic senses borrow the picture.

What an exercise trains is whatever the constraint isolates, and what it can't train is whatever the constraint removes. The adjective-free paragraph trains verbs and nouns; it does not train knowing when an adjective earns its place. The exercise is also, by design, not the work. In music the distinction is explicit: an [exercise](../music/exercise.md) (scales, Hanon) has no musical pretense, while an [étude](../music/etude.md) is an exercise that has been composed into a piece, a technical problem that is also music. The étude is the exercise's most ambitious form, and the fact that Chopin's are played in concert shows that a training task can also be the thing itself. In writing, the exercise and the [study](study.md) blur the same way.

A good exercise this week has a stated constraint, a stated capacity it works, and an artifact you can compare across attempts. If you cannot say what the constraint isolates, it is an assignment, not an exercise.
