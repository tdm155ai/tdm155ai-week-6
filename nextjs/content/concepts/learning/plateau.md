You improved fast for a month and then stopped. The handheld shots look the same as they did three weeks ago; the mixes sound fine, and no better. The plateau is the flat stretch in a learning curve where continued practice produces no visible gain. It is one of the oldest observations in the study of skill: Bryan and Harter found it in telegraph operators in the 1890s, who improved, stalled for weeks, and then improved again, and they proposed that the stall was the time it took for lower-level habits to become automatic enough to build on.

Ericsson's reading is sharper and more useful for practice. A plateau, he argues, is what happens when a skill becomes [automatic](automaticity.md) at an acceptable level: the attention that was driving improvement moves elsewhere, and the action runs on its own, maintained but not developed. Most adults drive, type, and cook on a plateau reached years ago. The way off is not more of the same practice but a change in it: pulling the action back into attention, raising the target past what is comfortable, isolating the part that is limiting the whole and drilling that, or changing the conditions so that the automatic version fails and has to be rebuilt. The expertise research this draws on is disputed in its claims about how much practice explains, and the plateau mechanism should be taken as Ericsson's account rather than settled fact; the practical advice, change something, survives either way. A [coach's](coaching.md) job is largely to see which something.

With a model the plateau is invisible, because the output keeps improving as the models do. Yours does not. If your own attempts have gone flat, the next exercise should make the automatic version break.
