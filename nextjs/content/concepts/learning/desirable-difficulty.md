---
title: Desirable difficulty
description: Bjork's term for conditions that slow the session and improve retention.
author: claude
order: 20
---

The study tactic that feels best, rereading the chapter until it looks familiar, is among the least effective; the one that feels worst, closing the book and trying to write down what it said, is among the most. Robert Bjork called the second kind of condition a desirable difficulty: something that makes practice harder and slower and makes what is learned last longer and travel farther. The known ones are [spacing](spacing.md) practice out instead of massing it, [interleaving](interleaving.md) different problems instead of blocking them, [testing yourself](retrieval-practice.md) instead of restudying, generating an answer before being shown it, and varying the conditions of practice.

The common mechanism is that each one forces retrieval or reconstruction rather than recognition, and the effort of reconstructing is what strengthens the memory or the skill. The difficulty is desirable only when the learner can meet it: a difficulty beyond what you can do with effort is a failure, and the right level moves as you improve. The word also has a trap. Not every difficulty is desirable; a confusing interface, a bad recording, or an unclear assignment is only difficulty. The test is whether the hard thing is the thing you are trying to learn. And because desirable difficulties make the session look worse, learners who judge their practice by how it feels abandon them, which is the [illusion of fluency](illusion-of-fluency.md) at work.

A model's great convenience is that it removes difficulty, and it removes the desirable kind with the rest. Before asking it, do the hard part once yourself: predict the result, make the first attempt, write the first draft of the instruction. Then let it help.
