"The dialogue is hard to hear." "The microphone was too far from the speaker." "Move it closer, record the same line, and compare." These are three different kinds of feedback, and they require three different capacities. The first names the outcome and needs only ears. The second is a diagnosis: it proposes a cause, and needs someone who can see the cause behind the result, which is often the learner's blind spot. The third is an intervention, a proposed change and a way to test it. Motor-learning research makes a related split between knowledge of results (did the ball go in?) and knowledge of performance (what did the movement look like?), and finds that learners can usually see the first and need help with the second.

Feedback is what makes [repetition](repetition.md) into practice; without it, attempts accumulate without improving. But more is not better. The guidance hypothesis in motor learning holds that feedback given on every attempt produces fast improvement in the session and worse retention, because the learner comes to depend on it instead of building their own sense of the result. Feedback also has to be usable: specific enough to act on, close enough in time, and about the thing being practiced. And a fluent diagnosis is not a correct one. A useful [practice log](practice-log.md) writes down the observation before the explanation, so that a wrong diagnosis can be found later without losing the evidence.

A model gives fluent feedback of all three kinds on request. Its diagnoses are plausible and unverified. Keep the outcome observation yours, treat the model's diagnosis as a hypothesis, and test the intervention by comparison rather than taking it on trust.
