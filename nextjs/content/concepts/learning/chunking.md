---
title: Chunking
description: Grouping into units that memory and the hands can hold; phrases, moves, patterns.
author: claude

---

You cannot hold the string 1 4 9 2 1 7 7 6 2 0 0 1 as twelve digits, but you can hold it as three dates. A pianist does not memorize a sonata note by note; they hold it as phrases, and phrases as sections. A chess master who glances at a mid-game position reproduces it from memory, while a beginner cannot, because the master sees a dozen familiar configurations where the beginner sees twenty-five pieces. Chunking is grouping elements into units that memory and the hands can hold as one thing. It is what expertise looks like from the inside: not a bigger memory but a bigger vocabulary of units.

Chunks are built by [repetition](repetition.md), which is why they are a product of practice and not a shortcut around it. The chess finding, from Chase and Simon, has a second half: shown a random arrangement of pieces, the master remembers no better than the beginner. The chunks are specific to the patterns the field produces, and they do not transfer. What chunking trains is fluency and perception: the ability to read a situation at a glance and to execute a sequence without attending to each step. What goes wrong is a chunk built around an error, which is then repeated as a unit, and the difficulty of taking a chunk apart once it has set. The dancer who learns the phrase with one wrong count must unlearn the phrase, not the count.

When a model produces a sequence for you, you get the output without the chunk. The sign that you have the chunk is that you can produce or recognize the unit yourself, without the model, at speed.
