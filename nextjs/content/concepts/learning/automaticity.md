When you first learned to drive, every action took attention: the mirror, the pedals, the turn signal. Now you drive while talking, and if someone asks what your feet are doing you have to think. The action has become automatic. Fitts and Posner described skill learning in three stages. In the cognitive stage you figure out what to do, slowly, with errors, talking yourself through it. In the associative stage the pieces link up and the errors drop. In the autonomous stage the action runs with little attention and is hard to describe, which is what musicians mean by muscle memory and what makes a touch typist unable to say where the letters are.

Automaticity is the goal of [drill](drill.md) and the reason [repetition](repetition.md) works: it moves an action out of attention so that attention is free for what can't be automated, the decisions, the listening, the scene. Its cost is that an automatic action is hard to change. The hands have stopped asking. This is why Ericsson argues that automaticity is the enemy of continued improvement: once performance is adequate and automatic, further practice maintains it without improving it, and getting better requires deliberately pulling the action back into attention and redesigning it. It is also why a wrong move, once automatic, takes more work to fix than it took to learn.

When a model does the task, nothing in you becomes automatic. That is sometimes fine; you don't need automatic color grading to judge a grade. But for the moves you want to make in the room, under time pressure, with people waiting, only your own repetitions will get them past the first stage.
