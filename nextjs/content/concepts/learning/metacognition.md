Before you export the new cut, you write one sentence: "This version will make the speaker's point clearer because the reaction shot now comes before the line." You export it, watch both versions, and check whether the sentence was right. That small routine is metacognition in use: thinking about your own thinking, and acting on it. *How People Learn II* describes it as monitoring and regulating one's own cognitive processes, and treats it as a major factor in how well people learn. Flavell, who named it, split it into knowledge about your own cognition and the regulation of it.

Three questions keep the parts apart. Confidence: how likely do I think I am to succeed? Knowledge of process: which steps or decisions do I think matter? Regulation: what will I do differently, given the evidence? They can come apart. A student can feel confident because a tool returns polished output and know nothing about which decision made it good; another can feel uncertain while getting measurably better at diagnosing what is wrong. Metacognition is not the reflective essay written afterward; a fine account of growth can coexist with none. It is useful when it governs an action, and the classroom move that makes it governable is predict-then-compare: an accountable forecast before the attempt, a check against the result after, and a changed strategy next time. Being wrong is evidence of regulation, not failure, as long as the record keeps the prediction.

This week's framing problem is that AI hides the division of labor: it is not obvious what you did and what the model did. The metacognitive move that answers it is to write that division down for each study, before and after.
