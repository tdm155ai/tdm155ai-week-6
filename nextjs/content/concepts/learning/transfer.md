You learn to cut dialogue cleanly in one editing program. Does the skill show up in a different program? In a scene with no dialogue? In a podcast edit? In deciding where a paragraph should break? Transfer is the question of whether what you practiced in one situation appears in another, and the answer is usually "less than you'd hope, and it depends on the distance." Near transfer, to a situation much like the practiced one (the same cut in a new program), is common. Far transfer, to a situation that shares a principle but little else (the editing sense applied to prose), is rare and hard to produce on purpose. The old hope that studying Latin or logic would train the mind in general did not survive testing.

What helps, according to the research summarized in *How People Learn*, is learning the thing well in the first place, understanding rather than memorizing, practicing in [varied](variation.md) contexts so the principle gets separated from its first setting, and being aware of what you know and when it applies. Transfer is hard because a skill is encoded with its conditions, and recognizing that a new situation calls for it is a separate act from being able to do it. This is why identical repetition in one setting is weaker than it feels, and why a skill drilled out of context may fail to appear when the context returns.

Name transfer carefully. Succeeding on a new example in the same software is narrower than recognizing the same problem in a new medium; both are useful, and neither is mastery. A study this week should state the exact variation it attempts rather than claim a skill in general.
