---
title: Interleaving
description: Mixing problem types in one session; why it feels worse and tests better; conditional, not universal.
author: claude
order: 23
---

Kornell and Bjork showed students paintings by twelve artists. One group saw each artist's paintings together, in a block; the other saw them shuffled. Asked later to name the painter of paintings they had not seen, the shuffled group did better, and most students in both groups believed blocking had helped them more. Interleaving is mixing different kinds of problems in one session instead of practicing one kind at a time. In math it means mixing problem types so you have to decide which method applies; in sport it is the difference between forty serves and a rotation of serves, volleys, and returns.

It works, when it works, because choosing which technique a case calls for is itself a skill, and blocked practice removes the choice: when every problem in the set is a continuity problem, you never have to recognize one. Interleaving feels worse because the session is slower and messier, and it tests better because the discrimination got practiced. The hedge matters. Dunlosky's review gave interleaving moderate rather than strong support; the benefit depends on the problem types being confusable and on the learner being past the point of being unable to do any of them. Beginners sometimes need a block to get one move stable before mixing. Choosing the move and executing the move are different acts, and a good practice record keeps them apart.

This week's version: instead of a set of clips that all have the same problem, take several with different problems and decide, for each, what needs attention. A wrong technique applied beautifully should stay distinguishable from the right one applied badly.
