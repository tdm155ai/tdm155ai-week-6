A basketball team runs layup lines. A typist repeats "asdf jkl;". A student editor cuts the same two-shot exchange on action twenty times. A drill takes one move out of the activity it belongs to and repeats it until it is reliable, usually fast, usually with the conditions simplified so that the move is all there is to attend to. The word's modern sense comes from soldiers: Dutch *drillen*, to train troops by repeated movements, and the military drill is still the clearest picture of what the form is for. It makes an action dependable under conditions in which thinking about it would be too slow.

The drill's strength is isolation; so is its blind spot. Because the move is taken out of context, the drill cannot teach when to use it. A player who has drilled a crossover a thousand times still has to read the defender; an editor who can cut on action in their sleep still has to decide that this exchange wants a cut on action and not a hold. Drills also simplify the conditions, which means the reliable version may fall apart when the conditions return, a concern the research on [part-whole practice](part-whole-practice.md) takes seriously. And a drilled error is a reliable error. Drills need a check at the start (is the move right?) and a return to the whole at the end (does it work in the game, the scene, the mix?).

Drills are the easiest exercises to hand to a model and the easiest to design for one. The question for you is which moves you want reliable in your own hands, so that your attention is free for the decisions the drill cannot make.
