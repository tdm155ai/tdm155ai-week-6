A beginner edits with a template: the structure is given, and they fill the slots. Next time the template has gaps. Later there is no template, and they build the structure themselves. That withdrawal is scaffolding. The term comes from Wood, Bruner, and Ross, who watched tutors help children build a block pyramid and described what the tutor did: simplifying the task, keeping the child on it, marking the critical features, demonstrating, and doing the parts the child couldn't, so that the child could complete a task that was beyond them alone. The idea rests on Vygotsky's zone of proximal development, the range of tasks a learner cannot do alone but can do with help, which is where instruction has the most to work with.

The scaffold's defining property is that it comes down. Support that stays is not scaffolding; it is a crutch, and the learner's competence has been replaced rather than grown. So the design question is always the fading schedule: which support is withdrawn, when, and on what evidence. [Worked examples](worked-example.md) that lose steps, templates that lose fields, a tempo that rises, a coach who speaks less, are all fading. What goes wrong is support pitched outside the zone (too much, and nothing is learned; too little, and the task fails) and support that never fades because the output is good with it in place.

A model is a scaffold of unusual power, and it has no fading schedule unless you give it one. For a capacity you want to keep, plan the withdrawal: what will you do with less help next week, and what evidence will tell you the support can come down?
