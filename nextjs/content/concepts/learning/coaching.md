The coach watches the serve, says "your toss drifted left," has you toss ten times without hitting, and then watches the serve again. The dance teacher touches your shoulder blade and says "here"; the director gives notes after the run; the music teacher stops you at the bar. Coaching is this loop, observe, diagnose, intervene, re-test, run by someone who can see what the learner can't. The coach's distinctive asset is the outside view: you cannot watch your own serve, hear your own voice as the room hears it, or see your own blocking from the house. Each discipline has institutionalized the loop and named its unit: the correction in dance, the note in theater, the lesson in music.

What the coach trains is whatever the loop is pointed at, and the pointing is the skill. A good coach picks the one thing to change now, from the many things that are wrong, and makes it testable, so that the next attempt can show whether the diagnosis was right. A bad coach gives everything at once, or gives diagnoses without interventions, or confuses a fluent explanation with a true one. The research on [feedback](feedback.md) adds a caution for the learner: a coach who corrects every attempt produces fast gains and dependence; the aim is to work yourself out of needing the correction by building your own sense of the result. [Film study](../athletics/film-study.md), the athlete's recording, is the coach's loop with the outside view supplied by a camera.

A model can run the loop as a [practice partner](../learning-with-ai/practice-partner.md), and its observations of a recording or a draft can be useful. Its diagnoses are hypotheses. Use it to organize comparisons and ask questions; keep the prediction and the decision about what to change in your own hands.
