Ask an experienced sound engineer how they know where to put the microphone and they will tell you some rules, and then they will say "and then you listen." Ask a good editor why the cut goes there and the answer, after the reasons run out, is "because it does." Michael Polanyi named this: "we can know more than we can tell." Tacit knowledge is the part of a skill that the person who has it cannot fully put into words, even when they try, and even when they are good teachers. Recognizing a face is his plain example: you know thousands of faces and could not describe one well enough for a stranger to pick it out of a crowd.

Tacit knowledge is not mystical; it is what repeated attempts with feedback produce when the relevant differences are too fine or too many to name. It is passed on by demonstration, by working beside someone ([apprenticeship](apprenticeship.md)), and by having your attention pointed at the right thing until you see it, which is why apprenticeship persists in fields that have textbooks. What it means for practice is that reading the rules is not having the skill, and that a learner who can state every rule may still be in the first stage. What it means for this week is more pointed. An [agent skill](../learning-with-ai/agent-skill.md) is a written procedure. Whatever part of your judgment you cannot write down will not be in the file, and the model will substitute its own default there. Writing the file is a good way to find out which part that is.

A useful exercise: write the instructions for something you can do well, give them to someone who can't, and watch where they fail. The failures mark the tacit part.
