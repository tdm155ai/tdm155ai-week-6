title: Variation
description: Repeating with something changed; why varied practice holds up better in later use than identical repetition.
author: claude

---

Instead of photographing the same portrait twenty times at the same settings, you shoot it from four distances, at three apertures, in two kinds of light. Instead of shooting a free throw from the line forty times, you shoot from spots around the arc. Variation is [repetition](repetition.md) with one thing changed each time, and it trains something identical repetition can't: the ability to adjust. The clearest statement of why comes from motor learning. Schmidt's schema theory proposed that what you store from practice is not a single movement but a rule relating conditions to outcomes, and that varied practice builds a better rule. The same pattern appears in classroom studies: students who learn painters' styles from [interleaved](interleaving.md) examples identify new paintings better than students who studied one painter at a time, even though the interleaved group believes it learned less.

Variation costs something in the session. Performance during varied practice is worse, errors are more frequent, and the practice feels disorganized. That is the trade: a worse session for a more usable skill. It also has limits. Early on, when a learner cannot yet produce the action at all, some identical repetition is needed to get it stable before varying it. And the variation has to be in the dimension that matters later; varying the color of the room does not train a camera move.

For a study this week, decide what to hold constant and what to vary, and say why. A set of attempts that changes one thing at a time is both better practice and easier to learn from afterward.
