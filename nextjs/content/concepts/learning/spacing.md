title: Spacing

Four hours of practice on Sunday or one hour on four days: the same time, and the second schedule produces more that lasts. Spacing (distributed practice) is spreading attempts over time with intervals between them, as against massing them in one block. It is among the oldest findings in the psychology of memory, going back to Ebbinghaus's experiments on himself in the 1880s, and among the best supported; Dunlosky and colleagues rate it, with [retrieval practice](retrieval-practice.md), as having the strongest and broadest evidence of any learning technique they reviewed.

The mechanism is partly that the interval forces a reconstruction each time, as retrieval practice does, and partly that what you learn over several occasions is encoded in more varied states and contexts. The cost is the usual one for [desirable difficulties](desirable-difficulty.md): the massed session looks better. After four hours on Sunday the move is sharp; the spaced practicer is rusty at the start of each session and has to find the move again, which is the work that builds it. Spacing does not need a rigid calendar of identical drills. For a maker it can be a two-minute return to last week's decision in this week's notebook, the same target reappearing in a changed project. The design choice is to keep a target constant across time while the surrounding work changes.

The course's weekly rhythm is a spacing schedule if you use it as one. Pick one capacity from this week's study and plan when, in a later week, you will try it again with the support changed.
