The assistant engineer sets up the microphones, watches the session from the back of the room, troubleshoots the patch bay, and keeps the log. Months later they are given a small session of their own. The atelier assistant ground pigment and painted backgrounds before touching a face. The second assistant director learns the set by running its edges. Apprenticeship is learning by working beside someone who can do the thing, inside the activity, with real stakes and growing responsibility. It is the oldest form of skill transmission and the one the disciplines keep returning to even when they have schools, because so much of what a master knows is [tacit](tacit-knowledge.md) and passes only by demonstration and corrected attempts.

Lave and Wenger's account of apprenticeship names the mechanism legitimate peripheral participation: the newcomer does real but peripheral work, watches the whole, and moves inward as they become able. Collins, Brown, and Newman proposed a cognitive apprenticeship for skills that are not visible, like reading, writing, and problem solving, by making the expert's thinking visible (modeling), supporting the learner's attempts (coaching and [scaffolding](scaffolding.md)), and fading the support. What apprenticeship trains is the whole practice, including the judgment and the tacit parts, and what it costs is time, access, and dependence on the quality of the master. It fails when the apprentice is kept peripheral, painting backgrounds forever, or when the master cannot or will not show the thinking.

An agent inverts the arrangement: you are the one who can do the thing, and the skill file is your attempt to make your thinking visible to it. Writing one is a cognitive-apprenticeship exercise run backwards, and it shows you which parts of your practice you can model and which you only have.
