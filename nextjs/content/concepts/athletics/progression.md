title: Progression
description: Ordering practice from simple to complex so each step is reachable from the one before it.

Youth baseball shows a progression in its purest form. Children hit off a tee, then from a coach's soft toss, then from a machine, then from a live pitcher. Each stage removes one simplification: first the ball is still, then it moves predictably, then it moves fast, then someone is trying to get you out. Nobody expects a seven-year-old to face live pitching, and nobody keeps a fourteen-year-old on the tee. The sequence is a ladder, and the coach's job is to know which rung the athlete is on.

A progression works by holding the target constant while adding difficulty around it. The swing stays the swing; what changes is speed, uncertainty, fatigue, and the presence of a defender. This is a form of [scaffolding](../learning/scaffolding.md): support that is withdrawn as competence grows. Two failures are common. The first is staying too long on an easy rung because performance there looks good, which confuses success in the session with readiness for the next one. The second is skipping rungs because the athlete is impatient or the coach is, so the athlete practices failing at something they can't yet attempt. The constraints-led coaches add a third option: rather than advancing the athlete up a fixed ladder, adjust one condition of the task and see what emerges.

For this week, the progression is something you design for yourself. Start with a version of the study where the hard variable is held still, then let it move. If the target is a camera move, do it on a static subject before a moving one; if the target is a prompt, fix the model and source material and vary one instruction before varying three. Record what the step was, so that when a later version fails you can tell which rung gave way.
