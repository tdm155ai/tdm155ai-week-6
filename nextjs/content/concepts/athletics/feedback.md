title: Feedback
description: Knowledge of results versus knowledge of performance, and the difference between a result, a diagnosis, and an intervention.

Motor-learning research splits feedback into two kinds. Knowledge of results is information about the outcome: the shot missed left, the lap was 58.2, the serve was out. Knowledge of performance is information about the movement itself: your hips opened early, your elbow dropped, your toss drifted behind you. The ball supplies the first kind for free. The second usually needs a coach or a camera, because the athlete, from inside the movement, can't see most of what their body did. Both are feedback. They answer different questions.

For working with AI, this reference adds a second split that cuts across the first. A result is an observation: the ball missed. A diagnosis is a claim about cause: your contact point changed when you rushed. An intervention is an experiment: try the same pace from a different setup. The three come in that order and carry different weights. A result is just true. A diagnosis needs evidence, and a fluent diagnosis is not a correct one, which matters now that a model can produce one in seconds. An intervention is how you find out whether the diagnosis was right. A good practice log records the observation before the explanation, so that a wrong diagnosis can be caught later without losing the evidence.

There is also a question of how much feedback helps. Some motor-learning studies find that feedback on every attempt improves performance in the session but produces dependence, with worse retention than feedback given less often or on request; researchers call this the guidance hypothesis. The conditions under which it holds are not fully settled, so treat it as a caution rather than a rule: if a tool tells you after every attempt what to fix, you may be learning to wait for the tool.
