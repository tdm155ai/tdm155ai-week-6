title: Chunking

A quarterback looks at the defense before the snap and sees a coverage: two deep safeties, a linebacker shaded toward the slot, which together mean one thing about where the ball should go. A first-year player looks at the same eleven people and sees eleven people. The difference is chunking. The experienced player has grouped the pieces into a pattern that carries a meaning, so the whole formation is one thing to perceive rather than eleven things to track. This is the same ability that lets a chess master reproduce a board position after a few seconds' look, which studies found works only for positions from real games; scramble the pieces randomly and the master's advantage mostly disappears.

Chunking is what lets the expert "read the game," and it is the part of sport that drills reach least. A cone drill never presents a coverage. The patterns are built from exposure to real or realistic situations, in the [scrimmage](scrimmage.md) and on [film](film-study.md), with someone pointing at the recurring shape until the learner sees it unprompted. It can go wrong in the predictable way: a player who has chunked one opponent's tendencies reads them into the next opponent.

A filmmaker reads a scene the way a quarterback reads a defense: a glance at the room tells them where the light is, where the camera can go, which wall the sound will bounce off. For this week, notice which situations you can already read at a glance and which you still take in piece by piece. A model that summarizes ten games of film can hand you the pattern as a sentence, and it is worth asking whether having the sentence is the same as being able to see the shape.
