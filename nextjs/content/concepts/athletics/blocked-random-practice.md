---
title: Blocked and random practice
description: Practicing one skill at a time versus mixing them, and why the order that feels worse often tests better.
author: claude
order: 6
---

Imagine two tennis players at a lesson. The first hits thirty forehands, then thirty backhands, then thirty volleys. The second hits ninety balls in a shuffled order, never knowing which stroke is coming next. By the end of the hour the first player looks better; their strokes have settled into a groove. Bring both back a week later and test them on mixed balls, and the second player usually wins. This is the contextual interference effect, one of the more replicated findings in motor learning, and it runs against intuition: the arrangement that looks like progress in the session is the one that produces less of it.

The explanation most researchers accept is that random practice forces the learner to reconstruct the movement plan on every attempt, because the previous attempt was a different stroke. Blocked practice lets them run the same plan again without thinking, which feels fluent and leaves less behind. The effect is a good example of the gap between learning and performance: what you can do at the end of the session is not a reliable guide to what you will be able to do later. It is also conditional. The advantage of random practice is clearer for learners who already have the basic movement and for skills that differ from one another; a complete beginner on a complex skill may need some blocked work first just to get a plan worth reconstructing.

For a media study, blocked practice is regenerating the same shot ten times; random practice is alternating between three different problems and having to decide, each time, which technique the problem calls for. The second is harder and feels less productive. That feeling is not evidence.
