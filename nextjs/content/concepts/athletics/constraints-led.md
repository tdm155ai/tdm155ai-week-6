title: Constraints-led approach
description: Changing the task so the skill emerges from solving it, with the coach guiding the search rather than prescribing the movement.

A soccer coach who wants players to look up while dribbling could tell them to look up, again and again. Or the coach could run a three-on-three game in which a goal only counts if the scorer called a teammate's name first. Now looking up is the only way to win, and the players discover it without being told. This is the constraints-led approach: instead of prescribing the correct movement and drilling it, the coach adjusts the conditions of the task so that the wanted behavior becomes the best solution to the problem, and the athlete finds it. The constraints come in three kinds: the individual's own body and abilities, the task and its rules, and the environment, and the movement that emerges is the one that fits all three.

The approach is often called guided discovery, and the word guided matters. The coaching literature on it is explicit that discovery does not mean the instructor stands back; the instructor designs the constraint, watches what emerges, and adjusts it. A badly chosen constraint removes the feature that matters in the real game. A well-chosen one isolates something meaningful while leaving the decision inside the task, which is the thing the plain [drill](drill.md) can't do. Much of the published work concerns children and team games, so claims about how it works for university students learning media tools are proposals, not findings.

The Lab's translation is a list of constraints for studies: to study coverage, allow exactly three shots; to study continuity, hold the action fixed and vary only the camera position; to study prompting, keep the model and the source material fixed and change one instruction. The rule to keep is the coach's: after the constrained study, return to the larger task and see whether the skill came with you.
