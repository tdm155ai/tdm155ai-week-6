title: Form

Every sport has a picture of the right way to do it. The textbook jump shot has the elbow under the ball, the guide hand off the release, the follow-through held. The textbook freestyle has a high elbow catch and a bilateral breath. Form is this codified picture: the technique a sport has agreed is teachable and worth drilling toward, the thing a coach compares the athlete against on film. For a beginner it is indispensable: without a target shape there is nothing to correct toward.

Form is also overrated with some regularity, and sport keeps producing the counterexamples. Dick Fosbury won the 1968 Olympic high jump going over the bar backward, in a style every coach would have corrected, and within a decade everyone jumped that way. Rick Barry shot free throws underhand and made about ninety percent of them, better than nearly anyone shooting the textbook way. In each case the form had been mistaken for the goal, and the goal was the effect: the bar cleared, the ball in. The constraints-led coaches draw the general lesson that the best movement for a given body in a given task is sometimes not the standard one, and that a coach who prescribes form too tightly can drill out the solution the athlete was finding. The external-focus research adds that a learner attending to the shape of their own limbs often performs worse than one attending to the effect.

The media version is the rule of thirds, the 180-degree line, the J-cut. Learn them, because they are the accumulated form of the craft and the fastest way to stop making beginner errors. Then judge every one by its effect in the shot, and keep a list of the times breaking it worked.
