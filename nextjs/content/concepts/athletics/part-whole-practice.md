---
title: Part-whole practice
description: When to practice a piece of a skill on its own and when the whole movement has to be practiced together.
author: claude
order: 4
---

A coach deciding how to run practice faces the same choice every day: drill the parts or run the whole. A gymnast can work the round-off, the back handspring, and the layout separately or run the tumbling pass end to end. A rowing crew can do pause drills at each point of the stroke or row full pressure. Both approaches have a history of producing champions, which is a sign that the right answer depends on the skill.

The research on part-task training, summarized in motor-learning textbooks such as Schmidt and Lee's, points to one main distinction. Skills whose parts happen one after another, like a gymnastics pass or a dance routine, decompose well, and practicing a weak segment on its own transfers back to the whole. Skills whose parts happen at the same time and depend on each other, like a golf swing or a freestyle stroke, decompose badly, because the thing that makes them hard is the coordination between parts, and that coordination is exactly what part practice removes. For those, slowing the whole movement down or simplifying it keeps the coordination intact in a way that splitting it does not. Even for serial skills, the joints between parts need their own practice, which is why coaches drill transitions as well as segments.

For a media exercise, the analogue is deciding whether to practice the shot, the edit, and the sound as separate studies or to make one short, complete piece. The sport evidence suggests both, in order: parts to fix a specific weakness, then a small whole task to find out whether the parts coordinate. A twenty-second scene with three shots is the scrimmage of this week; it tests coordination that no single study can.
