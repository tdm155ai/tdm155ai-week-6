title: Coach
description: The person who sees what the athlete can't see from inside the movement and chooses what to say about it.

A pitcher knows the ball sailed high. The pitching coach knows why: the front foot landed open, and the arm came through late to compensate. The pitcher could not have seen this, because the foot landed while they were watching the target. That is the coach's first job, to be a pair of eyes outside the body. The second job is harder, and it is selection. A coach sees a dozen things wrong and says one, the one the athlete can fix now and that will fix the most else.

The coach's loop is the model for feedback everywhere in this reference: observe, diagnose, intervene, and then test again to see whether the intervention worked. The last step is the one that separates coaching from commentary. A coach who says "your foot is open" and walks away has delivered a diagnosis; a coach who then watches ten more pitches has run an experiment on it. Coaches are also wrong, often, and a good one holds the diagnosis loosely until the next pitches confirm it. The thing a coach supplies that film can't is the education of attention: not the instruction, but the pointing, the "watch your foot here," that teaches the athlete where to look so they can eventually coach themselves.

A model can play parts of this role, and the Lab calls that a practice partner. It can watch, in the sense of analyzing a recording, and it can produce a diagnosis with great fluency. What it is worse at is selection and the test afterward, and what it can't do is want you to improve. If you ask a model for a critique, ask for one thing to change, not twelve, and then check it against the next attempt.
