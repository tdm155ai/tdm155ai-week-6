title: Decomposition
description: Breaking a sport into movements small enough to practice, count, and correct on their own.

Every coached sport has been taken apart into pieces. Freestyle swimming becomes the kick, the catch, the pull, the recovery, and the breath, each with its own drill. A tennis serve becomes the toss, the load, the contact, and the follow-through. A golf swing gets decomposed so finely that there are teaching aids for the grip alone. The decomposition is what makes a sport teachable: you can't tell a beginner to swim better, but you can tell them their elbow drops during the pull, and give them a drill for that.

The cost of taking a thing apart is that it has to go back together, and the joints between the parts are themselves a skill. Timing is the usual casualty. A swimmer who has perfected kick and pull separately may have built two rhythms that don't share a body, and a server whose toss is textbook in isolation may find it drifts once the load is added. The entry on [part and whole practice](part-whole-practice.md) covers what research says about when the parts help and when they don't. The short version is that decomposition pays off most when the parts happen one after another and least when they happen at once.

The same move shows up in a media project. "Make the interview intelligible" is too big to practice; "find out whether the microphone or the room is the problem in these clips" is a piece you can work on. The decomposition is only useful if the piece stays connected to the larger purpose. A student who can execute a clean pan but can't say what scene it belongs in has practiced a part and lost the whole.
