---
title: Drill
description: An isolated movement, pulled out of the sport and repeated until it is reliable.
author: claude
order: 1
---

A drill takes one movement out of the game and repeats it on its own. A basketball player shoots a hundred free throws from the same spot. A soccer player dribbles through a line of cones. A swimmer kicks a length holding a board so the arms can't help. Nothing in a drill is the sport itself; the opponent is gone, the clock is gone, the decision about what to do has been made in advance. That is the point. With everything else stripped away, the movement becomes something you can count, time, and correct.

What a drill trains is consistency and timing in a known situation. What it can't train is the thing it removed: knowing when the movement is called for. A player who has grooved a perfect crossover in the cone line still has to recognize, in a game, the half-second when the defender's weight shifts and the crossover will work. That recognition is built elsewhere, in the [scrimmage](scrimmage.md), in [film study](film-study.md), or in a drill redesigned so the decision is part of it, which is what the [constraints-led approach](constraints-led.md) does. A coach who only runs drills produces players who look good in warm-ups. A drill also repeats errors as faithfully as it repeats successes, so a drill without [feedback](feedback.md) can make a bad habit permanent.

The media version is the same shape: record the same camera move three times, or regenerate the same image with one prompt changed, and compare. That is a drill, and it is worth doing. The thing to notice is that it trains execution and not selection. Knowing when a slow pan serves a scene is a different skill from being able to execute one, and no number of pans teaches it.
