title: Deliberate practice
description: Ericsson's claim about how experts are made, its popular form as a number of hours, and the dispute over it in sport.
author: claude

---

In 1993 Anders Ericsson and his colleagues studied violinists at a Berlin academy and reported that the best ones had accumulated far more hours of a specific kind of practice: solitary, effortful, aimed at a weakness, designed by a teacher, with feedback. They called it deliberate practice and argued it was the main thing separating experts from everyone else. Sport took the idea up with enthusiasm, and the popular version, that ten thousand hours of practice makes an expert, became a slogan on locker-room walls. The paper describes a kind of practice, not a quantity.

The dispute matters, and any entry here that leans on this literature should say so. Meta-analyses by Macnamara and colleagues, one across fields in 2014 and one on sport in 2016, found that measured deliberate practice explained a modest share of the differences in performance between athletes, around eighteen percent in sport, and almost none among elite performers. Ericsson and Harwell replied in 2019 that many of the studies counted activities that did not meet the original definition, so the meta-analyses were measuring something looser than deliberate practice. The percentages are associations across people in those studies, not the share of any one athlete's skill caused by practice, and they are sensitive to how practice was measured and who was included. What the argument supports is rejecting a universal hours-to-mastery recipe. What it does not support is the idea that practice is dispensable.

For a short course week the practical move is modest. Call an activity purposeful practice when you pick a target, attempt it, get usable evidence, and try again, and leave claims about deliberate practice in the strict sense to the people still arguing about the definition. You don't need to settle the dispute to tell focused improvement from time spent.
