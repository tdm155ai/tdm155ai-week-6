---
title: Automaticity
description: Fitts and Posner's three stages of learning a movement, from thinking through every step to not thinking at all.
author: claude
order: 11
---

A beginner dribbling a basketball watches the ball. Every bounce is a decision. A few months later they dribble while scanning the court, calling a play, and reading the defender's hips, and they could not tell you what their hand is doing. The movement has gone underground. Fitts and Posner described this in 1967 as three stages: a cognitive stage, where the learner works out what to do, slowly and with errors; an associative stage, where the pattern is in place and the learner refines it; and an autonomous stage, where the movement runs with little attention and the attention is free for something else.

What automaticity buys is capacity. The point guard can read the defense because dribbling no longer costs anything to think about. What it costs is access. Once a movement is autonomous it is hard to change, because the learner no longer knows how they do it, and it is hard to inspect, which is why experts often explain their own technique poorly. One account of choking under pressure holds that the athlete, anxious, starts attending to a movement that had been running on its own, and the attention breaks it. The research on external focus points in the same direction: instructions that pull attention back onto the limb tend to hurt skilled performers.

This is the sense in which "muscle memory" is real, though the memory is in the nervous system, not the muscle. For this week, the question is which of your own movements have gone autonomous, including movements at a keyboard, and whether you could still open one up to change it. Ericsson argued that automaticity is where improvement stops unless the learner deliberately pulls the skill back into attention, a claim from the disputed deliberate-practice literature that every coach will nonetheless recognize.
