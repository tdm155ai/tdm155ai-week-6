---
title: Overload and recovery
description: Training the body is different from training the skill; the body adapts to load during rest, and reps done tired teach the wrong thing.
author: claude
order: 18
---

A lifter adds five pounds to the bar every week. A distance runner adds a mile to the long run. The principle is progressive overload: the body adapts to a demand slightly larger than what it is used to, and the adaptation happens afterward, during recovery, not during the session. Lift the same weight forever and the body stops changing; add too much too fast and it breaks. Every strength and conditioning program is a schedule for walking that line, and rest days are part of the program, not time off from it.

This is a different kind of training from the rest of this category, and the difference matters. Overload trains tissue: muscle, tendon, heart, lung. Skill practice trains coordination and perception. They overlap in the body but not in the rules. A skill does not improve because you did more of it when you were exhausted; movement practiced under fatigue is often different movement, and the body learns that version too. Coaches keep the two apart, putting the technical work early in a session when the athlete is fresh and the conditioning after it. A hundred repetitions can be conditioning, skill practice, or neither, depending on what the athlete was doing with their attention, which is why a count of reps is not a measure of either.

There is no bodily overload in editing a video, but the second half of the principle has a lesson. Fatigue degrades judgment the way it degrades a swing, and twenty hours in a row on a project includes a long stretch where the work got worse and you couldn't see it. The spacing research in the Learning category says the same thing from the other side: returning to a problem after an interval shows you what stayed and what didn't.
