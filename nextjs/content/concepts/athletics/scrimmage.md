At the end of a basketball practice the coach splits the team in two and lets them play. There is a clock, a score, and a defense that is trying, and everything the drills took out comes back in at once: the decision about when to use the crossover, the fatigue in the fourth quarter, the teammate who isn't where the play said they would be. A scrimmage is the sport itself with the stakes removed. It is the whole practice to the drill's part practice, and no team that only drills is ready to play.

What a scrimmage trains is the things that only exist in the whole: timing between people, reading a live opponent, choosing which movement to use and when. What it hides is also worth knowing. In a thirty-minute scrimmage a given player may shoot four free throws; in a drill they would shoot a hundred. A scrimmage gives few reps of any one skill, and it gives them at full speed, where an error is hard to see and harder to correct. That is why coaches stop scrimmages, run a situation again, and send the team back to drills on Monday. The constraints-led coaches work in the space between the two: a small-sided game with one rule changed keeps the decisions in and raises the rep count on the thing being studied.

The planning table for this week names the media version: make a twenty-second scene with three shots. It is small enough to finish and whole enough that the shot, the cut, and the sound have to coordinate. A student who has practiced each part cleanly finds out in the scrimmage whether the parts know about each other. That finding is the point, and it can't be had any other way.
