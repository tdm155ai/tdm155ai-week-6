Sport is the field where practice has been studied the hardest and argued about the most. Coaches have been decomposing movements, counting reps, and filming athletes for a century, and motor-learning researchers have been running controlled experiments on them for nearly as long. That makes athletics the best place in this reference to see what the practice vocabulary looks like when it is taken seriously, and also the place to see how much of it is still disputed. Borrow the words freely. Borrow the certainty carefully.

The unit of practice in sport is the rep: one free throw, one serve, one start out of the blocks. Reps gather into sets, sets into a [drill](drill.md), drills into a practice session, and at the far end of the session sits the [scrimmage](scrimmage.md), where the parts are put back together under something like game conditions. The whole ladder is built on a [decomposition](decomposition.md) of the sport into movements small enough to count and correct. A swim stroke becomes kick, pull, and breath; a jump shot becomes footwork, lift, release. The drill isolates one of these, repeats it until it is reliable, and then hands it back to the game. Whether the parts go back together cleanly is the oldest open question in coaching, and the entry on [part and whole practice](part-whole-practice.md) says what the research can and can't settle about it.

What gets repeated is the target. What varies is nearly everything else, and the variation is where the science gets interesting. A player who hits thirty forehands in a row and then thirty backhands looks sharper during practice than one who hits them in a random order, and tests worse a week later. The same pattern shows up for throwing to one distance versus many, for one defender versus a changing one, for a stable drill versus a small-sided game with the rules altered. The entries on [blocked and random practice](blocked-random-practice.md) and [variability](variability.md) lay out that evidence. Its lesson for this week is uncomfortable: the arrangement that feels like progress in the session is often not the one that produces learning, and you can't tell the difference by how the session felt.

Feedback in sport comes from three places. The first is the result: the ball went in or it didn't, the clock says what it says. The second is the coach, who sees what the athlete can't see from inside the movement and chooses what to say about it. The third is film, which since the camcorder and now the phone has made every athlete their own second coach. The [feedback](feedback.md) entry separates knowledge of results from knowledge of performance, and this reference adds a further split that matters for working with AI: a result is an observation, a diagnosis is a claim about cause that needs evidence, and an intervention is an experiment. The ball missed; your contact point drifted when you rushed; try the same pace from a different setup. Keeping those three apart is the single most transferable habit in this category.

Some things resist drilling. Reading the game, the ability to look at a defense or a pitch or a break in the pack and know what is about to happen, is built partly by exposure under real conditions and partly by [film study](film-study.md), and no isolated drill produces it. The decision about which movement to use is not trained by repeating the movement. Composure under pressure can be prepared for but not rehearsed in the absence of pressure. The constraints-led coaches have an answer to part of this: change the task so the decision becomes part of the drill. It is a good answer, and still only partial.

Then there is the dispute at the center of the field. Ericsson's deliberate-practice account, which came from violinists, was taken up by sport and popularized as a number of hours. Meta-analyses by Macnamara and colleagues found that measured deliberate practice explained a modest share of the differences between athletes and very little among elite ones; Ericsson and Harwell replied that the meta-analyses had counted the wrong activities as practice. The [deliberate practice](deliberate-practice.md) entry carries that argument. The position this reference takes is the one the Lab's planning takes: practice is not dispensable, there is no recipe, and the working term for the week is purposeful practice, which asks only that you choose a target, attempt it, get usable evidence, and try again.

What does AI change here? The tools of feedback, mostly. A phone and a pose-estimation model can measure a joint angle that a coach could only estimate; a language model can summarize ten games of film or draft a diagnosis in seconds. That last ability is the one to watch. A fluent diagnosis is not a correct one, and the athlete who accepts the model's explanation of a miss has skipped the part of the loop that was teaching them to see. What AI leaves is the rep. Nobody else's body can do your free throws, and nobody else's attention can notice what your contact point did. The week's question applies without translation: when the model reviews the film for you, what are you learning, and what are you no longer learning to notice?

One caution about the metaphor. A Markdown file that tells an agent how to do a job is not a muscle, and editing it is not practice. The sport analogy is strongest at the level of designing practice, examining evidence, and revising a routine, and it becomes misleading the moment a skill file is treated as a learner. Use the vocabulary here to plan your own studies. Use it on the agent only where it fits.
