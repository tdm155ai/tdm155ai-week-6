Sport counts repetition in reps and sets. Ten push-ups is a set of ten reps. A pitcher throws a bullpen of forty pitches. A sprinter runs six starts with full recovery between them. The rep is the unit everything else is measured in, and the count is the first thing a coach writes down. It is also the first thing a beginner gets wrong about practice, because the count looks like the work when it is only the container for it.

Repetition does two different things that are easy to confuse. It conditions the body: muscle, tendon, and lung adapt to repeated load, which is the subject of the [overload](overload.md) entry. And it stabilizes a movement, so the hundredth free throw looks like the ninety-ninth, which is the road to [automaticity](automaticity.md). The second kind only works when the movement being repeated is the one you want. Reps done tired, reps done wrong, and reps done without attention all count toward the total and all teach the body something, not necessarily the thing you intended. A hundred repetitions can include fatigue, repeated error, and movement that has nothing to do with the target. That is why this reference does not treat rep volume as evidence of learning and why no exercise this week is graded on how many times you did it.

The useful question is what changed between rep one and rep forty, and whether you could say what you were adjusting. Ten attempts with a target, a look at the result, and one change each time are a different activity from forty identical swings, even though the second produces a larger number. The music entry on repetition makes the same point about running a passage; the dance entry makes it about the combination done again on the other side.
