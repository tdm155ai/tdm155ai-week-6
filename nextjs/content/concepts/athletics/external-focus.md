Tell a golfer to focus on the swing of the club head, and they tend to hit better than if you tell them to focus on the swing of their arms. Tell a jumper to push the ground away rather than extend their knees, and they jump higher. Gabriele Wulf and her colleagues ran versions of this experiment across many sports and tasks, and the pattern held often enough to become a well-known result in motor learning: an external focus, on the effect of the movement in the world, usually produces better performance and better learning than an internal focus on the body part doing it.

In 2016 Wulf and Lewthwaite folded this into a larger account, the OPTIMAL theory of motor learning, which names three factors that help: enhanced expectations, meaning the learner believes they can succeed; autonomy support, meaning the learner has some say in the practice; and external focus. The theory is a synthesis, and it does not license the claim that every external cue works for every learner on every task; there are exceptions, and the effect sizes vary. What it does support is a habit for coaches and for anyone writing instructions: say what the movement should do before saying what the body should do, and give the learner a choice where one is possible.

The Lab has a study in mind that tests this on a camera move. One cue is internal: hold your elbow at this angle. The other is external: keep the foreground object steady while the background reveals the doorway. Nobody should assume the second wins; the point is to compare what the learner notices and whether it holds in another setup. The same split applies to prompts for a model: describing the effect wanted versus prescribing the steps.
