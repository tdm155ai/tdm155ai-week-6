Football teams spend more hours in the film room than on the field. A player watches their own game: the step they took too early. Then they watch the next opponent: which way the receiver likes to break, what the offensive line does before a run. A swimmer watches an underwater camera of their own stroke and sees for the first time that their hand enters crossed over the midline, which they would have sworn it didn't. Film is the second coach, the one that shows what the body did rather than what it felt like it did.

Film study does two separate things. Watching yourself is feedback, specifically knowledge of performance, and its great virtue is that it doesn't flatter; the camera shows the drop in the elbow whether or not you want to see it. Watching others is how the pattern library gets built, the [chunking](chunking.md) that lets a player read a situation. Both have a limit that matters this week. Film shows position and timing, and it does not show what the athlete saw or felt or decided, so a diagnosis drawn from film alone is still a guess about cause. And watching can produce the illusion of fluency: a player who has seen the right move on screen many times may feel they know it without being able to make it.

The media equivalent is dailies and the playback of your own takes, and this course's version is the practice log: recording attempts so they can be played back and inspected. A model can now watch the film for you and summarize what it saw. That is useful and it is also a place to be careful, since the point of film study was to teach you to see, and a summary skips the seeing.
