---
title: Variability of practice
description: Practicing a movement across a range of conditions rather than one fixed version of it.
author: claude
order: 7
---

A child learning to throw a beanbag into a bucket can practice from one spot or from many. The intuitive plan is to stand at the distance the test will use and throw from there. Experiments going back to the 1970s have repeatedly found the opposite: children who practice from several distances, none of them the test distance, often do as well or better on the test than children who practiced only from the test distance. Practicing the variations builds something more general than practicing the one case does.

Schema theory, the account associated with Richard Schmidt, explains this by proposing that what the learner stores is not a single movement but a rule relating conditions to outcomes, a kind of function for how much force a given distance needs. Varied practice gives the rule more data points. The claim is not that variability always wins. Skills that will only ever be performed under one condition, like a free throw from a fixed line, may benefit from specificity, and the research on when each applies is still argued over in the textbooks. The dependable part is narrower: if the skill will have to adapt, practice should include adaptation.

This is why the drills table in the Lab's planning distinguishes "repeat an isolated movement" from "change one constraint." Recording three versions of the same camera move trains consistency; recutting the same footage for two different audiences trains adaptation to a known variable. The two train different things, and a study should say which one it is after. Varying a prompt across several source images, rather than perfecting it on one, is the same move in a different medium.
