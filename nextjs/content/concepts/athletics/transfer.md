A player who makes ninety percent of their free throws in an empty gym makes seventy in the fourth quarter of a close game. The skill was there in practice and only partly there in the game, and the gap is the problem of transfer: whether practicing something under one set of conditions produces the ability to do it under another. In sport the usual answer is that transfer is narrower than people hope. Skills carry best to situations that resemble the practice, and the resemblance that matters includes the fatigue, the crowd, the opponent, and the decision, not just the movement.

This is why coaches talk about specificity, and why the categories in this reference keep returning to the scrimmage. A drill transfers to the game to the degree that it kept the features of the game that matter, and the trouble is that nobody knows in advance which features those are. The [variability](variability.md) research suggests that practicing across conditions produces something more portable than practicing one condition, and the [constraints-led](constraints-led.md) coaches try to keep the decision inside the drill for the same reason. Neither guarantees transfer. The honest claim after a study is narrow: "I can do this in that situation," with the situation named.

For this week, name transfer carefully. A student who succeeds on a new example in the same software has shown a narrower transfer than one who recognizes the same problem in a different medium, and neither is general mastery. The same applies to an agent skill: it has been shown to work on the case it was written from, and testing it on a case it was not written from is the only way to find out whether it transfers, which is what the evaluation entry in Learning with AI is about.
