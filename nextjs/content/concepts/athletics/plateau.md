---
title: Plateau
description: The flat stretch where practice continues and improvement stops, and the things a coach changes to get through it.
author: claude

---

A runner drops their mile time every month for a year and then it stops. A tennis player's second serve, which improved every week for a season, sits at the same speed and the same error rate. This is the plateau, and every athlete who stays in a sport long enough meets one. It is not a sign that the ceiling has been reached. It is usually a sign that the current practice has given what it can.

Coaches have a repertoire for it, and the repertoire is instructive because every item is a change in the practice rather than more of it. They change the constraint, so the task asks for something the old routine didn't. They change the cue, often from a body-focused instruction to an external one. They go to film and look for the thing the athlete stopped noticing. They reduce the load for a week, since a plateau is sometimes accumulated fatigue wearing the costume of a limit. Ericsson's account of expertise has a specific explanation: once a movement is automatic, continuing to perform it no longer improves it, and the learner has to pull the skill back into attention and work on a piece of it deliberately. That explanation belongs to the disputed deliberate-practice literature and should be read with the dispute in mind, but the coaching practice it describes, breaking the routine on purpose, predates the theory.

The plateau that matters this week is the one at the keyboard. A workflow that produced improvement for a month and now produces the same result at the same quality has stopped teaching, and the coach's list applies: change the constraint, change the cue, look at the record, rest. More of the same is the one option that reliably does nothing.
