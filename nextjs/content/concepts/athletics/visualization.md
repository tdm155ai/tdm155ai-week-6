Before a downhill run, a skier stands at the top with eyes closed, hands moving, running the whole course gate by gate in their head. A diver on the platform runs the dive once in imagination before running it in the air. A free-throw shooter sees the ball go through the net before the ball leaves their hand. This is visualization, or mental practice: rehearsing a movement in imagination, with as much of the feel of it as the athlete can summon and not just the picture.

Reviews of mental practice over several decades tend to find that it helps, that it helps less than physical practice, and that it helps most for skills with a large cognitive component, like a sequence of gates or a play to remember, and least for skills that are mostly strength or raw speed. The usual recommendation is to combine it with physical practice rather than substitute it; the mechanisms are still argued over. What visualization clearly does is rehearse the plan: the order of things, the decision points, what to do at the third gate. It also seems to do something for composure, letting the athlete meet a pressured moment they have already met in imagination. What it can't do is give the body the feedback a real attempt provides, since the imagined ball always goes in.

For this week, visualization is the practice of running a shot or an edit in your head before making it, and then comparing. The Lab's feedback routine builds this in as the first step: predict what will happen and name the criterion before you make the version. A prediction that turns out wrong is one of the most useful things a study can produce, and you can only have one if you visualized first.
