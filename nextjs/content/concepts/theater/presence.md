---
title: Presence
description: The quality of being there, available to the partner and the room, that audiences can feel and nobody can drill; what can be prepared instead.
author: claude

---

Two actors say the same line equally well, and one of them you cannot stop watching. Theater people call the difference presence, and they have been arguing about what it is for a long time. The actor and director Joseph Chaikin wrote a book with the word in its title; Peter Brook built a theory of theater on the bare fact of one person crossing an empty space while another watches. The working definition most teachers use is something like attention: the actor is there, in the room, with the partner, and not somewhere else (in the next line, in her own performance, in the review). Audiences feel it without being able to say what they felt.

Presence is the thing in theater that cannot be drilled, and trying to drill it tends to produce its opposite. An actor told to be more present starts performing presence, a visible effort of attentiveness that is the least present thing on stage. What can be done is to prepare the conditions. Lines so well known they need no thought; blocking that has gone automatic; a body warmed up and breathing; a character built from enough study that the choices are already made; and exercises like Meisner's repetition that put attention on the partner until that is the habit. Technique clears the ground. Presence is what happens, or does not, on the cleared ground.

This is the discipline's version of tacit knowledge, the part of a skill that cannot be written down, and it is why no acting book claims to teach acting by itself. For the week's question about skill files and instructions, presence is the limiting case: the thing no procedure produces and every procedure in the field exists to make room for.
