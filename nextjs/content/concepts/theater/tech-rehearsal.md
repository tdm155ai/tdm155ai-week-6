description: "Rehearsing the machine: the days when lights, sound, set, and actors are put together one cue at a time."

Tech is the week the actors stop being the point. The set is up, the lights are hung, and the company goes through the play cue to cue: skip to the line before a light change, the stage manager says "standby lights 14," the actor says the line, "lights 14, go," and everyone looks to see whether the state is right. If not, the designer fixes it and they do it again. A play with two hundred cues can take days of this, in the long sessions American professional theater calls ten-out-of-twelves. Actors stand for an hour while a scene change is timed. It is tedious, and it is practice.

What is being rehearsed is coordination, a skill separate from anyone's individual skill. A line can be perfectly delivered and arrive at the wrong moment in the cue sequence; a lighting state can be beautiful and its transition can hide the one gesture that matters. Tech decomposes the production into a sequence of handoffs (actor to stage manager to board operator to the lights), drills each until it is reliable, then runs them at speed. The record is the cue sheet and the prompt book, rewritten all week. What goes wrong is an actor who treats tech as a break and loses the show by the time the cues are set, and a cue sequence that works slowly and falls apart at tempo because the handoffs were never practiced at speed.

A small version makes a good study: four cues, run slowly, one breakdown logged, the cue document revised, run at speed, then a role swapped to see whether the document is enough for someone who was not there. A student could write a checker that finds missing triggers in a cue sheet, which requires knowing the event well enough to say what a missing trigger is. The checker cannot certify the performance; only running it can.
