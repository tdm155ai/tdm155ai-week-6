A play rehearses for weeks so that it can happen once a night as if it had never happened before. That is the odd thing theater asks of practice. A pianist practices to make a passage reliable; an actor practices to make a scene reliable and then has to hide the reliability, because the character does not know what comes next. Most of the vocabulary in this category comes out of that problem: how do you repeat something hundreds of times and keep it alive?

**The unit of practice.** Theater does not practice the play. It practices pieces of the play, at several sizes. The smallest is the [beat](beats.md), a stretch of a scene in which a character wants one thing, which ends when the want changes. The working size is the scene, taken apart in [scene work](scene-work.md): a few pages run, stopped, talked over, and run again from the top. The largest is the [run-through](run-through.md), the whole play without stopping, which tests what scene work cannot: transitions, stamina, the arc of the evening. A [rehearsal](rehearsal.md) process moves through these sizes in a rough order, from the first read through table work and [blocking](blocking.md) to runs, [tech](tech-rehearsal.md), and [previews](preview.md). Beside the play sits a second kind of unit that has nothing to do with the script: the [warm-up](warm-up.md) and the exercise, where the voice, the body, and the attention of the group are trained on their own, the way a musician plays scales before the piece.

**What is repeated and what varies.** The text is fixed. The same lines are said every night in the same order, with the same blocking, on the same cues. Everything else is allowed to move, and much of rehearsal is finding out how much can move without breaking the thing. Early rehearsal varies a lot: an actor tries a scene three ways, the director asks for the opposite choice to see what it shows, a beat gets a new intention. As opening approaches, more gets fixed. Blocking is set, cues are set, and the variation narrows to the fine grain of timing and response. The one thing that varies every night by design is the other actor. Meisner's [repetition exercise](repetition.md) trains exactly this: say the same words again, but let them change because of what your partner just did. A performance is repetition in which the only new thing is the listening.

**Where feedback comes from.** Two sources, arriving at different times. In rehearsal it is the director, who sits where the audience will sit and gives [notes](notes.md) after a run: what read, what did not, what to try tomorrow. A note is a diagnosis delivered to someone who could not see the scene from outside, since an actor is inside it. The second source is the audience, which arrives late, at the [preview](preview.md), and gives feedback nobody can argue with: a laugh in the wrong place, silence in the right one, coughing through a dead stretch. Companies restage between previews because of what the house told them. A third, quieter source is the stage manager, who keeps the record of what was decided and notices when tonight's run drifted from it.

**What can't be drilled.** [Presence](presence.md): the quality of being there, available to the partner and the room, that audiences can feel and nobody can define to everyone's satisfaction. You cannot run it fifty times until it is reliable; the attempt usually produces its opposite, an actor performing attentiveness instead of attending. What you can do is prepare the conditions for it. Lines so well known they need no thought ([memorization](memorization.md)), a warmed-up body, blocking that has gone automatic, a [character](character-work.md) built from enough study that the choices are already made. Technique clears the ground so that something unrehearsable can happen on it. Theater's [acting systems](acting-systems.md) are different bets about how to clear that ground, and they disagree about what the actor is learning.

**What AI changes and what it leaves.** A model can run lines with you at two in the morning, draft a cue sheet, summarize a dramaturg's research packet, transcribe a rehearsal and timestamp every place the director stopped. It can produce a plausible reading of a line, and a plausible voice saying it. None of that is the thing being practiced. A generated voice does not show your breath control; a generated reading of the scene does not show that you can respond to a partner who did something unexpected; a clean cue sheet does not prove the cue will land in a room full of people and moving scenery. Theater is a useful discipline for the week's question because its product cannot be delegated at all. It exists only while the people are in the room. What a model can do is sit where the [understudy](understudy.md) sits, watching and recording, so that more of the process is visible afterward and more of it can be inspected. The practice stays in the body and between the bodies, and the test for every tool is whether it gets you into the room better prepared or lets you skip the preparation.
