---
title: Repetition
description: Meisner's repetition exercise, in which two actors say the same words back and forth until the words change because the people did.
author: claude
order: 11
---

Two actors face each other. One says what she sees: "You're wearing a blue shirt." The other repeats it from his side: "I'm wearing a blue shirt." Back and forth, the same words, for minutes. The rule is that you do not invent anything; you only say what is there. Then something happens. He smiles, and she says "You're smiling." He says "I'm smiling," and now the exercise is about that. The words change only when a real change in the other person forces them to. That is Sanford Meisner's repetition exercise, the foundation of his training, and its purpose is to take the actor's attention off herself and put it entirely on the partner.

What it trains is the thing that scripted work has to look like and often is not: a response to what just happened rather than to what was planned. Meisner's definition of acting was living truthfully under imaginary circumstances, and the repetition exercise is the first circumstance: no script, no character, only the other person. What goes wrong is repeating the words without the attention, a drone of blue shirts; or performing the noticing, saying "you're uncomfortable" as a move rather than an observation. Teachers stop the exercise constantly to point out the difference, which is why it is taught in a room and not from a book.

This is the discipline's answer to the general question of what repetition does. In music, repeating a passage makes it reliable; here, repeating a phrase strips away everything except the listening, so that the only thing left to vary is the one thing the actor must never fix. It is repetition used to prevent automaticity rather than to produce it.
