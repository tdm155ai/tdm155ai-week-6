---
title: Beats
description: Units and beats, the Stanislavski-derived habit of breaking a scene into stretches in which a character wants one thing.
author: claude
order: 2
---

Take a breakup scene. For half a page she wants him to stay; then she wants him to admit he lied; then she wants to end it with some dignity. Three beats. A beat is the stretch of a scene during which a character is after one thing, and it ends when the want changes, usually because the other person did something. Actors mark beats in the script with a slash or a pencil line, and under each one write what the character wants (the objective), what they are doing to get it (an action, best as a verb: to plead, to corner, to dismiss), and what is in the way. The vocabulary descends from Stanislavski's "units and objectives." The story that "beat" is how "bit" sounded in the accents of the Russian-born teachers who brought the system to New York is repeated more often than documented.

Scoring a script this way is a decomposition: it turns a scene into a sequence of playable intentions, so that at every moment the actor has something to do rather than a feeling to produce. That is what makes a scene repeatable. You cannot be sad on cue eight times a week, but you can try to make someone stay eight times a week, and the sadness tends to follow. What goes wrong is over-scoring. A scene analyzed to the last line can play mechanically, each beat an item on a list; the beats are the map, not the walk, and good actors revise them from what happens in the room.

A model will produce a beat breakdown of any scene in seconds, and it will be plausible. Doing it yourself is the study. If you do both, the places where the two disagree are the interesting ones: they show where the scene has more than one reading.
