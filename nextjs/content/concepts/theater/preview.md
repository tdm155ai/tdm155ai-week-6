title: Preview
description: Performances before opening night, in front of a paying audience, that are still being changed; practice in front of the people it is for.

A play opens on a Thursday, but it has been playing to audiences since the previous week. Those performances are previews: full shows, tickets sold, usually at a discount, and the production still under revision. The company rehearses in the afternoon and performs at night. Between previews a scene is cut, a cue is moved, a laugh that is not coming gets a new setup. On Broadway, previews can run for weeks and critics attend the last of them. Opening night is less the first performance than the point after which the company stops changing it.

A preview is the first feedback from the one source that cannot be argued with. In rehearsal the director stands in for the audience; in previews the audience stands in for itself, and it tells you things no director can. Where the laugh is. Where a thousand people shift in their seats at the same moment because the scene is a minute too long. Whether the ending lands or whether the applause is polite. Much of what actors mean by timing can only be learned here, because it is timing against an audience's response, and there is no audience in the rehearsal room. What goes wrong is overcorrecting to one house: a Tuesday crowd that did not laugh is not proof that the joke is dead, and companies learn to read several audiences before changing something.

The preview is also the discipline's cleanest illustration of the difference between performing well and having learned. A run that went beautifully in the empty room is not evidence that the play works; the preview is the test under real conditions, and it is scheduled, on purpose, while there is still time to use what it shows.
