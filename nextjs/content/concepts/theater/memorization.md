---
title: Memorization
description: Learning lines and cues well enough that they need no thought, so that attention can go to the partner.
author: claude
order: 10
---

Every production has an off-book date, the rehearsal after which scripts are put down and anyone who dries calls "line" and gets it from the stage manager. Before that date the actor is doing the least glamorous work in theater: pages covered with a hand, lines said into a phone, a roommate reading the other parts at midnight. What is learned is not only your lines but the cues, the ends of the other characters' lines that your lines answer. An actor who knows her lines and not her cues is lost the moment her partner pauses.

Memorizing is repetition, and the learning research applies without much translation: test yourself rather than rereading, spread the sessions out, and learn the lines in chunks that follow the scene's beats rather than page by page. Actors mostly discover this on their own. Psychologists who have studied how actors learn long scripts found that they rarely memorize by rote; they work out why the character says each line, and the words come along with the reasons. That is the practical argument for doing beats before lines. What goes wrong is lines learned with a tune, a fixed melody of delivery that was set alone in a bedroom and now cannot respond to the partner; and lines learned so late that scene work is spent on remembering instead of acting.

A model is a serviceable line-running partner. It will read the other parts at any hour, never get bored, and feed you the cue as often as you want. It is a poor partner for the thing the lines are for, which is responding to a person who might do something different tonight.
