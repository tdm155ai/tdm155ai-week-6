title: Run-through
description: The whole play without stopping, which tests what working one scene at a time cannot.

The first run-through of a play is usually a mess, and companies have names for it: the stumble-through, the stagger-through. Lines go, props are missed, a scene change takes a full minute of silence. Nobody minds much, because the point of the first run is not to be good but to find out what the play is when it is all in one piece. Later runs tighten. By the last week before tech, a company may be running the whole play every day, and the director's notes shift from single moments to the shape of the evening.

A run-through is whole practice, the complement of scene work's part practice. It tests the things that only exist at full scale: transitions between scenes, pacing across an act, stamina (a three-hour play is physically hard), costume changes, and the way an early choice pays off or does not two hours later. It cannot fix anything, because you do not stop; a run shows you where the problems are and scene work the next day addresses them. Some companies also run a speed-through, lines said as fast as possible with no acting, as a test of memory alone; it is sometimes called an Italian run. What goes wrong is running too early, before the parts are reliable, so that the run just rehearses the mistakes; or running so often that the play goes smooth and automatic, every laugh in its place and nothing alive.

The part-and-whole rhythm is the same one the learning research describes: parts for precision, the whole for integration, and a judgment call about when to switch. Rehearsal schedules are that judgment made into a calendar.
