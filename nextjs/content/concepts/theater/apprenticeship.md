In Shakespeare's company the boys who played the women's parts were apprenticed to the sharers, living in their houses and learning by playing beside them. The pattern lasted. For most of the history of the profession, actors learned in repertory companies, playing small parts for years in the same building as the leads, watching from the wings, taking over a role when someone left, and absorbing the craft without anyone naming it. The conservatory is the newer route: three or four years of classes in voice, movement, and text, scene study, and student productions, with the company replaced by a faculty and a cohort. Most working actors today have had some of both, and the summer theaters still run apprentice companies in which young people build sets, run props, and get to act a little.

What apprenticeship trains that a class cannot is the whole job: how a rehearsal room behaves, what a stage manager's call means, how a professional takes a note, what to do when the prop breaks on a Tuesday. It is learning by being around the work, an education of attention, being shown where to look rather than told what to do. What it cannot guarantee is instruction. An apprentice learns from whoever is there, habits and mistakes included, and a busy company has little time to teach. The conservatory's answer is to make the teaching explicit, and its cost is that a classroom is not a company; graduates often describe their first professional job as the place they learned the trade.

Assistant directors, assistant stage managers, and understudies are the apprentice positions inside a modern production, and they share the feature that matters for this week: you learn by watching someone do the thing, with the record in your hands.
