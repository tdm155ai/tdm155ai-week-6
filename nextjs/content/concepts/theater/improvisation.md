---
title: Improvisation
description: Practice without a script, as a training form of its own and as a tool inside the rehearsal of a scripted play.
author: claude
order: 8
---

Two actors are given a place (a laundromat) and a relationship (siblings who have not spoken in a year) and told to begin. There are no lines. The rule most improv training starts from is to accept what your partner offers and add to it, the "yes, and" of the comedy schools; the rule under that is to listen, because you have nothing else to work from. Improvisation as a form has its own institutions, from Viola Spolin's theater games to Keith Johnstone's work and the comedy theaters that grew out of both. Inside the rehearsal of a scripted play it is a tool: actors improvise the scene before the scene (the car ride over, the night they met) to fill in what the text leaves out, or play a written scene in their own words to find its beats before they go back to the lines.

What improvisation trains is responsiveness. With no script to hide in, an actor who is not listening has nothing to say, so the exercise exposes that fast. It also trains the willingness to make a choice without knowing whether it is right, which scripted work can let you avoid. What it cannot train is the text: the specific rhythm of a written line, the discipline of saying the same thing every night and keeping it alive. Improvisers put into scripted plays sometimes bend the lines; actors trained only on scripts sometimes freeze when a prop breaks. What goes wrong in improv training is a different thing, a room full of people being funny at each other instead of building a scene together.

Improvisation is the clearest case in theater of practice whose output is thrown away. The scene in the laundromat will never be performed; what was practiced was the listening.
