title: Performance
description: Each night new; the run of a play as repetition that has to look like the first time.

A Broadway actor does eight shows a week, and a long run can last a year. Same lines, same blocking, same cues, several hundred times. The character, meanwhile, is hearing the news for the first time every night. The American actor William Gillette named the problem over a century ago as the illusion of the first time: the audience must believe the actor does not know what is coming, though she has said the next line four hundred times. Rehearsal makes the play reliable; performance has to make the reliability invisible.

What varies, by design, is the listening. The lines are fixed, but the partner is a person, and tonight he paused a half-second longer, and the audience is different, and the laugh came early. An actor responding to those things is giving a performance that is new in the only way it can be; an actor reproducing last night's performance is giving a recording. Companies know how a show goes stale and have remedies: the stage manager's nightly report notes drift from what was set, a brush-up rehearsal restores it, a new cast member makes everyone listen again. What goes wrong is what the learning research describes from the other side: automaticity, the hands knowing the work so well that attention leaves. In most skills that is the goal. Here it is the danger, and the craft of the long run is keeping a learned thing from becoming automatic.

A performance is the opposite of a take in film: it cannot be kept, compared, or chosen from. It happens once, in front of the people it is for, and the only record is memory and a report. That is the discipline's answer to what a model leaves alone. It can help make the play; it cannot be in the room while the play is happening.
