title: Acting systems
description: Stanislavski, Meisner, and Viewpoints as trainable systems, each a different bet about what an actor can learn by exercise.
author: claude

---

An acting system is a set of exercises and a vocabulary that claims an actor can be trained, not just born. Stanislavski's is the first and the source of most of the others: objectives, given circumstances, the "magic if" (what would I do if this were happening to me), emotion memory, and later a method of physical actions. The American teachers who inherited it split over what mattered. Lee Strasberg's Method built on emotion memory; Stella Adler on imagination and the given circumstances; Sanford Meisner on responding to the partner. Viewpoints, developed by Anne Bogart and Tina Landau from the dancer Mary Overlie's work, trains something else again: a set of named aspects of time and space (tempo, duration, repetition, shape, gesture, spatial relationship, and so on) practiced by a group moving in an open room until they can compose together without a director.

What makes these systems rather than opinions is that each has drills. You can run a repetition exercise, an emotion-memory exercise, an open Viewpoints session, and a teacher can watch and correct. That is the definition of technique in any field: a way of doing the thing codified enough to be taught. What the systems cannot agree on is what the drills are for. One school says the actor is learning to feel; another that she is learning to imagine; another to listen; another to see the room. Do not collapse them into one account of what rehearsal teaches.

For a course that is also building skill files for a model, the acting systems are a useful comparison. Each is an attempt to write down a procedure for something partly tacit, and each is honest that the writing is not the training; the book sends you to the room.
