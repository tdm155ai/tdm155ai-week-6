title: Rehearsal
description: The weeks of preparation that take a play from a first read-through to opening night, in a rough shared order.

A rehearsal process has a shape most companies share, even when the calendar stretches or compresses it. It begins with a first read, the cast around a table reading the whole script aloud. Then table work: days of talking through the scenes, settling what each one is about. Then [blocking](blocking.md), when the actors get up and the director arranges the movement. Then [scene work](scene-work.md), taking the play apart a few pages at a time. Then [run-throughs](run-through.md), putting it back together. Then [tech](tech-rehearsal.md), when lights, sound, and set join the actors, then dress rehearsals, [previews](preview.md) in front of a paying audience, and opening. A professional play might get three to five weeks; a college production spreads the same stages across a semester.

Rehearsal is practice for a group, which makes it different from practice alone. A musician can run a passage until it is right; a scene is only right when two or more people are right at the same time, so every repetition is also a test of coordination. The word is usually traced through Old French to a root meaning to harrow a field again, to go over the ground once more. The order of stages is a decomposition: the play is too big to practice as one thing, so a company practices its parts at increasing scale and the whole only late. What goes wrong is usually a mismatch between stage and task: fine-grained notes before the actors know their lines, or a cast that has run the play so often it has gone smooth and dead.

Rehearsal is the clearest case of practice with a deadline and a product. Every stage exists because something has to be reliable by opening. A model can help with the paper around the process (the schedule, the script breakdown, the cue sheet) but not with the part that is rehearsed, which is people doing a thing together in a room.
