---
title: Blocking
description: Where the actors move and stand, decided in rehearsal and written down, so that the scene is a sequence of movements as well as lines.
author: claude
order: 3
---

"On 'I can't do this anymore' she crosses to the window. He stays at the table. He doesn't get up until she's got the door open." That is blocking: the positions and movements of the actors on stage, worked out in rehearsal and recorded by the stage manager in the prompt book in a shorthand of its own (X DSL for a cross to down-stage left). Blocking decomposes the scene into movement the way beats decompose it into wants. Each cross happens on a line or for a reason, and once set, it is the same every night.

Blocking is one of the first things a production fixes, and it gets drilled until it is automatic, so that the actor can stop thinking about where to go and think about why. That is the practical argument for setting it early: a body that knows its path frees attention for the partner. What goes wrong is blocking that is only traffic, moves made to keep the picture varied with no want behind them, and actors who "walk the blocking," arriving at the window because the prompt book says so rather than because the line pushed them there. Some directors block tightly from the start; others let it emerge from improvisation and spatial work before writing anything down. Either way the record matters, because the understudy, the stage manager, and the actor who has forgotten will all need it.

Film blocks too, but for the camera and for one take at a time; theater blocks for a room full of sightlines and for a run of a hundred nights. The same word covers two different practices with a shared root in organizing action in space.
