"Let's take it from the top of the scene." Scene work is the middle of a rehearsal process and most of its hours: two or three actors and a director on a few pages of script, running them, stopping when something is unclear or dead, trying a different choice, and running them again. A scene might be worked for an afternoon, set aside, and worked again a week later. The stopping is the point. In a run you cannot stop; here you can go back to one line and ask what the character wants on it.

This is part practice. The play is too big to improve all at once, so the company isolates a scene, and inside the scene often a single beat, and repeats it with something changed each time: a new intention, the opposite choice, faster, with the blocking removed, in the actors' own words. The variation is what makes the repetition useful; running the scene the same way five times mostly teaches the actors to run it that way. What scene work trains is the specific: this moment, this choice, this partner. What goes wrong is of two kinds. A scene can get good on its own and stop fitting the play, so that it has to be re-tuned when it goes back into a run. And a director can work a scene past the point of return, noodling on a page for hours while the actors lose the thread of why.

The sketches-and-studies version is the one the research for this week proposes as a classroom study: a short exchange, three passes, a stated intention for each, and an observer writing down what changed. That is scene work reduced to a form you can log.
