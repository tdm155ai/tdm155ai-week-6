title: Character work
description: Building a person through study: what the text says, what the actor researches, and what gets tried in the body.

---

An actor cast as a nineteenth-century doctor starts with the text. What does he say about himself, what do the others say about him, what does he do? Those are the given circumstances, and they are fixed. Then comes what the text leaves out: where he trained, what he is afraid of, how he walks, what his hands do when he lies. Some of this the actor writes (a biography, a day in the character's life); some she researches (what doctors of that decade carried, how they spoke to patients); some she can only find by trying it in the body. Rehearsal shoes, the right coat, a way of sitting. Some teachers use animal exercises, building a physicality from a heron or a bear and then letting it recede. Character work is a study in the painter's sense: work made to work something out, most of which never appears.

What it trains is making choices from evidence, so that on stage the actor does not have to decide what the doctor would do, because she already knows. What goes wrong is decoration: a biography that never reaches the stage, a limp with no reason, a character built as an idea rather than a set of wants. The acting systems disagree about how much of this to do; some teachers want the research, others only the imagination, and Meisner's line of work would rather you found the character in how you respond to your partner.

This course has its own version of the problem: keeping a character the same across generated images and scenes. A model will write a two-thousand-word biography for anyone. The judgment about which details matter, and whether the thing on the page has become a person who could be in a room, stays with you.
