title: Warm-up
description: The voice, body, and ensemble work done before rehearsal that has nothing to do with the script; the exercise and the warm-up as theater's scales.
author: claude

---

Before a rehearsal or a show, the cast stands in a circle. Twenty minutes of stretching, breathing low into the belly, humming up through the resonators, tongue-twisters at increasing speed, then a game: a clap passed around the circle, a word thrown across it, something that has to be caught. None of it is the play. A warm-up prepares the instrument and the group for the work about to happen. An exercise, in the stricter sense, builds a capacity over time: a voice class that adds range across a semester, a movement sequence that makes a body more available. The same activity can be either, and it helps to know which you are doing.

The distinction the research for this week draws is between capacity work and interpretive work. A breathing exercise trains the instrument; a scene trains how the instrument serves an intention. Theater keeps them separate on purpose, so that the actor does not have to discover in the middle of a scene that she has no breath for the long speech. The ensemble games do a third thing: they train the group's attention to itself, the ability to catch what someone across the room just threw, which is the skill every scene then depends on. What a warm-up cannot do is replace the rehearsal. A company that is loose and connected and does not know the play is loose, connected, and in trouble.

This is theater's version of scales. The question to carry into the week is which of your own practices are warm-ups (getting ready for today) and which are exercises (getting better over months), and whether you have confused the two.
